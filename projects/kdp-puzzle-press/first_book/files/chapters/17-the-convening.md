They come after dark, the way these things are done up here — not at a set hour, no one having said one, but in the loose window between supper and bed when a person can leave the house without it meaning anything to the neighbors. The Lehtinens first, Marta's car with Juhani in the passenger seat, the headlights swinging across the kitchen wall as they turn in off the road. Then Erik's truck, ten minutes behind, and behind that, before Aino has the door shut on Erik, a second set of lights that slows at the mailbox and sits there a moment in the dark with the engine running, deciding, and then turns in too.

Reino. Erik glances back at it on the porch and says nothing. Aino does not ask. She holds the door.

It is the first time in her life she has had four people in this house who were not family, and it occurs to her, taking coats, that it may be the first time in the house's life. Eino built it to keep to itself. Her father kept it that way. She hangs Marta's coat on a peg that has held one coat, her father's brown wool, since November, and she thinks: a hundred years of the door shut, and tonight it stands open and the cold comes in on five people who all know why.

She makes coffee because it is what is done. Mikko gets the chairs — the two from the bedrooms, the stool from the workshop — and they crowd them around the kitchen table that was built for two. There are not enough cups that match. Nobody remarks on it. Reino does not sit. He stands by the door with his back to the counter and his arms crossed, the way he stood in Erik's kitchen, and he keeps his coat on.

---

"All right," Juhani says, when the cups are down. "Let's have it. What is it we're agreeing to."

So she lays it out. Plainly, the way Louise laid it out for her, the way she has learned this week is the only way that holds. First light Wednesday, all of them at the shore — her shore, below the sauna, where the footprints come up. They stand. That is the whole of their part. What Louise's people do, Louise's people lead, and the families defer to it; that is theirs, not ours, and no one asks past that line. Aino gives back the records — the ledgers, the notebooks, the things her father kept in the cold — because those are the woman's history and the woman's history belongs with her people. And the sauna comes down after, when Louise tells her it is time, by fire most likely, because it is a sauna and saunas burn.

"Stand where," Marta says.

"On the shore. Wherever there's room. It's not a — there's no arrangement to it." She hears how thin that sounds. "We just don't look away."

Marta nods slowly, both hands around her cup. Juhani turns his a quarter-turn on the table, the old habit, and says, "And that's it. We stand on the ice in the dark in March and we don't look away."

"Not the ice," Erik says. "The shore. You don't want to be on that ice Wednesday." He says it flatly, a man who has been watching his own end of the lake. "It won't hold by then."

"The shore," Aino says.

Reino makes a sound by the counter. Not a word. Just air, pushed out through the nose, the sound of a man who has heard enough to confirm what he came knowing.

---

It is Marta who breaks it open. Aino would not have guessed it would be her — would have put money on Juhani, who had thirty years of it banked. But it is Marta, setting her cup down, who says into the small kitchen, not loud:

"My father told me it was four men."

The room goes still in a way the room has not been still all evening.

"He never named the fourth," Marta says. "Said three and then stopped, and I always thought he didn't know it, and then I got older and understood he knew it and wouldn't say it because the fourth was nobody. The fourth line died out. Risto Mäkelä's boy, no children." She glances at Mikko, who carries that blood from the side that lived. "I'm sorry. I don't mean nobody."

"No," Mikko says. "I know what you mean."

"Four men," Juhani says. He has both spotted hands flat on the oilcloth now. "Eino Hietala. The Lehtinen — that's my grandfather. Saari." He does not look at Erik when he says it. "And the Mäkelä. In the fall. Nineteen and nineteen. They went out to a cabin." He stops. The melt is not ticking tonight; the night has gone cold again and the eaves have stopped running, and the quiet where that sound should be is its own thing in the room. "That's as far as my father ever got. They went out to a cabin and after that there was a watch on the lake and a sauna that wasn't a sauna, and the Hietalas kept it, and the rest of us kept our mouths shut and let them."

"My father said the same," Erik says. Low. "Almost the word for word. They went out to a cabin." He keeps both hands flat on the table and does not touch the cup. "Fifty years I've had that sentence and never the end of it. A man learns not to want the end of a sentence like that."

And there it is — the thing she did not expect, the strange heavy ease of it once the first plain words are out. Not relief, exactly; relief is too clean a word for what moves through the kitchen. It is more like the give in a frozen door when the second person leans on it: a hundred years of weight against the inside of it, four families each with a shoulder to it alone in the dark, and now all the shoulders at once and the thing finally moving. Nobody is comforted. Everybody is, somehow, lighter, and ashamed of the lightness, and saying it anyway because there is no one left to keep it from.

"A woman," Marta says quietly. "It was a woman they — " She does not finish that one either.

"Elderly," Aino says. "She lived alone on the land my great-grandfather claimed. She wouldn't leave. They went out there in the fall, four of them, and she died, and they put her in the lake." She says it the way Louise said it to her, the flat way that is the only honest way, and she watches it go around the table and land on each of them in turn — Juhani's jaw, Marta's hands, Erik's gone-still face. They knew. Every one of them knew the shape of it. But the shape said aloud in one room, by all of them together, is not the shape carried alone, and she sees it reach them as a new thing even though it is the oldest thing any of them owns.

"They never told us her name," Marta says. It is half a question, the closest these people will come to asking.

Aino sets her cup down. She has carried the name since Louise gave it to her across a coffee table in Watersmeet — has held it in her mouth a hundred times alone in this house and never let it out, the way her father and his father held the whole of it, the way the door stayed shut.

"Her name was Mindemoyen," she says.

It is not loud. She does not make it loud. She says it the way you set something down that you have carried a long way, carefully, on a flat surface, so that it will not tip.

The kitchen takes it in. No one repeats it. Marta's eyes are wet and she does not wipe them. Juhani looks at his hands. It is, Aino knows — the certainty arriving plainly, the way the cold arrives — the first time the woman's name has been spoken in this house in a hundred years, or in any of their houses, or among these families, who had her death between them all that time and never the one word that would have made her a person. Eino did not write it. Her father did not say it. And now it is in the room, four syllables in a cold kitchen, and the room is not the same room it was.

"Mindemoyen," Marta says, finally, very low. Saying it once, the way you say a name at a graveside.

"Yes."

---

Then the floor knocks.

Three times. Even, spaced, hard — coming up through the boards under the table from somewhere below the foundation, somewhere below the basement, somewhere there is nothing to knock. The cups go quiet. Aino feels it more than hears it, in her feet, in the legs of her chair.

A pause.

She knows the pause. She has lived inside it for a week. She counts it without deciding to, the way she always does now — one, two, three — and across the table she sees Juhani counting it too, his lips not moving, his eyes on the middle of the table, and she watches him reach seven the same instant she does —

— three more. The same three, the same weight, up through the floor.

Nobody moves. Marta's hand has stopped on her cup. Erik has gone the stillness of the man splitting wood with the maul resting, the stillness of a body waiting to know which way to break. The cold comes up off the boards in the held quiet, a flat lake-cold pushing out across the floor and finding their ankles one by one, and Aino sees them feel it — sees Marta's feet draw back under her chair, sees Juhani's hand close.

"That's it," Aino says, into the silence. Her own voice is steady and she is glad of it. "That's what's been in my floor every night."

"Lord God," Marta says, almost without breath.

Three knocks. The pause. The count. Three more. It is not the siege — she does not have the word for it yet but she knows this is the smaller version, the evening version, the thing announcing itself rather than the thing throwing its whole weight, and even so it has emptied the kitchen of everything but itself. Juhani has the look of a man who has spent thirty years angry at a dead grandfather and has just understood, finally, in his feet, that the grandfather was real and so is the rest of it. Erik's eyes are closed. He is listening to it the way he listened in his own kitchen when she said the seven seconds — the way a man listens to a thing he was hoping he had invented.

It goes a while. Then it thins, and stops, the way it stops in the morning, between one knock and the one that does not come.

The kitchen sits in what is left.

---

It is Reino who speaks first, and his voice is wrong for the room — ordinary, level, loud only by being normal in a place that has gone past normal.

"And that," he says, "is the thing you're going to stand on a shore and *nod at*."

Erik says, "Reino."

"No." He comes off the counter. He has heard it now too — she watched him hear it, watched even Reino go still while the floor counted — and where it has cracked the others open, it has closed him like a fist. "You all felt that. That's not grief looking to be acknowledged. You acknowledge a thing that's *done*. That" — he points down, at the floor, at the boards — "is not done. That is beating on the underside of this woman's house at" — he checks nothing, he does not need to — "nine at night on a Monday. And the plan is sunrise Wednesday we go down to the water and *witness* it." He says the word the way he said *the lake* in his brother's kitchen, with the bark off. "Two more nights of that. Because somebody downstate hasn't finished getting ready."

"That's theirs to lead," Mikko says. "We've been over it."

"I heard you the first time." He looks around the table, at the wet-eyed woman and the old men and the spread hands, and what is on his face is not heat. That is still the worst of it. It is contempt gone cold and certain, a man who has made up his mind so long ago that the meeting was only ever going to confirm it. "You want to know what I think you are. I think you're four people who've spent your whole lives being ashamed and you finally found a way to feel ashamed *together*, in a kitchen, with coffee, and you've mistaken that for doing something." He picks his cap up off the counter. "Standing on a shore at dawn with your hat in your hand is not doing something. It's the same nothing your grandfathers did, with better manners on it."

"Reino," Erik says again, and there is something under it now, his voice come down low. "Don't."

Reino looks at his brother a long moment. Erik lost his wife slow and came out quiet; Reino lost a boy fast, through ice, before any of them knew what the ice was, and never put the boy and the woman in the same sentence and has carried them in the same pocket ever since. None of that is said. It does not need to be. It is in the two faces, the same face with the patience taken out of one of them.

"You stand there and nod, then," Reino says. Quiet. Final. "I'll be doing the other thing." He puts the cap on. "Somebody's got to."

He goes. The door, when he opens it, lets the real cold in, the March cold, ordinary and clean, and then he is on the porch and down the steps and the truck door opens and shuts and the engine turns over and the headlights swing back across the kitchen wall and away.

Erik does not go after him. He sits with his cup and his eyes on the door, and he does not get up, and that is the thing Aino will remember — that the brother who could have gone after him chose, in front of all of them, not to. Because there was no point. Because they all knew it. Because the man who left is not going to be argued back across a threshold by anybody, least of all the brother who has spent a lifetime failing to.

---

The door is shut. The kitchen holds five people where a minute ago it held six, and the missing one is louder than the rest of them.

"He's going to do something," Marta says. Not to anyone. The way Erik said it in his own kitchen. *He won't wait.*

"Yes," Erik says.

Nobody says what. Nobody knows what, exactly, and Aino understands that the not-knowing is part of it now — that they will all of them, tonight and tomorrow, be living inside the gap where Reino's plan is, listening for it the way she has learned to listen for the floor.

The coffee has gone cold in the unmatched cups. The eaves are not running. Out the window over the sink the night is hard and still and starless, and somewhere across the dark of it the ice is taking the day's thaw back into itself, knitting shut for one more night the cracks that will not knit much longer.

"Wednesday," Juhani says, at last, the word set down one at a time. "First light. We'll be there. All of us that are coming." He looks at the door Reino went out of, and then away from it. "It'll have to be enough."

Aino does not say whether it will. She does not know. She gathers the cold cups, because it is what is done, and stands at the sink with the cold water running over her hands, and counts, without meaning to, the nights that are left.

Two. If they are lucky.

She is no longer sure they are going to be lucky.
