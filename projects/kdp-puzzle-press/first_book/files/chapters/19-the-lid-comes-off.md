title: The Lid Comes Off

It is past three when Erik calls, and she knows from the first word it has come due.

She has been at the kitchen table since they got back from the inlet, not doing anything, the cold cup of coffee in front of her that she made hours ago and did not drink. The afternoon is the warmest it has been. The eave runs steady now, no ticking to it, a thin continuous string of water off the corner of the porch, and the yard between the house and the sauna has gone from snow to a gray slush that holds the shape of her boots when she walks it. She has not walked it. She has sat at the table and waited for the dark to come, because Mikko said he would be back before dark, and the dark is the thing she is counting toward.

The phone is loud in the still house.

"Aino." It is Erik. His voice is wrong. Not loud. Pressed flat, the way a man's voice goes when he is holding it level with both hands. "Is Mikko there."

"He's on his way back. He's at the job."

"Reino's truck is gone." A breath. "I went by his place. Truck's gone and the saw's gone off the bench. The good saw, the big one." Another breath, and she can hear, under the level voice, the thing he is keeping out of it. "He's not answering. I been calling since noon."

She is standing. She does not remember standing.

"The lake," she says.

"Our spot." It comes out of him like something pulled. "The Saari spot, the point on the north end. It's where we always — it's where the boy went in, Aino. It's where we keep. He's gone down to the spot."

"With the saw."

"With the saw."

She is already reaching for her coat.

---

Mikko's truck comes up the road as she is on the porch, and she does not have to flag him; he sees her standing in the slush with her coat half on and he stops in the yard with the door open before she reaches him.

"Reino," she says. "Erik can't find him. The truck's gone, the chainsaw's gone. Erik thinks he's gone to the Saari spot to do it himself."

Mikko does not ask what *it* is. He has been in the gap with the rest of them since Monday night. His face does the thing she has watched it do all week, the practical man arriving fast at the end of his explanations, and then he says, "Get in," and she is in, and the truck is already turning before her door is shut.

The north end is twenty minutes by the road around the bay. Mikko does it in less. Neither of them talks. The lake runs along on their left between the trees, white still, but a wrong white, a flat dull white with no shine to it, the white of ice that has stopped being ice on the inside and has not yet admitted it on the top. Where the road comes near the shore she can see the dark seams in it, the long gray lines standing out from the point, and the open black where the creek mouths come in.

The cold is in her chest the whole way. Not the room cold of the house at night. A live one, climbing.

She thinks: *let his truck not be there.* She thinks it the way you think a thing you already know the answer to.

His truck is there. Pulled off at the end of the spur where the Saaris put in, nosed up against the snowbank, the door hanging open and the dome light burning weak in the daylight.

---

He is out on the ice.

She sees him the moment they come over the bank, and the seeing of him is the worst thing yet, worse than the black hole at the inlet, worse than the glove, because the glove was after and this is during. He is a long way out. Two hundred feet, more, out past where any man with sense would stand on the best ice of January, out at the family's spot where the point reaches and the water goes deep fast, a small dark figure on a white field gone the color of a dead tooth, and the sound reaches them a half second behind the sight — the saw, the high tearing whine of it, carrying flat across the open ice the way sound carries over water.

Erik is on the shore. His truck is below them on the spur and he is down at the edge of the ice, not on it, at the very lip of it, and he is shouting his brother's name. It does not carry. The saw eats it. *Reino.* The word goes out across the ice and the saw takes it and Reino does not turn.

They come down the bank fast, the slush giving under them, and Erik hears them and turns and his face is the face of a man at the end of something.

"He won't —" Erik can't finish it. He points. "He cut. He's already cut."

She looks where he points and she sees it. Out at the spot there is a line in the ice, dark, an arc of it, a curve cut into the white, and Reino is bent over the far end of it with the saw, finishing the circle. Cutting himself a circle on the worst ice on the lake in the warmest March in forty years. Doing the lake's own work for it, with a tool from his own bench.

And he is shouting. Between the cuts, when the saw drops to an idle, she can hear it — his voice coming back across the ice in pieces, the way the sheriff's voice came back across the inlet, broken into syllables that do not make words. He is shouting down at the cut. Down at the water coming up dark in the cut. She catches a piece of it, a hard guttural piece that might be Finnish, a word his grandmother might have had, and then the saw climbs again and takes the rest.

She does not understand it. She is not meant to. There is no one out there to understand it but him, and he does not understand it either. He has gone out onto the ice with a chainsaw and some words he half-remembers and a lifetime of rage at the thing that took his boy, and he is going to make it stop by cutting it open and standing over it and saying the words at it, the way you would face down a dog, the way you would face down a man. He has decided it is a thing you can face. He has decided it is a thing you can beat if you only stop being afraid of it.

He has it exactly wrong, and there is no way to tell him, and he is two hundred feet out.

---

Erik goes for the ice.

He is past her before she knows it, down the last of the bank and out onto the white, two steps, three, his boots breaking through the rotten skin of it at the shore where the inch of meltwater stands, and Mikko has him.

Mikko has him from behind, both arms locked around Erik's chest, hauling backward, and Erik fights it. Erik is a big man and grief has made him stronger and he fights, drives back with his heels, gets a hand on the ice and Mikko drags him off it by main force, the two of them going down together in the slush at the shoreline, Mikko underneath and not letting go.

"It won't hold you," Mikko says into the back of Erik's neck. He is not shouting. He says it flat and hard and close. "Erik. It won't hold him and it won't hold you. You go out there you go in too. You hear me. You go in too and there's two of you."

"That's my brother." Erik's voice breaks on it. "That's my *brother*, that's all that's —"

"I know it is." Mikko does not loosen his arms. "I know. You can't reach him. There's no reaching him. You go out there and Aino watches both of you go down."

And Erik stops fighting.

That is the thing she will carry out of this, the way she carries the shape under the blanket: the moment Erik stops fighting. Not because he agrees. Because he knows it is true. He goes slack in Mikko's arms in the meltwater at the edge of the lake, a big man gone boneless with the truth of it, and Mikko holds him anyway, holds him so he cannot change his mind, and over his brother's shoulder Erik looks out at the ice at the small dark figure of the man he cannot reach, and he watches.

They all watch. That is what the holding costs them. They cannot do anything but the one thing, which is watch, and they do it.

---

The saw stops.

Not the way it stopped before, dropping to an idle for the shouting. It stops all the way, the whine falling out of the air, and in the place where it was there is the enormous quiet of the open lake in the afternoon, the eave-drip quiet, the March quiet, a crow somewhere back in the trees, and Reino standing at the spot with the circle cut and the saw gone silent in his hands.

He stands over it. From the shore he is very small and very clear. He has the saw down at his side and he is looking into the cut, into the dark coming up, and he says one more thing to it — she sees his head go back, sees the shape of him draw up to say it — and she does not hear any of this one. Whatever it was. The last of his words, gone out over the water with no one to receive them.

Then the ice lets go under him.

There is no drama to it. That is the horror of it, that there is no drama. The white around the cut simply ceases to be a surface. It does not crack and pitch and throw him; it gives, all at once, a circle of the world going from solid to not, and he drops. Straight down. The way a man drops through a trapdoor. There and then a darkness where he was and the saw gone with him and a low flat sound coming across the ice a half second late, a sound like a heavy door closing in a far room, and water standing up black in the place where a man was standing.

Erik makes a sound she will not describe to anyone, ever.

She does not look away. She had thought she would. At the inlet she turned and looked at the far trees and was glad of it after. She does not turn now. She makes herself hold it, the black place out at the spot where the white closed and reopened, because someone should, because Erik is held face-down in the slush in Mikko's arms with his eyes shut and his mouth open and no sound coming now, and Mikko has his face in Erik's coat, and somebody on this shore has to be the one who saw it, and it is going to be her.

The water does not move. It stands in the cut, black and flat, the way it stood at the inlet, the lake's own depth come up where it has no business being, and it does not give him back. The saw does not float. Nothing floats. There is the cut, and the dark in it, and the white all around going gray in the late light, and the cold goes through the soles of her boots and up through her and out the top of her head, and she stands in the slush at the edge of the lake and she does not look away.

---

Then the lake goes.

She feels it before she hears it, up through her feet, through the rotten shore ice and the slush and the ground — a long shudder, a deep give, the whole white field out in front of her shifting its weight like an animal that has been lying still too long. And then the sound. It comes from the spot, from the place where he went down, and it runs out from there across the whole lake at once, a cracking that is not one crack but ten thousand, a tearing that goes out to every shore, north and south and the far trees and back, the cracks running out from the point the way a windshield runs from the stone, fast, faster than a thing that size should be able to move, and under it a deeper sound, a groan she feels in her sternum, the sound of a hundred square miles of ice letting go of itself in one breath.

It runs the length of the lake toward the south end. Toward her end. She can see it go — a line of dark opening seams racing down the white toward the Hietala shore, toward the point she was told never to swim past, toward the slope below her father's sauna.

And she knows, standing on the north shore with her boots full of meltwater and Erik on his knees in front of her, exactly what is happening at her own shore. She knows it the way she has come to know things this week, below the place where questions live. Down at the Hietala end the seams are reaching the shallows under the sauna. The lake is pushing up against the pilings her great-grandfather drove into the bottom a hundred years ago, the pilings that have held the little building down at the water's edge through a hundred breakups, and the ice is shoving against them now all at once with the weight of the whole lake behind it, and the old wood is taking it, and the old wood is groaning, out there in the empty yard with no one to hear it, the way a thing groans that has been holding and is asked, suddenly, to hold more.

The lid. She thinks the word once, flat, the way her father wrote a number in a ledger. The lid that her great-grandfather and her grandfather and her father each sat on once a week for a hundred years with the heat going up around them and the weight going down. The watch that was not a watch. The man in the sauna keeping the weight of it down.

There is no man in the sauna. There has not been, since October.

And now there is no ice either.

---

It is dusk before they get Erik up the bank.

He walks between them, finally, when there is nothing else to do — when it is plain even to him that there is no part of the afternoon he can change by staying at the water — and they put him in the cab of his own truck and Mikko calls it in on the truck radio, the township channel, the flat low voices starting up that she heard at the inlet, *north end, the Saari spot, no, there's no recovering anybody off that ice tonight or tomorrow either,* and a man's voice answering, careful, that they'll send what they can in the morning if the lake will let them, and everybody on the channel knowing it will not.

There will be no shape under a blanket for Reino. There is no ice left to walk out on and no honest water under it. He is in the lake now, with her, at the spot, and that is where he will stay.

She stands by the open door of his truck while Mikko sees to Erik, and she looks back out at the lake one more time before the light is gone. It is open in long black channels now, north to south, the white broken into floes already starting to move on the slow current, and the cold coming off it is no longer the soft rotten warmth of the afternoon. It is the cold off open water. The cold of the thing with nothing over it.

She thinks of the south shore, dark now, the sauna standing at the edge of the moving ice with its pilings groaning in the dusk and no one there. She thinks of the house above it, the cold already coming up through the floor, waiting for her.

She has to go back to that tonight.

"One more night," she says. Not to Mikko, not to Erik. To the lake, or to the woman in it, or to no one. She says it the way she said it to the cedar wall yesterday, the way you say a number you mean to outlast, except that yesterday it was one more day and a thing held down, and now it is one more night and nothing holding it.

The eave is not running anymore. The day's warmth has gone with the light, and the cold has come down hard and clean, and out on the black water the broken ice grinds against itself and moves.
