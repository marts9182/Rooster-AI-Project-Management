Highway 2 to 45 to 51. The same road in reverse, the trees opening up as she crosses back into Wisconsin, the right-of-way widening, the towns getting larger and farther apart in a different way — not the emptying-out of the iron range but the filling-in of the lower half of the state, more houses, more traffic, more of everything that means she is going south.

The percolator rides on the passenger seat. She has wrapped it in her fleece jacket and buckled the seatbelt around it, which she did without thinking about it and noticed only after, and left it.

The radio is on. The basketball season is still going — it is only March, there is still basketball to be played — and she finds a game and lets it run. The same big unhurried voice. Something definite happening.

She drives through Ironwood. She drives through Hurley. The body, she finds, leaves more slowly than the car. The car does sixty-five and the towns fall away behind her at sixty-five, and some part of her is paying them out like line off a reel, going with them, staying. She had expected the miles to subtract. To take a little of the house with each one, a little of the lake, until at some town with a stoplight and a Dollar General she would be only herself again, only Aino, going home. That is not what the miles do. They lengthen the thing between her and the place without thinning it. Her shoulders come down from her ears somewhere past Hurley — that much loosens, the watchfulness she'd carried for six weeks, the held readiness of a person sleeping in a dead man's house. But underneath the watchfulness there is something that does not loosen, that the distance only draws out finer and tighter, a wire run north out of her sternum and anchored to a frozen shore she can no longer see. She is facing south. A part of her is still pointed the other way, the way a compass needle does not care which direction you walk.

She crosses back into Wisconsin and the road straightens and the sky opens up and she has the clear, clean feeling of a highway in front of her for a long distance, and behind her, steady, the weight of everything she is driving away from, not letting go.

She thinks about the six weeks. She lines them up in her mind the way she would line up the facts of a case at work, in order, each one leading to the next. The drive up. Hakala's. The house. The funeral. The will. The sauna. The ledgers. Watersmeet. The shore. The fire. She can see the shape of it now from the outside, which she couldn't while she was in it — the way each thing opened the next, the way she kept going one step further than she'd planned, the way the book she'd thought she was reading turned out to be a different book entirely.

She thinks about Mikko. The way he dried dishes without being asked. The way he said *I'll come back* after she said it, quietly, as though he were testing the weight of it.

She thinks about her father's ledger. *I am running out of time to look.*

She is thirty-eight. She has time.

The question is what she wants to do with it.

She stops for gas at the Kwik Trip off exit 185, Maple Ridge Road, north of Wausau — the same stretch of highway she'd been on the day the lawyer called, when she'd been on hold with Social Security and her whole life had changed direction without her permission. It had started snowing right about here, that first drive up. Now it is bare and brown, the median grass flattened and dead, the white sky pressing down the way it does in March before it has decided whether to be spring.

She fills the tank. The pump handle is cold through her glove. She goes inside for coffee, past the glass case of cheese curds and the cooler of half-gallon milk in the Kwik Trip way, and stands at the counter waiting for her change and becomes aware, gradually, of something she can't immediately name.

Not a sound. Not a feeling exactly. More like — a direction. A pull, faint but specific, the way you can feel a draft in a room without being able to say where it's coming from. It is coming from the northeast. She knows this without turning to check. She knows it the way she knew the lake was there even when she couldn't see it from the driveway, the weight of it sitting in the dark beyond the trees, attending.

She takes her change. She takes her coffee. She stands in the parking lot.

The highway is right there. South. Milwaukee is four hours. Her apartment, her job, the radiator that will start banging at four in the morning, the building that looks indifferent when she leaves it.

She gets in the car.

She pulls back onto the highway. The basketball game comes back on. She drives south and the pull is still there, specific and quiet, from the northeast, and she notes it the way her father would have noted it — in the language of observation, without interpretation, a fact to be recorded and returned to.

She drives for another twenty minutes.

There is an exit. She has not planned to take it. It goes northeast, toward a part of Wisconsin she has driven through but doesn't know well — lake country, small towns, a different kind of quiet than the iron range. She doesn't know why this exit. She doesn't know what is up that road.

She takes it.

The highway recedes behind her. The trees close in on either side, not the pines of the U.P. but hardwoods, bare-branched, gray and white in the March light. The road narrows. A lake appears through the trees on her right, frozen, the ice still holding at the edges, open water showing dark at the center.

She slows.

The pull is stronger here. Not urgent, not alarming — the same quality as the thing under the hatch in the sauna, something that has been waiting a long time and has the patience of a thing that has been waiting a long time.

She pulls over on the shoulder and shuts the engine off, and the radio dies, and the heat dies, and in the quiet that comes in after them she understands that she is not only looking at the lake. The lake is the colder of the two of them and the cold comes off it the way it comes off an open freezer door, a slow exhalation across the ditch, through the seams of the car, onto the backs of her hands. The air near the water has a different grain than the air at the gas station, denser, older, the way a deep house feels different from a new one. She had thought of it as a pull, a draft, something with a source she was the subject of. Sitting here she revises it. It is not pulling. It is regarding her. Without eyes, without urgency, with the long even attention of water under ice — it has registered that a person has stopped on its shore, and taken her in, and gone on holding still. Her father had this. She is sure of it now, sure of it in the cold on her hands: this was what he meant by *attending to the lake*, not watching it but being inside the circle of its noticing. She is inside it. It knows the shape of the car and the warmth going out of it and the small live heat of her, and it is in no hurry at all.

She thinks about what Louise said. *It's a beginning. Not an ending.*

She thinks about her father's note. *Maybe you will.*

She thinks about what she said in the sauna the second time, to the cold and the heat and whatever was listening: *I'm going to find out how to make it right.*

She picks up her phone. She searches for the county. She searches for recorded land disputes, for townships, for families. She is a paralegal. She knows how to read a paper trail.

She finds something in ten minutes that takes her another ten to understand fully. A property on this lake, a deed dispute going back four generations, a family name that appears in county records in 1887 and then in different records in 1921 and the gap between those two dates is the shape of something she recognizes.

She puts the phone down.

She looks at the lake.

She will call Louise. She will ask if she knows this lake, this family, this name. She will find out if there is someone else who needs to know what she now knows how to look for.

She doesn't do any of that yet. She just sits in the car on the shoulder of a road she didn't plan to take, the cold steady on the backs of her hands, and looks at a frozen lake she didn't know existed this morning, and she thinks about her father counting the minutes of phone calls, and her grandfather going to a pastor who didn't understand the question, and her great-grandfather writing *kaunis* in a notebook in 1919, beautiful, underlined.

She thinks: I can hear it now.

She doesn't say it out loud. She doesn't need to.

She puts the car in drive.

She does not go back to the highway.
