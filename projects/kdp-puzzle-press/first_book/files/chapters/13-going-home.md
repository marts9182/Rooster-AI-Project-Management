She rides back from Watersmeet in Mikko's passenger seat, watching the pines go by in the fading light. He drops her at the house and she stands in the cold driveway a moment watching his taillights disappear down the gravel road before going inside.

The sky outside the kitchen window is the dull pewter of a day that never quite found the sun. She puts the kettle on and stands at the counter thinking about what Louise said.

*The watching — it was containment. It also kept things from getting better.*

She has been thinking about her father's life, since she got here, in terms of what he failed to do. What he didn't tell her, what he didn't fix, what he couldn't bring himself to start. But there is another way to look at it, she thinks now. He sat in that sauna for thirty years. Every week, sometimes more. He went down the hill in the dark, in the cold, in every season, and he lit the stove and he opened the hatch and he watched. That is thirty years of keeping a promise he never made, inherited from a man who made the wrong one.

It is not enough. Louise said so and she already knew it. But it is not nothing, either.

She thinks about the first Aino. Her great-grandmother, who looked at Eino for a long time and did not speak, and then lived in that house for forty-three years with the thing he'd done and the thing he'd built over it and never went near the sauna. She wonders what the first Aino thought about in the night. Whether she talked to anyone. Whether there was a Helmi Korhonen for her, someone to tell the true version to.

She thinks there probably wasn't. 1919 was a different kind of alone.

---

At home she makes soup from what's in the refrigerator — there's not much, she needs to go back to the grocery store — and eats standing at the counter, which she does sometimes in Milwaukee and which in her father's kitchen feels different, feels like something he would have done, and she lets herself stand there and eat his soup and be his daughter for a few minutes without that being complicated.

Then she washes the bowl and sits at the kitchen table with a cup of tea and thinks clearly, as clearly as she has since she got here.

The ledgers need to go to Louise. All of them, or at least the ones from before her father — Eino's notebook, Toivo's ledgers. Her father's forty-two she is less certain about. They are his handwriting, his life, his thirty years of watching. She doesn't know if they belong with Louise or with her or nowhere, buried with her father. That question can wait.

The sauna itself. She has been avoiding thinking about this directly, but she makes herself think about it now. The structure has been there since 1919 in one form or another. Her father put new cedar in it the last summer she was home. He kept wood stacked and ready for the stove. He maintained it the way you maintain something important, something that has a function you cannot abandon.

Now she is the one deciding its function.

The things in the lockbox — the key she hasn't tried yet, whatever door it opens. The carvings on the walls. The tools in the basement whose purpose she still doesn't fully understand. All of this is information she doesn't have yet, and she needs it before she decides anything.

She gets out a piece of paper and makes a list, because she is a paralegal and lists are how she thinks:

*What Louise's family wants and when.*

*What the key in the lockbox opens.*

*What the carvings mean and who to ask.*

*What to do with father's ledgers.*

*What to do with the sauna.*

*What to do with the house.*

*What to do after.*

She looks at the last item. She has not thought past the immediate problem in any serious way. She has an apartment in Milwaukee, a job, a life that has been on hold for two weeks and will eventually need to be returned to. She will have to go back.

She thinks about Mikko saying: *Are you ever thinking about coming back?*

She thinks about the way the cold hit her when she got out of the car that first night, the quality of it, the specific U.P. dryness she'd forgotten. She thinks about the percolator. The photograph. The pines outside the window making the light different from any light in Milwaukee.

She thinks about the drive north on Highway 51, the trees closing in, the radio cutting out on that stretch of highway where it always cuts out, the silence in the car that wasn't just silence.

She has been here for nearly three weeks. She has not thought about Milwaukee in several of them.

She folds the list and puts it in her coat pocket with the envelope and the photograph of herself at eight. She goes to bed at a reasonable hour and lies in the dark and listens to the house, which is quiet tonight — no sound from the lake, no knocking, just the furnace and the pines.

She said something into the hatch and the knocking stopped.

She doesn't know what that means. She doesn't know if it means anything. But she is aware, lying in the dark of her childhood bedroom under her grandmother's quilt, that the house feels different than it did when she arrived. Not less strange. But differently strange. As though whatever is in the lake has registered her presence and is — not waiting, exactly. Attending. The way you attend to someone who has finally said the right thing after a long time of saying the wrong things.

She sleeps. She dreams of nothing she can remember in the morning.

When she wakes the sun is coming through the bedsheet curtains and the yard outside is empty. No new prints in the snow.

She makes coffee. She looks at her list.

She starts with the key.
