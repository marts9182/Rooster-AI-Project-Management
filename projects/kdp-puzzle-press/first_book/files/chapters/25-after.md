She gives herself a week.

The house needs to be gone through properly — not the frantic triage of the first few days but the real work, the kind that requires decision rather than just sorting. What goes with her, what goes to the Lutheran church thrift store, what goes in the dumpster she rents from a place in Iron Mountain that delivers it on a Thursday morning without comment.

The man who drops the dumpster does the work in under five minutes and is gone, and she stands looking at it in the yard, green and dented and far too large for one house, and thinks she will never fill it. She fills it. It takes four days.

The furniture she leaves for the estate sale, which Ernest Autio arranges through a woman in Marquette who does this for a living. The woman comes on a Friday and walks through the house with a clipboard, assigning values to things Aino has been looking at for five weeks without quite seeing. Her father's chair. The bookshelf of westerns. The kitchen table where she has eaten most of her meals since she arrived. The woman says the table is worth something, it's old, good wood. Aino tells her to sell it.

The woman is brisk and kind and treats the contents of the house as inventory, which is exactly what Aino needs. She asks no questions about the family. She mentions, while pricing the dishes, that she's been busy this spring, more estates than usual, the old people going and the children gone downstate with no use for any of it. She says it the way you'd remark on the weather. She prices the percolator at four dollars and Aino tells her that one stays.

She keeps three things from the main house. The quilt her grandmother made her, which she folds and puts in the back seat of the Subaru. The photograph of herself at eight with the fish, which she wraps in a dish towel. And the percolator, which she wraps in a sweater and nestles into a box of kitchen things and then at the last minute takes out of the box and puts on the passenger seat, where she can see it.

The forty-two ledgers she goes back and forth on for days.

She reads through more of them — not all, she doesn't have time for all, but enough to understand the full shape of what they contain. Thirty years of her father's life in those pages. Thirty years of weather and ice and the other kind of entry, the ones that sound like a man talking to something he can't name. They are, she thinks, the most honest thing he ever wrote. More honest than the phone calls, more honest than the note he left in the envelope, because they were written without an audience, for no one, just a man and a lake and the thing in it and the words he used to describe the watching.

She wraps them carefully in plastic sheeting from the basement and packs them in a box and puts the box in the Subaru. She will figure out later whether they belong with Louise or with her or somewhere else. She's not ready to decide and she's learned, in the past few weeks, to trust that instinct.

The stone foundation at the water's edge she leaves alone. The ash and debris from the fire she leaves for weather and time. In spring, when the ice goes out, Louise and her family will come back. They said so. She has left a key with Mikko.

---

She goes into town twice that week, both times for things she could have done without — boxes from the hardware store, a roll of packing tape, gas — but the truth is the house gets to be too much by the middle of the afternoon and she needs to be a person in a place where other people are.

The first thing she sees is the sign in the window of Hakala's. FOR SALE, hand-lettered, and a phone number with a Marquette area code, and under it, smaller, BY OWNER. The Leinenkugel's sign is still lit in the other window, red and gold in the gray afternoon, which somehow makes it worse. She stands on the sidewalk and looks at it. Forty years, maybe more. Her father drank there. Paavo Maki drank there. It was where the neighbor figured out something was wrong the morning her father didn't show, because Hank always showed, and the not-showing was the first thing anyone noticed. Now there is a number to call.

The girl at the gas station tells her the rest of it, unasked, the way news moves up here. The diner closed in February — Aino had not known, had not thought to wonder — and now Hakala's, and the Marathon two towns over went to pumps-only with no one inside. The girl is maybe nineteen and says it without much feeling, the way you'd describe a road you don't drive anymore. Seven hundred and some people now, she says, like that's a number everybody carries. Aino remembers fourteen hundred. She remembers a town. She pays for the gas.

It is a hard week to be in Kivijärvi. There is a funeral notice taped inside the door of the gas station, photocopied, a young man's face — Dale Numminen, the snowmobiler, out of Tioga, gone through the ice at the inlet in the thaw. He wasn't from here but the funeral is here, the church up the hill, because the U.P. is small that way and the families know each other across two counties. The flag at the post office is at half-staff and she doesn't know for whom — Numminen, or the season generally, or for Reino Saari, who went through the ice the same way and is simply gone, no body, nothing to put in the ground when it thaws. You can't bury a man you don't have. The town is doing its slow arithmetic of the dead and she is at the edge of it, a person passing through, and that is the right place for her to be.

She sees Erik Saari once, outside the hardware store, getting into his truck. Reino's brother. He looks like a man who has aged a winter in a month. She doesn't go to him. There is nothing she could say that would not be an intrusion, and she suspects he is grateful, in town, for the people who let him alone. She lifts a hand. He lifts one back. That is the whole of it.

She has a conversation with Ernest about the house. She is selling it below market because she wants it gone quickly and because market in Kivijärvi in early spring is not a number that requires much deliberation. There is a couple from downstate — Marquette, which barely counts as downstate up here, but close enough — who have been looking for a lake property and are willing to move fast. They have not been told anything about the sauna or the foundation or what happened here. Aino has thought about this and decided that what they don't know won't hurt them. The foundation is just stones. The lake is just a lake.

She hopes this is true.

She thinks it probably is, mostly.

---

Mikko comes over the last evening. She makes dinner — the last of what's in the refrigerator, a cobbled-together thing involving eggs and the end of a loaf of bread and some cheese — and they eat at the kitchen table, which will be someone else's kitchen table by the end of the month. The dog stays in the truck with the window cracked, which he allows because Mikko tells him to, and once or twice during dinner Aino hears it shift and settle out there in the dark.

"You could stay," he says. Not for the first time, but differently this time — quieter, without the expectation of being argued with. He says it and then goes on eating, as if he has set the thing down on the table between them and has no intention of picking it back up.

"I have a job," she says. "An apartment."

"I know."

She looks at him across the table. He is looking at his plate with the expression of a man who has said what he wanted to say and is not going to belabor it. She has come, in these past weeks, to understand this about him — the saying of the thing once, cleanly, and then the letting it be. She finds it, against her expectations, one of the things she likes most about him.

"It's not that I don't —" she starts, and stops, because she doesn't know how that sentence ends and she's not going to fake the end of it. He waits. He's good at that, the waiting. "I don't know what I'm going back to," she says instead. "I mean I know what's there. The work. The people I know. I just don't know if it's — " She turns her fork over. "I've been here six weeks. The worst six weeks of my life, some of them. And I feel more like myself than I have in years. I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it right now," he says.

"That's what people say."

"It's still true."

She almost smiles. "What about you," she says. "You could come down."

"To Milwaukee." He considers it the way he considers everything, without hurry, giving it real weight before he sets it aside. "I've got the crew. The plowing through April. The dog." He says it without self-pity, a man inventorying the actual shape of his life. "Laura's in Marquette. We don't talk, but she's there. I left once. Came back. I'm not built for leaving twice." He looks up. "That's not a no. It's just where I am."

"I know where you are," she says, and means more by it than the geography, and he hears the more and lets that be too.

They finish dinner. She washes the dishes and he dries them, which is something they've fallen into without discussing it, and she is aware of the specific domesticity of it, two people in a kitchen, the ordinary rhythm of the wash and the hand-off and the wipe and the stacking. The water goes from hot to lukewarm. There is one good knife and they both reach for it and laugh, a little, at nothing. She thinks about the apartment in Bay View. The radiator that will start banging at four in the morning as soon as the temperature drops. The building that looks indifferent when she leaves and indifferent when she comes home, which she had thought, for years, was what she wanted.

She thinks about this house. What it felt like to pull into the driveway that first night, in the dark, her father's truck sitting there. What it felt like to put her hand on the cold percolator. How much has happened in the room they are standing in.

"I'll come back," she says. She says it to the dish she's washing, not to him.

"Okay," he says.

He doesn't ask when. He doesn't say he'll hold her to it. He takes the dish out of her hand and dries it and puts it on the stack with the others, and the not-asking is the kindest thing he could have done, because she does not actually know if it's true. She wants it to be true. She has learned the difference between those two things this spring and she is not going to pretend, tonight, in this kitchen, that she hasn't.

Their hands meet over the next dish, briefly, the way accidental things happen, and then less accidentally, and neither of them says anything about it.

He leaves at nine. The dog is glad to see him. She stands in the doorway and watches the taillights go down the gravel road for what might be the last time and might not be, and she lets it be the second thing for as long as she can.

She goes back inside. The kitchen is clean. The house is nearly empty. The percolator sits on the counter and she looks at it for a moment and then leaves it where it is for tonight.

She goes to bed in her childhood bedroom under her grandmother's quilt and listens to the house — the furnace, the pines, the particular language of an old house settling into cold — and she falls asleep faster than she has since she arrived.

She does not dream.

In the morning she loads the Subaru. The percolator goes on the passenger seat. She locks the house and puts the key in the lockbox Ernest installed on the front porch. She stands in the driveway for a moment.

The lake is not visible from here. She can feel it anyway, the way she always could, the weight of that much cold water at the edge of the property, the particular quality of the air that comes off it. She has been able to feel it her whole life, she thinks. She just never let herself know what she was feeling.

She gets in the car. She drives down the gravel road. At the end of it she turns south, toward the highway, toward Milwaukee, toward the life that is waiting for her to decide what to do with it.

She passes the cross street that goes down to the lake.

She doesn't stop.

But she slows.
