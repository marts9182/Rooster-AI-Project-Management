She goes to the gas station in the morning for coffee because the percolator is doing something wrong — not broken, just off, the coffee coming out thin and pale, which might be the grind or the water or something she's doing differently from her father — and she is standing at the counter waiting for the attendant to ring her up when someone says her name.

She turns. A man about her age, maybe a year or two older, coming in from outside with the cold still on him. Tall, broad through the shoulders, wearing a canvas work jacket and boots that have actually been worked in. Dark blond hair going gray at the sides, a face she knows.

"Mikko," she says.

Mikko Aaltonen. She has not thought about him in years. She has thought about him occasionally, in the way you think about people who were important to you at seventeen and whom you've reduced, over time, to a single representative image: in his case the front seat of a truck, winter, the heater running, a conversation she can no longer reconstruct but remembers the feeling of. Important, at the time. She'd left and he hadn't and that had been that.

"I heard you were in town," he says. He looks at her with the careful expression of a man who has rehearsed this moment. "I'm sorry about your dad."

"Thank you. He was — " she stops. "He was good to you, I think? You two stayed close?"

"He hired my crew a few times over the years. Siding, a few years back. Roof repairs." He takes a cup from the coffee station, fills it. He doesn't look at her while he does this. "He talked about you."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Then it's probably true." He looks at her now. Something in his face that she can't read, or isn't trying hard enough to read. "I was going to head over to the diner for breakfast if you want to —"

"Yeah," she says. "I do."

The diner is warm and smells like bacon and the particular coffee of a place that has been making it the same way since 1974. They take a booth by the window. The waitress knows Mikko and looks at Aino with the frank curiosity of someone who has been briefed. Aino orders eggs and toast and is aware that this will be talked about before she reaches the end of the block.

They talk about easy things first, the way people do who have history and are being careful with it. She asks about his business. He runs a small construction outfit, he and two other guys, mostly residential work around the range. He asks about Milwaukee. She tells him about the job, the apartment, the outline of a life that sounds, as she describes it, more provisional than she'd thought of it.

"You ever think about coming back?" he asks.

"No," she says, without hesitation, and then immediately thinks of the notebooks spread across the kitchen table last night, her grandfather's last entry, *she would not have to carry this.* "I don't know," she says. "Maybe."

He looks at her. "How long are you staying?"

"I'm not sure anymore."

He nods like this is the answer he expected. He wraps both hands around his coffee cup. Outside the window the main street of Kivijärvi is quiet, a pickup truck going past, a woman walking a dog. The ordinary world.

"The sauna," he says, and her attention sharpens.

She waits.

"I saw the smoke yesterday," he says. "From the road. I wasn't going to say anything." He is looking at his coffee. "I knew your dad. Not the way you knew him, but — enough. And I know what the sauna meant."

"What do you know about it?" She keeps her voice even.

He is quiet for a moment. "My grandfather knew your great-grandfather. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Eino and my grandfather were friends. They came over around the same time, same region. Worked the mine together." He pauses. "There are things my grandfather told my father and my father told me. Things you don't — " He stops. He looks out the window. "My grandfather said that what Eino did, that winter, was the worst thing he'd ever seen a man do. And that Eino spent the rest of his life trying to make it right by watching, and that watching wasn't making it right, and that by the time my grandfather understood that it was too late to do anything about it."

She stares at him. "Your grandfather was there."

"No," Mikko says. "He wasn't. But he knew the men who were. He knew what happened." He finally looks at her. "I've known my whole life. Everyone whose grandfather was around in 1919 knows, on some level, though most of them have worked very hard not to. It's a small town. It has always been a small town."

She thinks about Helmi Korhonen in the church basement, glancing around to see who was in earshot before she spoke.

"What happened when you went in?" he asks.

"I saw something in the water." She says it plainly, as a fact, the same way she would report something at work. This is what I observed. "Under the ice. I don't know what it was."

He lets out a breath. "Your father went every week. For thirty years."

"I know. I read the ledgers."

He nods. He looks out the window again. The pickup is long gone. The street is empty.

"What do you want to do?" he asks.

It's the right question, she thinks. Not what should she do, not what does she have to do. What does she want to do. She has not been asked that question since she got here. She has not asked it of herself.

"I want to understand what happened in 1919," she says. "And I want to talk to someone who knows."

"Someone from the band," he says. Not a question.

"I have a name."

He nods slowly. "They may not want to talk to you."

"I know."

"But if they do — if they're willing —" He picks up his coffee cup. Sets it down again. "Whatever you decide to do about it, I'll help. If you want. That's all I wanted to say."

She looks at him across the table, this man she knew when she was seventeen, who has lived his whole life in the long shadow of something his grandfather knew and never spoke of properly, who is offering now, carefully and without drama, to help.

"Okay," she says.

They finish their breakfast. The conversation finds its way to easier things after that, old names, what became of people, who is still around. She laughs once, genuinely, about something she can't remember the moment after, and it surprises her — the laugh itself, the ease of it. It has been a long week.

Outside on the sidewalk they stand in the cold for a moment.

"Call me," he says. "When you know what you want to do."

She says she will. She watches him walk to his truck. She stands on the sidewalk in the February cold with her hands in her pockets and thinks that she has, without quite deciding to, found someone in this town who is not trying to talk her into leaving.

She drives back to the house. She finds the name she wrote on the back of her father's envelope.

She calls.
