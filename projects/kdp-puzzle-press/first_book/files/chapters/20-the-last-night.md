Tuesday into Wednesday. The worst night.

There are two dead today. Dale Numminen at the inlet in the morning, found by his own headlight, the snowmobile idling against a drift. And in the afternoon a Saari — one of Erik's people, the one who'd said the only mercy left was to end it now — had taken a chainsaw down to his family's spot and cut a circle in the ice and let it take him. After that the ice began to go. Not in one place. Everywhere. The whole lake, all at once, the cracks running out from the point the way a windshield runs when the stone has already hit.

So Wednesday cannot come fast enough, and Wednesday is still a whole night away.

Mikko is staying. He'd told her that yesterday, before any of this, before either man was dead — *I'll be back before dark and you are not going to be alone for this.* He came back before dark with his duffel and his own coat and a second thermometer in his pocket, and he has not left her side since the sun went down.

It is the kind of cold that has nothing to do with weather. The thermometer on the porch reads twenty-six, an ordinary March night that has taken back the day's thaw the way these nights do. That is not the cold she means. The cold she means is the other one, the one that comes up through the boards.

---

**Nine o'clock.**

It comes before she is even thinking about bed, before the dark is fully settled, before she has decided whether she is going to try to sleep at all. It comes through the floor the way it has the past two nights, but stronger now, and it comes through the walls too, from the direction of the lake, a flat cold with weight behind it, and she feels it move across her feet like water finding the low place in a room.

She and Mikko are in the kitchen when it starts. She watches him notice it — the slight stiffening, the look of a man registering something his body knows before his mind has caught up to it. He looks at her. She looks at him.

"It knows tomorrow is Wednesday," she says.

She goes and gets the small thermometer from the bathroom, the cheap white plastic one with the red line and the little curl of metal hook for hanging it on a nail. She is going to walk the house. She tells herself it is so she will know. The truth is closer to the way a person counts stairs in the dark — to have something to do with the part of the mind that would otherwise be screaming.

Front room, sixty-eight. The furnace running, the register ticking warm air out across her shins. Hallway, sixty-four. Her bedroom, sixty-one. She holds the thermometer a foot off the floor and then a foot below the ceiling and the difference is real; the cold is coming up, not down, here. The back bedroom. She stands in the doorway with the thermometer out in front of her like a candle and watches the red line settle, and it settles at fifty-two, and the room is the same room it was that afternoon — the iron bed, the wardrobe, the window facing the lake — except that it is twelve degrees colder than the front room with nothing in between to explain it.

Kitchen, fifty-eight and dropping. She holds it there a full minute and watches it drop.

She crouches and sets the thermometer on the floor at the base of the back door, in the seam where the cold comes in, and the line slides down to forty-seven, and then, while she is watching it, to forty-four.

Mikko has his own. He's taken it out of his pocket and he's walking the same circuit a step behind her, not talking, and when they meet back in the kitchen they hold the two thermometers side by side under the light and the readings are within a degree of each other. He'd half-thought, she can see, that hers was wrong. That it was a bad thermometer. That there would turn out to be a reasonable seam of warmth somewhere that the numbers had missed.

"Same," he says.

"Same," she says.

---

**Nine-fifteen.**

The knocking begins.

Not from the floor this time. From every direction. Three knocks in the floor, under the kitchen, hard and even and spaced. Then a pause. Then three from the east wall, the lake wall, at about the height of her shoulder. Then a pause. And then — for the first time — three from above. From the ceiling. Directly over the table, where there is nothing, where there is only the attic and the roof and the cold sky.

She has not told anyone about the ceiling. Not Louise, not Mikko, not the night two nights ago when she first heard it and stood in the dark telling herself it was the house settling, knowing it was not. She does not tell anyone now.

Floor. Wall. Ceiling. Floor. Wall. Ceiling. Rotating, even, patient, so that the house itself becomes a drum being played from inside, struck in sequence by something that knows exactly where the next blow goes. She turns on another light. She fills the kettle and sets it on the burner. She stands in the kitchen with her hands flat on the counter and breathes, and counts without meaning to, three and three and three, because the count is already in her now and there is no turning it off.

---

**Ten o'clock.**

She calls Louise.

"Tonight is bad," she says. "It's in the walls. The ceiling."

Louise is quiet for a long moment. Aino can hear something on the other end — a radio, low, or wind.

"She knows we're coming," Louise says finally. "This is the last time she can —" She stops. Starts again. "She has been waiting a hundred years. What she's doing now is what she has always been doing. It's just that there's no one sitting in the sauna anymore keeping the weight of it down."

"I know," Aino says.

"You need to stay in the house. Don't go outside. Don't go near the water. Whatever you hear — whatever you think you hear from outside — you stay inside. Both of you."

"Okay."

"First light," Louise says. "We'll be there."

The line clicks. Aino stands holding the phone after the dial tone has come back, until the dial tone gives way to that flat warning tone they use to tell you the receiver is off the hook, and then she hangs it up. The knocking has not paused for the call. Floor, wall, ceiling. It does not care that she was on the phone. It was never talking to her.

---

**Midnight.**

The kitchen reads forty-six. They are wearing their coats inside the house. She has on the brown wool one of her father's that still smells faintly of him, of pipe tobacco he gave up decades ago and a closet in a house by a lake, and Mikko has on his own.

The knocking has been going for nearly three hours without a break. She has stopped counting it on purpose — has made herself stop, the way you make yourself stop probing a sore tooth — but the count keeps starting again on its own at the edge of her attention, three and three and three, like a faucet she cannot get to seat.

She does the thermometer walk again, because it is something to do. The back bedroom is forty-one now. She stands in the doorway and her breath shows, a little ghost of it in the light from the hall. There is a rim of ice forming on the inside of the window glass. Not frost on the outside, where frost belongs. Ice on the inside, a thin bright lip of it growing up from the bottom of the pane, the way ice grows at the edge of open water.

She can hear the furnace running. It has not stopped running all night. It runs and runs and makes no difference at all, the way a man can shout into a wind.

She closes the door of the back bedroom. It does not help. She had not really thought it would.

---

**One o'clock.**

Something hits the back door.

Not a knock. A blow. A single flat impact that shakes the door in its frame and rattles the window above it and sends her two steps back before she knows she has moved. Mikko is on his feet. They stand in the kitchen and look at the door, at the four panes of it gone black with night, at the deadbolt she threw at sundown.

Silence. A real silence, the first in four hours.

Then the knocking resumes — the measured three-and-pause — as though the blow had been a thing apart, an interruption from somewhere else, and the counting has simply picked back up where it left off.

"She's angry," Aino says. Her voice is steadier than she expects it to be. "She has every right to be angry."

"I know," Mikko says.

"Don't open the door."

"I know."

She stands in her kitchen at one in the morning, in her father's coat, in forty-six-degree air, with the knocking moving around her in the walls, and she thinks about Eino writing *kaunis* in a notebook and underlining the word twice. She thinks about Toivo's last ledger entry, the careful copperplate gone shaky at the end — *perhaps he will not have to carry this.* She thinks about her father, the year before he died, calling her and not being able to do it. They are still there, those calls, scattered down the log on her own phone — his number a dozen times across those last months, most of them seconds long, a few that reached her voicemail and held nothing in them but the sound of his kitchen and a breath and the small click of him setting the receiver back down. He would call her up to the edge of speaking and then not speak.

---

**One-fifteen.**

"I should have come sooner," she says.

Mikko looks at her.

"Not just after he died. Years ago. I should have —" She stops. The armor she has worn all night, all week, the flat dry voice she keeps the count out with, slips, and underneath it is the thing she has not let herself look at, and she lets herself look at it now because there is no longer any reason not to. "He was waiting for me. He'd been waiting for years, Mikko, he was sitting in this house calling me and hanging up because he couldn't think of how to start, and I was in Milwaukee not answering the questions I wouldn't let myself ask. I knew the area code. I knew it was him. I let it ring."

"You didn't know," Mikko says. "About — any of this. You couldn't have known."

"I knew something was wrong. I have always known something was wrong with this lake and this house and this family. I knew it the way you know a smell in a house you grew up in, the way I knew not to swim past the point and never once asked anyone why. I just decided I was allowed to not know it officially." She looks at the back door. "Nineteen years."

She does the math. She has done it before; she does it again now, here, in the cold, because the count is already running and the count will take any numbers it is given.

She times every phone call. She has always timed them — looks at the clock when she picks up, looks again when she sets the receiver down — the way he did, the way her father did, a thing she got from him without ever being taught it. So she knows. Four calls a year, maybe. Holidays, his birthday, the one in spring when the ice went out and he'd call to tell her the date so she could write it down somewhere she would never look at again. Ten minutes a call, the long ones. Forty minutes a year. She left at nineteen and she has been gone nineteen years, and nineteen times forty is seven hundred and sixty minutes, and seven hundred and sixty minutes is twelve hours and forty minutes.

Twelve hours. In nineteen years she had given her father twelve hours of her voice, and he had given her twelve hours of his, and that is all there is now, that is the whole sum of it, twelve hours laid end to end would not get a person from here to Milwaukee, and somewhere in those twelve hours, in the long pauses between sentences that she had always been impatient with, he had been trying to tell her this and she had been waiting for him to get to the point so she could go.

Mikko does not say anything. There is nothing useful to say and he knows it, and she is grateful for that, grateful for the specific quality of his silence, which is not absence but presence, a man standing in a cold kitchen at one in the morning being there.

---

**Two o'clock.**

The blow comes against the door again.

Harder. The whole frame jumps; a fine dust of old paint sifts down from the top of the casing. The window above the door rattles in its glazing and she thinks, distantly, that it is going to break, that they are going to be in here with the cold and whatever the cold is coming in through a broken window, and then it does not break.

She makes herself look at Mikko's face, because she needs to know what is on it, and what is on it is the thing she has been afraid of all night. Not fear, exactly. Worse than fear. The look of a practical man — a man who plows roads and fixes furnaces and knows the weight of snow and the load a roof will take — running, fast and silent, through every reasonable explanation he has, and reaching the end of them, and finding nothing there. His jaw is set. There is a muscle going in his cheek. He is forty-something years old and he has lived on this lake his whole life and he is learning, tonight, in real time, that the thing he has spent that whole life half-knowing and never saying is real, is here, is on the other side of an inch and a half of door, and his face is the face of a man watching the floor of his own life give way.

He catches her looking. He doesn't pretend.

"Yeah," he says.

"Yeah," she says.

---

**Two-fifteen.**

The blow again. Then the measured knocking resumes, patient, counting, three and three and three, around and around the house. She goes and stands at the back window beside the door, not too close, and looks out.

The yard is blue-black under no moon. The snow holds what light there is. And out past the dooryard, at the edge of where she can see, between the house and the slope down to the sauna, the snow is not still. She cannot say it is anything. That is the thing she will keep coming back to, after, that she cannot say it is anything — only that there is a place in the snow at the limit of the light where the dark seems to gather and shift and stand, a height to it that is roughly the height of a person, a stillness in it that is the wrong kind of stillness, the stillness of something looking back. She does not lean in to see it better. Louise told her not to go near the water and she understands, suddenly, that this is the same instruction. She steps back from the glass.

"Don't," she says, when Mikko starts toward the window.

He stops. He looks at her instead of out the window, which is right, which is the thing to do.

---

**Three o'clock.**

The kitchen reads forty-two. She can no longer feel her feet. When she looks down at them she is faintly surprised to find them still there in her boots, doing their job.

They make the decision the way two cold people make a decision, which is mostly without words. She looks at the front room, dark and twenty degrees colder, the cold pouring out of it now like a draft from an open cellar. She looks at the kitchen, the one room with the stove in it, the warmest room in the house at forty-two degrees. She looks at Mikko. He is already standing.

They drag the couch.

It is heavy and the doorway is narrow and they have to tip it on its end and walk it through sideways, the two of them grunting in the cold, their breath hanging, and it is an absurd thing to be doing at three in the morning while a hundred-year-old grief beats on the walls, and they do it anyway because the alternative is to sit and freeze and listen, and at least this is moving. They get it into the kitchen and shove it sideways against the table, under the window, as close to the stove as it will go without catching.

She turns on every burner. Four blue rings of gas, low, hissing. The oven too, door cracked. The lights all blazing, every light in the house she could reach. They sit on the couch with the quilt — the one from the back bedroom, the wedding-ring quilt, gone stiff with cold — pulled up over both of them, shoulder to shoulder, and the heat off the burners is a real thing, a small dome of it, and it is still forty-two degrees in the room and the knocking is in the floor under the couch and in the wall behind their heads and in the ceiling over them, and the four burners hiss and the furnace runs somewhere below and none of it, none of it, makes the line on the thermometer move.

She does not think about the temperature. She thinks about Louise saying *we'll be there.* She thinks about what those two words mean after a hundred years of silence — of watching without acknowledgment, of keeping a woman's body and her name and her right to be properly grieved pinned flat under the weight of a family's guilt and a town's agreement not to discuss it.

She thinks: *tomorrow.*

She thinks: *hold on.*

---

**Four o'clock.**

The blows against the door stop.

The knocking in the walls goes on, but something in it has changed. The rhythm is the same — three and three and three, around the house — but the quality of it is different now, lower, or slower, or further off, something she cannot name. She thinks of a voice that has been shouting for hours and is starting to go. She thinks that is not the right way to think about it. She thinks there is no right way to think about it. She thinks that maybe it is exactly right.

Mikko has gone quiet beside her, his head tipped back against the cushion, his eyes shut, not asleep — she can tell from his breathing he is not asleep — but resting, going somewhere inside himself to wait out the last of it. She lets him. She keeps watch on the window and the door for both of them.

---

**Five o'clock.**

She gets up, the quilt sliding off her shoulder, and the cold takes the warm place where it was instantly. She makes coffee. The simple acts of it — the grounds, the water, the percolator on the one free burner — are steadying in a way she is grateful for. She stands at the stove with her hands around the first cup and looks out the kitchen window, where, over the black line of the trees across the lake, the sky has begun, very faintly, to change. Not light yet. The idea of light. The gray that comes before the gray.

The knocking goes on. Three and three and three. It has gone on for eight hours. She finds that she is counting it again, openly now, all pretense of not counting gone, counting it down toward the thing Louise promised — *first light* — counting the way you count the last contractions, the way you count a fever breaking, counting because the count is the only clock she has left and it is finally, after eight hours, running toward something.

---

**Five-thirty.**

The knocking stops.

Between the second and third knock.

There is the first. There is the second. And there is not the third. The count opens its mouth for the third blow and the third blow does not come, and the house goes into a silence so total and so sudden that she hears her own pulse in her ears, hears the gas hissing in the burners, hears Mikko's breathing change behind her as he comes up out of his rest into the silence and registers it.

Just like every morning. Between the second and the third. She stands in the kitchen in the sudden enormous silence, the coffee cup hot against both her palms, and breathes.

Mikko says: "It's Wednesday."

"It's Wednesday," she says.
