What is left at the water's edge is coals and stone.

The fire is essentially done. It has been done for a while now — the structure came down through the late morning, the roof and then the walls and then the frame, and what remains is the fieldstone foundation Eino set in 1919 and a bed of embers inside it, banked and glowing and throwing heat she can feel from the kitchen window. The smoke is thin now, a slow gray thread off the coals, rising straight in the still air and then bending east where it finds the wind above the trees. By mid-afternoon the temperature has climbed to thirty-four degrees, the warmest it's been since she arrived. Melt runs from the eaves in a steady drip. The snow in the yard has gone heavy and wet, and the path the men beat down to the shore has turned to slush.

She looks at the smoke once, the way you'd look back at something you'd carried a long way and finally set down, and then she stops looking at it.

The others are still down at the shore. They came back through the morning to watch it burn and they have not all left yet. Louise's uncle says something quietly to the younger man and the younger man nods. Marta Lehtinen cries, briefly, and Juhani puts his arm around her and she lets him.

Erik Saari walks down to the foundation and stands at the edge of it, as close to the coals as the heat will allow, his hands at his sides. He stands there a long time. His brother went through the ice yesterday and they have not found him, and Aino thinks of that and then makes herself stop, because it is not hers to look at, and because she is not sure looking at it is even what he's doing. She doesn't know what he's doing. She thinks maybe he doesn't either. He just stands at the lip of the warm stone with the smoke going up past his face, and the lake behind him holding everything it holds, and after a while he turns and comes back up the hill.

Louise comes to find her at the kitchen window.

"We're going to go," she says. "We'll come back. There are other things we need to do here, at the shore. Not today."

"Whenever you want," Aino says. "I'll leave a key with Mikko for when I'm not here."

Louise looks at her. "You're going back to Milwaukee."

"Eventually. I don't know exactly when."

Louise is quiet for a moment. "What happened today — it's a beginning. Not an ending." She pauses. "I want you to understand that. For your own sake. You did a good thing. It's not finished, because these things don't finish cleanly, but you moved it in the right direction." Another pause. "Your father did too. In his way."

Aino thinks about the ledger entries. March, April, May, September. He called and hung up. He wrote names he couldn't deliver. He sat in the sauna for thirty years.

"He did what he could," she says.

"Yes," Louise says. "That's usually how it goes."

She and her family go. The Lehtinens go. Erik Saari shakes Aino's hand at the door — a real handshake, deliberate, meeting her eyes — and goes. The cars pull out one at a time onto the gravel, slow over the ruts, and then the yard is quiet and the only sound is the drip from the eaves and the faint tick of cooling stone down at the shore.

Mikko stays.

---

They sit at the kitchen table with the last of the coffee and the remains of the afternoon. The smoke is barely anything now, a haze over the foundation that the wind keeps thinning.

"How do you feel?" Mikko asks.

She thinks about it honestly. "Empty," she says. "Not bad empty. Just — like something that was full isn't anymore."

He nods. He doesn't try to fill it.

"She's still there," Aino says. "In the lake. I don't know what today meant for her. I don't know if it helped." She wraps her hands around her cup. "Louise said it's a beginning."

"Probably the most honest thing anyone can say about it."

She looks out the window at the haze. "A man is dead," she says. "Dale Numminen. He has a family somewhere."

"I know."

"I keep thinking about him. That he had nothing to do with any of this and it happened to him anyway." She pauses. "That's the thing about old wrongs, I think. They don't stay contained to the people who deserve them."

Mikko is quiet for a moment. "No," he says. "They don't."

They sit with that. Outside the drip is steady. After a while he reaches across the small distance between them and puts his hand over hers where it rests on the table, just for a moment — not a holding, not anything that asks to be answered, only the flat warmth of a palm laid over the back of her hand and then lifted again. He goes back to his coffee. Neither of them says anything about it. She finds that she is glad of it in a way she does not examine, the brief weight and then the lifting, a man telling her he is there in the only register he has.

He leaves before dark. He has the dog and the long drive back up the county road and he says he'll come by tomorrow, and she says all right, and she watches his truck take the bend and then she is alone in the house with the light going out of the windows and the foundation cooling down at the water's edge.

---

She makes herself eat something standing up at the counter. Then she takes her phone off the charger and she sits at the kitchen table in the dark and she calls her mother.

She has not done this in years — initiated it, dialed the number herself, sat and waited for Lila to pick up rather than letting Lila be the one who calls and the one who is owed the calling. It is not lost on her, the shape of it. She let her father's calls ring out for years and now she has spent a week learning what an unanswered call costs and tonight she is the one who picks up the phone. She does the thing she failed to do for him, for the parent who is still here to do it for.

Lila answers on the fourth ring. In Phoenix it is two hours earlier and the evening is warm and ordinary; Aino can hear a television somewhere behind her, the dry hum of a house that has never once made a sound that meant something.

"Aino," her mother says, and there is surprise in it, and under the surprise something else, quick and braced. "Is everything all right?"

"I'm fine. I'm still up at the house." She turns her cup a quarter turn on the table. "I'm selling it. I wanted you to know."

A pause. "All right," Lila says carefully. "That's — I think that's the right thing. There's no reason to keep it."

"No."

She doesn't tell her about the sauna or the foundation or the men at the shore or the name on the ledger. She tells her a sayable version: that there were things to deal with, things to do with people up here, that they're settled now, mostly, that the house is going to a couple from Marquette. She tells her the lake will be someone else's lake by summer.

Lila is quiet for a long time. Long enough that Aino looks at the screen to be sure the call is still connected.

"It was always quiet up there," her mother says finally, and it is the same thing she said weeks ago, the thing that made Aino want to ask a question she couldn't form. But this time Lila does not move on. This time the quiet on the line keeps going, and Aino does not fill it, and into it her mother says, very plainly, with no weight on any one word:

"I shouldn't have brought you back."

Aino does not say anything.

She had lived her whole life inside a thing she could not name. The summers — the drive north, the trees closing in, the silence that wasn't only silence, the cold off the water she could feel from the driveway before the lake even came into view. The certainty, never spoken to anyone, that something was wrong here, with the lake or the house or the family or all three at once, a wrongness she'd assumed was hers, a flaw in her own way of perceiving, because no one ever once said otherwise. And here, in the dark of her father's kitchen, is the one person who knew, who lived at this lake and felt it and left, and who put her child in the car summer after summer and drove her right back into it anyway. Her mother. Saying it. *I shouldn't have brought you back.*

It does not go through her like grief. It goes through her like a latch finding its catch. Something that had been holding itself at an angle her whole life turns, very slightly, and seats. She feels it in her chest and then lower, in the long muscles of her back, a loosening she had not known was clenched. She is aware that her eyes have filled but she is not crying, not the way she cried at the fire; this is quieter than that and it is for herself, which the crying at the fire was not.

The thing she has waited her whole life to hear is a single sentence, and it is over in three seconds, and no one is going to say anything to follow it.

"You knew," Aino says. Not an accusation. Almost a question. Almost a thank-you.

"I knew enough," Lila says. "I told myself it was just the place. That you'd want your father." A breath. "I don't know what I knew. I knew I wanted to leave and I did and I kept bringing you back." Another pause, and then, in the old register, the surface coming back over the water: "How are the roads up there? They salting?"

"They're fine," Aino says. "Melt today. It's warming up."

"Good. You drive careful when you come down."

"I will."

They talk a few more minutes about nothing, about the drive south and the timing of it and whether she'll come through Chicago, and there is no reconciliation in it, no scene, nothing that would look like anything from the outside. But the sentence has been said. It is in the room with her now and it will be in the car with her when she drives, and it cannot be unsaid, and Aino understands that this is all she is going to get and that it is, against everything, enough. You do not always get the whole of a thing. Sometimes you get the one sentence.

"Goodnight, Mom," she says.

"Goodnight," Lila says. "I'm glad you called."

She is, Aino thinks. That much is true.

---

She sits at the table for a while after the line goes dead, in the dark, the phone face-down in front of her.

Outside the drip from the eaves has slowed as the temperature drops back toward freezing. The foundation at the water's edge is no longer glowing; it is just stone now, fieldstone Eino mortared a hundred years ago, going cold under the heavy snow. In a few weeks the ice will go out and the lake will open and the foundation will stand there in the sunlight, empty, just stones, just a rectangle of old rock the weather will slowly work on. That is fine. That is what stones do.

She thinks of the list she made days ago, the one still in her coat pocket. *What to do after.* She had not answered that item. She is not sure she can answer it tonight either, but it feels less like a wall than it did.

She thinks of Dale Numminen's family, who will have something to bury when the ice goes out. She thinks of her father, forty years in this house, the soup bowls in their place and the percolator on the counter. She thinks of the silence after Louise said *we see you* down at the shore, the quality of the air in that long quiet moment, and she thinks: yes. Something changed. She can't prove it and she won't try to.

She hopes, without knowing if hope is the right word, that whatever is in the lake is freer than it was this morning.

The rest of it — the summers, the cold, the thing she carried her whole life without a name for it, and the name her mother finally gave it tonight in three plain seconds across two thousand miles — the rest of it she will carry. Some things you just carry. She knows that now in a way she didn't a few weeks ago.

She gets up and puts the kettle on for tea and the evening settles down around her and the house is warm and the smoke is gone and the lake outside is still.
