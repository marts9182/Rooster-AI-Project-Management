It has been five weeks since her father died in his kitchen making coffee, and in those five weeks nobody has kept the watch.

She has been in the sauna twice — once to understand it, once to speak a name into the dark — but she has not done what her father did. Has not come down the hill every week, lit the stove, opened the hatch, sat in the heat and let whatever is below know that someone was still paying attention. The watch her great-grandfather started in 1919 and her grandfather maintained and her father kept for thirty years ended the morning Hank Hietala didn't show up at Hakala's and Paavo Maki let himself in through the unlocked back door.

She has felt the difference. The footprints in the snow under the kitchen window every morning, coming up from the lake, going nowhere, ending at the foundation. The knocking. The cold in the floor that no furnace explains.

The footprints are the worst of it, she thinks, until she decides the knocking is the worst of it, and then she stops ranking them.

Whatever patience Mindemoyen had, a hundred years of it, is running out.

The forecast comes in on a Saturday — unseasonable warmth pushing up from the south, temperatures climbing into the high thirties by the middle of the week, possibly touching forty by Thursday. The meteorologist on the Iron Mountain station stands in front of a map smeared orange where it ought to be white and calls it the warmest March stretch on record for the region, the earliest thaw they've seen in forty years. He says it the way a man says a thing he expects people to be glad about. He is wearing a short-sleeved shirt. Behind him, on the wall of the studio, somebody has hung a paper shamrock.

She watches the forecast on her father's television, in her father's chair, with her father's afghan over her knees, and feels something cold move through her that has nothing to do with the temperature outside.

She goes to the basement. The shelf where her father kept the ledgers is at the bottom of the stairs, where it has always been, the spines dated in his hand in grease pencil. She pulls the last five years and carries them up to the kitchen table and reads the March entries across those years by the overhead light, looking for what she already knows she will find.

It's there. Every year, in the entries from the third week of March onward, a note on ice thickness. He augered the same three holes — inlet, midwater, the deep end off the sauna — and wrote the numbers down in inches without comment, the way you record a thing you intend to outlive. The numbers decline across the years. The ice that ran twenty-four, twenty-six inches through early March five years ago is running eighteen, then sixteen, then fourteen. Last March he'd recorded twelve inches at the deep end and noted in the margin, the only year he wrote anything in the margin: *thinner than I have ever seen it this time of year.*

This March, if the forecast holds, there may not be safe ice at all by the end of the week.

She closes the last ledger and squares the stack and keeps her hand flat on the top cover for a while. Then she goes to the phone.

She calls Mikko first.

"Have you seen the forecast?" she asks when he picks up.

A pause. "Yeah," he says. "I was going to call you."

"The ice is going to go early. Maybe by the weekend."

"I know." She can hear him thinking, the particular quality of his quiet, the breath he takes before he says a hard thing plainly. "You need to call Louise."

She calls Louise. She tells her about the forecast, about the ledger entries, about the pattern she's read across five years of her father's careful records — the numbers dropping, the margin note, the bare orange map on the television. Louise listens without interrupting. When the silence on the line stretches Aino realizes she has stopped talking and that she is gripping the receiver hard enough to feel it in her wrist.

"Wednesday," Louise says at last. "We'll come Wednesday. That's as early as I can arrange it. There are people who have to be asked, and a thing like this is not asked quickly."

"Wednesday," Aino says.

"Wednesday. Hold the days until then."

Four days.

She thinks four days is manageable. She has held harder things than four days. She has held a man's affairs and a man's house and a man's hundred-year list, and four days is a number, and numbers she can do.

She is wrong about this, though not in the way she expects. The days are not the problem. The nights are.

---

It starts at midnight.

She knows it is midnight because she is awake to hear the beginning of it, which she has not been on the other nights — the other nights it was already going when she surfaced into it, already established, as though it had been there a while before she noticed and her noticing was beside the point. This time she is lying in her childhood bed in the dark with her eyes open, listening to the furnace cycle and the drip starting at the south eave, and the house settling, and she hears the clock in the front room begin its twelve and somewhere under the twelfth stroke the first knock comes up through the floor.

Three knocks. Then nothing.

She lies still. She tells herself it is the furnace, the expansion of a duct, the well pump kicking on against a cold line. She knows the sounds this house makes. She has known them her whole life, has slept through every one of them in this bed, and none of them is this.

Three knocks again.

She counts the gap between the sets in her head, the way you'd count anything you wanted to keep at arm's length, and she gets to a number, and she does not trust the number because she counted it lying down in the dark with her heart going. So she gets up.

She does not turn on the bedside lamp. She crosses to the bedroom door in her socks and her father's old flannel shirt that she has been sleeping in, and the floor is cold, colder near the door, and she stands in the doorway and listens to the next set with her bare logic intact and her feet on the boards.

Three knocks. The boards carry it into the soles of her feet. It is below her. It is below the house, in the floor of the lake, and the floor of the lake is a quarter mile downhill from the bedroom, and there is no way for a sound made in the floor of the lake to arrive in the soles of her feet, and it arrives in the soles of her feet.

She turns on the light.

The bulb is unkind and ordinary and the room is just the room — the dresser, the mirror gone grey at one corner, the water stain on the ceiling shaped like the lower peninsula that she used to find the upper in when she was small. Three knocks. She goes to the kitchen and turns that light on too, and the porch light, and the front room lamp, until the house is lit up like something is wrong, which it is.

She puts the kettle on. Not because she wants tea. Because the gesture is normal and her hands know how to do it and while she is doing it she is a person standing at a stove at midnight and not a person listening to the floor.

Three knocks. The pause. She watches the second hand on the kitchen clock and counts the pause against it.

Seven seconds.

She watches the next pause. Seven seconds. The next. Seven seconds. Not about seven. Not seven, give or take. Seven, on the sweep of the second hand, every time, the way a thing keeps time when it has all the time there is and means to spend it precisely. The precision is worse than randomness. Randomness she could lay at the door of the house, the ice, the cold, her own frayed nerves filling a quiet with pattern. This is a count. Something is counting. Something below the floor of the lake is keeping a beat as exact as the clock on her wall and it knows she is awake and listening, and it is in no hurry, and it is not going to stop.

She thinks: I will not listen to it. I will read.

She gets the ledgers back out and opens the most recent one to the spring she missed and reads her father's hand and after a while she realizes she has read the same line four times and that each time her eye reached the end of the line it went to the clock and counted the next seven and that she has not taken in a single word. She closes the ledger.

She thinks: I will call Mikko.

The phone is on the wall by the back door. She crosses the kitchen to it and lifts the receiver and gets the dial tone, that flat steady note, and she puts her finger in the dial for the first number of his — and the knocking comes, three, up through the floor at the base of the door not three feet from where she is standing, and she takes her finger out of the dial.

It is past midnight. It is past midnight on a Saturday and Mikko has the lake watch in the morning and a body that needs sleep, and there is nothing he can do about the floor that she cannot do, which is nothing. She is not going to be a woman who calls a man at midnight because the dark is loud. She hangs up the phone. She stands with her hand on the receiver for a moment longer than that decision required, and then she takes it away.

Three knocks. Seven seconds. Three knocks.

She makes a circuit of the house instead. Front room to hall to bedroom to kitchen and back, slow, the way you'd walk a fence line, checking what there is to check — the locks, all of them, the back door bolt thrown and the knob testing solid, the front door, the basement door at the top of its stairs. The windows latched. There is nothing to find. She finds it all anyway, each lock confirmed, and the confirming does nothing for the knocking and she knows it will do nothing and she does it because the alternative is to sit still, and she cannot sit still.

She gets thirsty. She doesn't notice getting thirsty; she notices that she has filled a glass at the tap and drunk it and filled it again, and that her mouth is dry the way it gets when you have been breathing through it, fast, for a long time. She makes herself slow down on the water. She makes herself slow down on the walking. She gets her coat off the hook by the door — not to go out, there is no going out, but because the house is cold now in a way the furnace is not catching and the coat is the warmth she has — and she puts it over her shoulders without her arms in the sleeves and sits down at the kitchen table where she can see two doors and the dark window over the sink and she stays there.

The clock says one. The knocking does not change. Three and seven and three. She does not count the sets. She had counted them on other nights, fifty and then a hundred, had written the running total on the back of an envelope as if the number were a thing she could present to someone, evidence, a receipt. Tonight she does not write anything down. The number is not the point and she knows it is not the point. The point is the seven seconds. The point is that something is patient enough to wait exactly seven seconds, a hundred times, a thousand times, and was patient enough to wait a hundred years, and her father sat in the heat once a week for thirty years so that the seven seconds would stay seven seconds and never become six.

The clock says two.

She gets up and turns the radio on, low, the AM station out of Marquette that plays old country all night for the truckers, and a man with a voice like gravel sings about a town he is never going back to, and for three or four songs the knocking is one sound among others and she can almost. She can almost. Then the song ends and there is the dead air the station leaves between cuts and into the dead air comes three knocks and seven seconds and three knocks and she reaches over and turns the radio off because it is worse, somehow, to give it the chance.

The clock says three. She is standing at the sink with both hands flat on the cold steel of it, looking at her own face in the black window, and she thinks of her father standing where she is standing, how many times, three in the morning with the watch broken open in his chest, and no one in the house to know. She had thought the watching was vigilance — a man going down a hill to a stove, a chore handed father to son like the woodpile and the well. She understands now, with her hands on the steel and the count coming up through the floor, that it was endurance, and that endurance is a separate thing from courage, slower and lonelier, and that she has no measure for what thirty years of it cost a man who never once said a word about it at a dinner table.

The clock says four. The drip has stopped at the eave; the temperature has fallen back overnight the way it does, the thaw a daytime promise only, and the cold has come up to meet it. Three knocks. Seven seconds. She has stopped flinching at them. This frightens her more than the flinching did — that the body, given enough of a thing, will make room for it, will file it under the ordinary, will let her sit at a table at four in the morning with a count coming up through the floor and think about whether there is enough coffee. There is enough coffee. She does not make it yet. Making it would be conceding the night, and she is not conceding the night; she is outlasting it.

The clock says five. The window over the sink is still black but it is a different black, the flat dead black of deep night giving over to the grained black that comes before grey. Three knocks. Seven seconds. Three knocks.

She stands at the window and watches the black go grey at the bottom, over the trees, over the lake she cannot see but knows is there, and she counts — not the sets, she has not counted the sets in hours — she counts the lightening, the slow filling of the world back in. The dresser comes back. The fence comes back. The shape of the woodshed comes back. Three knocks. Seven seconds. The grey reaches the top of the trees and the trees separate from the sky and become individual trees, and the snow in the yard goes from a smear to a surface, and somewhere a crow says the one thing crows say, and —

three knocks —

seven seconds —

two knocks.

And nothing.

She waits. She stands at the window and waits for the third knock with the whole front of her body and it does not come. The seven seconds passes, and the next seven, and the next, and the third knock of that set does not come and no new set begins. It has stopped between the second and the third knock, in the middle of its own count, the way a man stops talking when someone calls his name from the next room. Not finished. Interrupted. Cut off mid-stride and gone, leaving the third knock owed and unpaid and hanging in the floor where she can feel the shape of it, the absence of it, the seven seconds it never got to.

Outside the window it is morning. The crow says its thing again.

---

She makes the coffee.

She does it the way she does everything in this kitchen now, by the route her father wore into it — the percolator from the second shelf, the grounds in the basket, the water to the line, the basket seated and the lid down and the knob to the click. The blue flame. The first knock and hiss of the water taking the heat. She stands at the stove and watches it climb to its rhythm and her hands are steady. She holds one out flat over the burner and looks at it and it does not shake.

She is surprised by this. She examines the surprise the way she examines the steadiness, turning it over, and decides it is not nothing — that a hand that does not shake at six in the morning after that night is a hand that has decided something the rest of her has not caught up to yet.

The percolator finds its tempo and holds it. Outside the light comes up grey and then, briefly, gold along the tops of the trees, the sun clearing the ridge across the lake, the snow on the yard going pink and then white and then, in the patches the day will get to first, beginning very faintly to shine. The thaw, coming back. The orange map. Four days down to three sometime in the next hour, when the day turns over into Sunday all the way and the count begins again.

She thinks about the third knock that never came. She does not like what it tells her — that the thing in the floor can stop when it chooses and choose not to finish, that the stopping was a stopping and not an ending, that it will be there again at midnight with its seven seconds intact and the owed knock waiting. Three more nights of this. She does not let her mind run forward past the three. She has learned that much from her father's ledger, from a man who augered three holes and wrote three numbers and did not write a word about how he felt doing it: you keep the watch one night at a time, and you do not let the size of the whole thing into the room with you, because the whole thing will not fit, and trying to make it fit is how a man breaks.

She pours the coffee. She wraps both hands around the cup and feels the heat come into her palms, real heat, kitchen heat, the ordinary warmth of a thing she made.

She is the last Hietala. Her great-grandfather started the watch and her grandfather kept it and her father kept it for thirty years and died with it broken in his chest, and there is no one left to hand it to, and Louise comes Wednesday, and between now and Wednesday there is a count coming up through the floor of the lake every night, patient and exact and a hundred years angry, and it is going to do everything it can to find the place in her where she gives.

It is not going to find it. Not tonight, with the sun on the snow and the coffee in her hands. Whatever breaks before Wednesday, it is not going to be her.

She drinks the coffee while it is hot. The day, when it gets going, is going to be warm.
