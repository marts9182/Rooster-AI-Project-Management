Mikko is at the door before eight, which is not when he comes. He comes at nine, with coffee, after his crew is set for the day; he is a man who puts the work in motion first and then attends to the rest. So when she hears the truck on the gravel before eight and looks out and sees him already out of it, not bringing anything in his hands, she knows it is something and goes to the door without her coat.

The morning is warm. That is the first wrong thing, though it is not new: the eave is running, the gravel is dark and wet where the snow has pulled back from it overnight, and the air coming off the lake has the soft rotten smell of March, of water working under ice. She has stopped being able to enjoy a warm morning. The warmth is a clock.

"Dale Numminen," Mikko says. He says the name the way you say a name you have just learned and already can't put down. "From over in Tioga. Went out on his sled last night and didn't come home. His wife called it in around five this morning." He looks at her. "They found the machine at the inlet."

She does not ask the next thing. She knows it the way she has come to know things this past week, below the level where questions live.

"Engine running," Mikko says anyway. "No sign of him."

She stands in the doorway with the warm wet air on her face and her hands going cold in it.

"I want to go down there," she says.

He doesn't tell her she shouldn't. That is one of the things about Mikko she has stopped being surprised by. He just looks at her for a second, deciding nothing she can see, and says, "Get your coat."

---

The inlet is on the north end, where the creek comes in, and even in deep winter it is the soft spot — her father augered it every year and wrote the smallest number there, always, because moving water never lets the ice get honest. She knows this the way she knows the lake's other facts now, from the ledgers, from a man's grease-pencil hand. A local would know it without the ledgers. A local would not cross there.

There are vehicles along the shoulder when they come up the inlet road. The county sheriff's truck, a township fire truck, two pickups she doesn't know and one she does, and an ambulance backed up to the turnaround with its rear doors open and nobody in any hurry around it, which tells her more than the open doors would. People who hurry around an ambulance are people who think there is a reason to. These men are moving at the speed of a thing already decided.

Mikko parks behind the fire truck and they walk down. Nobody stops them. A few of the men nod at Mikko, who knows everyone, who has poured foundations and pulled stumps for half the township and has the standing of a man you don't question at a shoreline. One of them, a big kid in fire department coveralls, says, "Mikko," and Mikko says, "Cal," and that is the whole of the permission they need.

The recovery is out maybe sixty feet. They have laid a ladder flat on the ice and run a line out past it, and two men in dry suits are working at the edge of a hole, and the hole is the thing she cannot look away from.

It is black. That is what gets into her. The ice around it is the gray-white of rotten March ice, soft-looking, sun-cupped, and the water in the hole is not gray and is not white; it is black, flat black, the black of the lake's own depth come up to the surface where it has no business being, and it does not move. The creek runs into the lake somewhere under there and you would think the water would show it, would carry some current at the surface, and it lies in the hole as still as something poured.

The snowmobile is already up. They have it on the ice off to the side, on its track, water sheeting off it and going to slush where it pools. She had thought — she doesn't know what she had thought. That it would be wrecked, somehow. It isn't. It is just a snowmobile, a red one, a few years old, sitting on the ice tilted slightly with meltwater running out of every seam of it, and a man at the back of it is bent down doing something with the cowling, and as she watches, the engine, which had been running when they found it, idling out here for hours over black water with no one on it, ticks and cools and is silent. Somebody had finally reached in and killed it. She had not known it was still running until it stopped.

She stands on the rotten ice in the warm air and looks at the red machine that ran all night with no one on it, and the cold goes up through the soles of her boots.

---

There is a glove on the ice.

It is by the hole, at the lip, where one of the dry-suit men has set the things they bring up out of the water before they go back down — a procedure, an order to it, items laid in a row on a tarp the way you'd lay out anything you meant to account for. A glove. Just the one. A good winter glove, leather and insulation, the kind a careful man buys, and it is lying palm-down on the tarp with the water leaching out of it dark, and the fingers of it are slightly curled the way a glove curls when it has held a grip and been pulled off the hand still curled, and she looks at it and her stomach does a slow thing and she makes herself look at the water instead.

The dry-suit men go back down. One and then the other, into the black, and the line tender pays out the rope hand over hand, and the surface closes over them and lies flat again. Nobody on the shore says anything. The sheriff is up by the trucks talking to a man who must be a deputy, writing on a clipboard, and his voice carries down across the ice in pieces. *Spring ice.* *Inlet's always bad.* *Out-of-towner, wife says he didn't know the lake.* *Cut across to save the long way around the bay.* The deputy says something and the sheriff says, flat, not unkind, the verdict of a man who has stood at a lot of shorelines, "Accident. Plain accident. Happens every year somewhere."

It does happen every year somewhere. She knows that. Thin spring ice and a man in a hurry who didn't know which water to trust. It is the most ordinary death there is up here, ordinary as the warm wet wind, and the sheriff is right, and he is not right, and there is no version of the truth she could hand him that he could do anything with.

She looks at the black water where the two men went down to bring up a man who crossed the wrong water, and she does the thing she came down here to do, which she had not known was the thing until she is doing it.

She stops telling herself it is aimed.

For a week she has thought of it as aimed — at her, at the house, at the last name on a list. She had needed it to be aimed, because a thing that is aimed has a logic and a logic has an end and the end is Wednesday. Standing on the inlet ice she understands that she had that wrong, or only half right, and the half she had wrong is the half that matters. Dale Numminen was not on the list. Dale Numminen was a man from the next township who took a shortcut across a bay on a warm night because the long way around is the long way around. There is no version of the hundred years that has his name in it. He crossed the wrong water at the worst place in the warmest March in forty years, and the wrong water took him, and it was not because of who he was. It was because of where he was, and when.

A hundred years of it, she thinks. A hundred years held down under a man in a sauna once a week, and now no man, and the warmth coming up out of the south, and the ice going honest all over the lake at once. It is not aiming anymore. It has stopped being able to aim, if it ever could, the way a thing that has been compressed for a century is not aiming when it finally comes apart — it is only coming apart, in every direction, into whatever happens to be there. And what happened to be there, last night, at the softest spot on the whole lake, was Dale Numminen.

She thinks: *she is in pain.* She thinks it plainly, standing in the wind, and lets it sit, the way her father let things sit in the ledger without a word in the margin. A hundred years in cold water with the wrong done and never undone, and the only ones who knew built a man's life around keeping her quiet and called it a watch. That is not patience. She had thought it was patience. It is pain that has been still for so long it learned to look like patience, and the stillness has broken, and pain that breaks does not aim. It just comes up. And anything in the water when it comes up is in the water when it comes up.

---

They bring him up while she is standing there. She does not see it, and she is glad, after, that she does not see it. The dry-suit men surface and there is a change in the men on the ice, a closing-in, the line tender and the kid named Cal and another man going to the hole and crouching, their backs to the shore, their bodies making a wall the way men's bodies do at a thing like this without anyone telling them to, and she understands from the wall what is happening on the other side of it and she turns and looks at the trees on the far shore instead.

Mikko's hand comes onto her shoulder. He doesn't say anything. His hand is warm and it stays.

Behind her there is the sound of the ambulance crew coming down at last, unhurried, with the long basket, and the careful low voices of men doing a thing they have done before and hate, and the creak of the rotten ice taking the weight, and she keeps her eyes on the far trees and does not turn around until the basket goes by behind her, and even then she only sees the shape of it under the blanket going up the bank, four men carrying, watching their feet on the soft ice, and that is all. That is all she lets herself have of it, and it is enough. It is more than enough. It will be in her for the rest of her life, the shape going up the bank under the blanket and the men watching their feet.

Dale Numminen. She has the name now. She will carry it. She thinks of the Hietalas carrying a name for a hundred years without ever once saying it out loud at a table, her great-grandfather and her grandfather and her father each in turn folding a dead woman's name into the marrow of an ordinary life and going down to a stove once a week and never telling a soul, and she understands that this is how the name gets carried — not in a ledger, not in a margin, but like this, a shape under a blanket on a March morning that you were there to see and cannot put down. She is a carrier now. She joined them this morning, on the inlet ice, in the warm wrong wind.

The knocking, all week, had frightened her. The footprints, the cold in the floor, the count coming up at midnight seven seconds apart. She had been afraid the way you are afraid of a thing that wants to scare you. She is done being afraid that way. This is a different country and she has crossed into it standing on the ice. It is not trying to scare her. Being scared, it turns out, was the safe version, the version where the worst that happens is a bad night and a steady hand at dawn. The worst that happens is a red snowmobile idling all night over black water with the glove pulled off the hand, and a man from Tioga who never heard the name Mindemoyen going up a bank under a blanket. It can do that. It did that. It will do it again, to whoever is on the water, until Wednesday, and Wednesday is not soon enough, and there is nothing between now and Wednesday but the days, and the nights, and the count.

---

They drive back the long way, around the bay, the way Dale Numminen did not.

She does not go in when Mikko drops her. She tells him she will be fine, which is not a thing she believes, and he looks at her like he does not believe it either, and then he says he'll be back before dark and that she is not to be alone tonight, and she does not argue, because she does not want to be alone tonight and there is no use pretending otherwise to a man who has had his hand on her shoulder at a recovery.

When the truck is gone she does not go in. She walks down across the yard to the sauna, in the warm air, with the snow gone slick and gray under her boots, and she stands outside the door of it with her hand flat on the cedar wall the way she has done before. The wood is cold. The day is warm and the wood is cold, and under her feet, down through the slumping snow and the frozen ground and the soft going-honest ice, there is the thing she has no word for except that it knows she is standing there. It counts her. She is on the list and Dale Numminen never was, and it took Dale Numminen anyway, and she stands with her cold hand on the cold wood and does not take it away.

"One more day," she says to the wall. She says it the way you say a number you mean to outlast, not the way you say a thing for comfort. "Hold on until tomorrow."

The wood does not warm under her hand. She did not expect it to.
