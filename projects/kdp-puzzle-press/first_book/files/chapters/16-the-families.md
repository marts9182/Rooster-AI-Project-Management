She and Mikko go to the Lehtinen house Sunday afternoon, while the knocking from the night before is still present in her body, in the specific way that a sound you've heard all night doesn't leave when it stops but settles into you and keeps going at a frequency only you can feel.

The Lehtinens are over toward Crystal Falls, a half hour of two-lane that the thaw has turned dark and wet down the wheel ruts, the snowbanks along the shoulder slumped and grey and pulling away from the asphalt the way old caulk pulls away from a window. Water stands in the low spots. Mikko drives around the worst of it without comment. Aino watches the country go by — the trailers, the woodpiles, the sugar shacks not yet tapped — and thinks it looks like every March she has ever seen here and is not, underneath, anything like them.

Marta Lehtinen answers the door. A woman in her sixties, heavyset, careful eyes, an apron on over a Sunday cardigan as though they have caught her between one task and another. She looks at Aino for a moment before she looks at Mikko, and Aino understands from the look that Marta knows who she is — knows the face, or the name, or the shape of what the name carries — and that the knowing does not make her glad.

"Aino Hietala," Marta says. Not a question.

"Yes."

"You'd better come in, then."

The kitchen is warm and smells of coffee and of something baked earlier in the day, cardamom, the *pulla* gone now but the smell of it still in the walls. Her brother Juhani is at the table with a cup in front of him and the expression of a man who has been told something he didn't want to know and is still deciding how to hold it. He is older than Marta, thin where she is heavy, with big spotted hands flat on the oilcloth. He does not get up. He nods at Mikko, whom he knows, and at Aino, whom he does not, and goes back to looking at his coffee.

Marta makes coffee for the new arrivals because it is what is done. She does not ask whether they want it. She sets the cups out, and the cream in its glass pitcher, and a plate she does not need to set out but does, and only when everyone has a cup in front of them does she sit, and then there is the small ordinary silence of four people at a table with nothing to do but say the thing.

Aino says it.

She tells them everything. Not the careful version, not the version she'd told before that left out the worst of it — she tells them about the watch her great-grandfather started and her father kept and how it ended the morning her father died in this same kind of kitchen making this same coffee. She tells them about the footprints coming up from the lake to the foundation and going nowhere. She tells them about the knocking, three and then the pause and then three, and about the seven seconds, and she watches Juhani's hand go still on the oilcloth when she says the seven seconds. She tells them about the cold in the floor that no furnace explains, and about the forecast, the orange map, the ice running thinner every March in her father's own numbers, fourteen inches and then twelve and now whatever it is, going. She tells it plainly because there is no time left for careful.

When she finishes the kitchen is very quiet. Out the window over the sink a line of melt runs off the eave in a thin steady rope and ticks into a pail somebody has set there to catch it.

Marta has both hands around her cup. "My father told us some of it," she says. "Not all. Enough that when I was a girl I was afraid of that lake and didn't have a word for why." She looks at her brother. "Juhani had it worse."

Juhani sets his cup down.

"What are you asking us to do," he says.

"Stand at the shore on Wednesday. That's all."

He looks at her, and she can see him deciding something, running calculations she isn't privy to. He is quiet long enough that the pail ticks twice more. When he speaks it comes slow, the words set down one at a time the way his hands are set down on the table.

"My grandfather is dead," he says. "He's been dead for thirty years. He never told me what he did — my father told me, and my father only told me because he was dying and couldn't carry it out the door with him. My grandfather died without ever saying a word about it to anyone. Sat in this family for fifty years like a stone in a coat pocket and never once put it on the table." He pauses. "I've spent thirty years being angry at a dead man. Which is a very particular kind of useless. You can't get anywhere with it. You can't make him answer. You can't even make him sorry, because for all I know he was sorry every day of his life and just never said, and that's the worst of the two, not knowing which it was."

"Juhani," Marta says quietly.

"I'm not refusing." He says it to his sister first and then to Aino, turning the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer as he does, an old man's small habit. "I'm just saying. A man waits thirty years to be asked to do a thing, he's allowed a minute to say what it was like, waiting." He picks up the coffee again. "Wednesday. We'll be there. Both of us. What time."

"First light."

He nods. "First light. We'll be there before the sun's up. I've been up before the sun every day of my life; one more won't hurt me." Something moves at the corner of his mouth that on a less tired man might have been the start of a smile and is not, quite. "Tell the Saaris, did you?"

"Tomorrow," Aino says. "We're going tomorrow."

Marta and Juhani look at each other across the table, and Aino sees the look and does not understand it yet, and files it.

"Mm," Juhani says, and that is all he says about the Saaris.

---

The Saari property she leaves until Monday, because there is an order to these things and because some part of her wanted the easier visit first, the way you take the smaller of two hard calls first to learn how to make the larger one. Mikko comes by in the morning. He drives. The Saaris are on the north end of the lake, the far end, the end her father's sauna looks at across a quarter mile of ice on the rare days the air is clear enough to see that far.

On the way he tells her what he knows about Erik Saari. Seventies, retired off the mine and then off twenty more years of small engine repair he did out of a shed. Widower — wife died four years ago, cancer, the slow kind, and Erik nursed her through it himself and came out the other side of it quieter than he went in. Keeps mostly to himself. Has a way of looking at the lake, Mikko says, that Mikko has always understood without having the words for it, and now that he's older he thinks he has a few of the words and wishes he didn't.

"And there's a brother," Mikko says, after a stretch of road.

"A brother."

"Reino. Younger. Five, six years younger." Mikko's mouth does a thing. "He's a different animal. You'll see if he's around. Hope he's not."

The Saari place comes up on the left, set back off the road behind a stand of bare birch — a low house, a barn gone to a lean, a yard kept the way Erik keeps things, which is to say kept. A dog comes off the porch and stops at the edge of the yard and barks twice without much in it and then just watches.

Erik is outside splitting wood. He works in the methodical way of a man who has split a great many cords, the maul up and the maul down and the round falling open in two clean halves and the next round set up on the block without a wasted motion, and he watches the truck come in with the same stillness, the maul resting head-down on the block and his hand on the haft. But when he sees Aino get out of the truck something changes in his face. Not fear, exactly. More like recognition — the look of a man who has been waiting for a knock at the door and has just heard the car that brings it.

He has felt the cold too. She can see it on him the way she saw it on Juhani.

He sets the maul against the block. "Hietala," he says, the same way Marta said it. Then, after a beat, to Mikko: "Mikko." And then he opens the porch door and holds it, which is the whole of his invitation, and they go inside.

The house is clean and spare and a little too quiet, a house run by one person who used to be two. He puts coffee on without asking, the way Marta did, and while it heats he sits down across from her at the table with his hands flat on his knees and his eyes on a point past her shoulder, and she tells it again. The full version. The watch, the footprints, the knocking, the count. He listens without moving. When she gets to the seven seconds he closes his eyes briefly, just for a moment, the way a man does when a doctor names the thing he already knew the name of, and she understands that he has heard it too, that it has come up to him across the ice from wherever he sleeps on the north end of this lake, three knocks and seven seconds, faint maybe but his.

When she finishes he is quiet for a long time. The coffee comes to its boil behind him and he gets up and pours it and sets the cups down and sits again before he says anything.

"No," he says.

She waits.

"I'm not going to stand on that shore," he says. "I'm not going to stand there and — perform something. My grandfather is dead. What he did is done. Standing on a shore doesn't undo it."

"Nobody said it undoes it," Mikko says carefully.

"Then what does it do?"

The room is very quiet. Out the window the bare birches are still. Beyond them the lake, and on the lake, faint, the first dark lines she has been watching come and go on her own end — cracks opening in the white where the thaw is getting down into the ice and working it, thin as pencil yet, nothing like the webbing she half expects to see by the end of the week, but there, and not where March cracks belong. She makes herself not look at them too long.

"What does it *do*," Erik says again, lower.

And before anyone can answer there is a truck in the yard.

The dog does not bark at it. Erik's shoulders set. A door slams, and boots come up the porch, and a man lets himself in without knocking — younger than Erik but not young, hard through the shoulders, a face that is Erik's face with the patience taken out of it and something else put in its place. He stops in the kitchen doorway and takes in the table, the cups, the strangers, and his eyes land on Aino and stay there.

"Reino," Erik says. "We've got company."

"I see that." Reino does not sit. He leans on the doorframe with his arms crossed and looks at Aino the way you look at a thing somebody's trying to sell you. "Hietala. Sure. Come up here to tell us about the lake." He says *the lake* the way another man might say a curse. "Heard the speech yet?" he asks Erik, not taking his eyes off her. "The standing-on-the-shore speech?"

"We were getting to it," Mikko says.

"I'll save you the time." Reino comes off the doorframe. "Wednesday. First light. Everybody lines up on the ice and holds hands and the Indians do a thing and we all feel better and the lake's our friend again. That about it?"

"That's not it," Aino says.

"It's close enough." He is not loud. That is the thing she notices, the thing that makes him worse than loud — there is no heat in it, or there is heat under it but he has it banked, controlled, the way Erik has the wood under control. "My grandfather drowned a woman in that lake. So they tell it. I never met him. I never met her. And I'm supposed to go stand on a shore at dawn and *acknowledge* it." The word comes out with the bark off. "You know what that does? Nothing. You want to know what stops a thing? You stop it. You don't ask it nice. You don't wait till Wednesday because somebody downstate has to be *asked*, and asking a thing like this is not asked quickly" — and she goes cold, because that is nearly Louise's own phrase, and she has no idea how it has gotten into this man's mouth, unless it is only that there is one way these things get said. "Three days. We sit on our hands for three more days because that's the polite schedule. You've had it in your floor every night. How many more nights you want."

"None," Aino says.

"None." He nods like she's proved his point. "Me neither. So why are we waiting."

"Because there's a way it's done," Mikko says. "And the way it's done is theirs to lead, not ours."

"The way it's *done*." Reino looks at his brother. "Erik. Tell me you're not buying this. Standing on a shore. You've been hearing it all week, same as me, worse than me, you're closer. You going to fix it by standing still?"

Erik does not answer right away. His hands are flat on the table. "I haven't decided what I'm doing," he says.

"That's a no with a hat on." Reino reaches over and takes a heel of bread off the counter as if he owns the place, which in some old way he half does, and turns it over in his hand and doesn't eat it. "I'll tell you what's getting done. That ice is going by Wednesday or Thursday, your own forecast says so. When it goes, the water opens up. And a thing that lives in the water under the ice, you've got a window, right there, while it's between. You don't stand on the shore and watch the window close. You go to the water and you end it." He sets the bread down. "I'm not going to a funeral for a hundred-year-old crime and calling it fixed. I'm done waiting. I've been done waiting a while."

"Reino," Erik says.

"Don't." He is already moving toward the door. "You do your Wednesday. Stand there with the woman and the priest from Crystal Falls and whoever else. I'll handle the part that handles itself." At the door he stops and looks back at Aino, and for one moment there is something under the contempt that is not contempt at all, that is closer to what she saw in Juhani — thirty years of it, fifty, grief gone hard from having nowhere to go. "You think it wants to be acknowledged," he says. "It doesn't want to be acknowledged. It wants what it wants. The difference is going to cost you."

The door bangs. The truck starts. The dog, outside, does not bark at it leaving either.

In the quiet after, the pencil-lines on the lake are exactly where they were, and the coffee has stopped steaming, and Erik Saari sits with his hands flat on the table and his eyes on the place where his brother was standing.

"He lost his boy to that lake," Erik says, to no one in particular. "Long time ago. Through the ice, December, before any of us knew what the ice was doing or why. Reino's never said the two things in the same breath — the boy and the woman. But he carries them in the same pocket. He's been carrying them a long time."

Nobody says anything.

"He won't wait," Erik says. It is not a question and it is not a warning, exactly; it is a man stating a fact about his brother that he has known longer than he has known these strangers. "He never has."

Then he is quiet for a very long time. Long enough that Aino thinks he is going to come back to his *no*, going to stand up and indicate the meeting is over. The melt ticks somewhere outside. The dog comes up onto the porch and lies down against the door with a thump she can feel through the floor, and the floor here is just a floor, just wood and a dog's weight on it, and she is grateful for that out of all proportion to what it is.

"What does it do," Erik says. Back to his question, but the heat gone out of it; he is asking now, not pushing. "Standing there. I want a real answer. Not the speech my brother thinks you've got. The real one."

"It means not looking away," Mikko says. "It means your family was there and you're the one who's left and you show up. That's all. Whatever Louise Swifthawk's family does — that's theirs. We just stand there and we don't look away."

Erik looks at his hands. He is a big man, still solid despite his age, the kind who has dealt with hard things by dealing with them physically — the wood, the kept yard, the wife nursed through to the end, the life lived in deliberate order. She can see him trying to set this thing down in that order and finding that it will not lie flat.

"My grandfather wouldn't stand there," he says slowly. "If he were alive. He'd do what Reino's going to do. Or he'd do nothing, which is what he did, all his life. Stand there or stand away — that's the choice in this family. There's never been a middle of it." He turns the cup a quarter-turn, the same small habit Juhani had, and she wonders if it is a thing all the old men here do or only the ones who are deciding something. "I haven't slept well in a week."

"Neither have I," she says.

"No," he says. "I expect not." He looks up at her then, full on, for the first time since she came in. "What time."

"First light," she says. "Wednesday."

He nods once. It is not a warm nod. It is the nod of a man who has looked at something he has been avoiding for fifty years and decided, with great difficulty, to stop avoiding it — and underneath it, she thinks, a second decision he does not say out loud, which is to be the brother who stands and not the brother who goes to the water.

"I'll be there," he says. Then, after a moment, looking at the window, at the truck-shaped absence in the yard: "I don't know that I can answer for Reino."

"Nobody's asking you to," Mikko says.

"I know." Erik keeps looking at the window. "I'm telling you anyway."

---

In the truck driving back the relief she expected doesn't come. She had thought that getting all four families to yes would feel like setting something down. It does not. It feels like she has spent two days carrying the same weight up to two more doors and that the weight is now larger, because she is carrying Reino in it too — the truck banging out of the yard, *the difference is going to cost you*, a man who will not wait three days for a thing that has waited a hundred years.

"That went about how I figured," Mikko says.

"Erik said yes."

"Erik said yes." He drives a while. "Reino's the one to watch. The families are going to have to sit down before Wednesday and get straight with each other — who's doing what, who's standing where. There's two minds about it now and there can't be two minds about it on Wednesday." He glances at her. "Tonight, maybe. Get everybody in one room while Erik's still got the warm of saying yes on him. Before Reino gets to him again."

"Tonight," she says.

The lake goes by on the left, between the trees, then open along the western shore. The melt is running off every roof they pass and standing in every ditch, and the sky has come down low and grey and close, and the snowbanks are dark and slumping, and the whole country has the wet heavy smell of a thing thawing that does not yet know whether it is the smell of spring or of something opening that should have stayed shut. Out on the white she can see the lines from the road, faint, here and there, the thaw getting its fingers into the ice.

She thinks about her father. All the nights he drove this road in the dark, down to the sauna, to sit in the heat above the water and keep the thing below aware of his presence, his name, his attendance. Thirty years of it. Alone. And it occurs to her, watching the cracks come and go between the trees, that her father was the only one of all of them who chose the middle of it — not standing on the shore and not going to the water, but sitting in the heat, week after week, holding the line where it was. She does not know yet whether that was the brave thing or only the longest way of doing nothing. She thinks her father did not know either. She thinks that is why he never said a word.

"One more night," Mikko says.

"One more night," she says, and does not say what she is thinking, which is that one more night assumes that everyone waits for Wednesday, and that she has just met a man who will not.

She looks over at Mikko. He is watching the road.

She looks back at the lake.

One more night, if they are lucky. She is no longer sure they are going to be lucky.
