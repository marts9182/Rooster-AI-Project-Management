The silence is the strange part.

Not the knocking. The knocking she had grown used to, the way you grow used to a clock, until the room was the count and the count was the room. It is the absence of it that she cannot find a place to put. She stands in the kitchen with the coffee going cold in her hand and the burners still hissing low and the couch shoved sideways against the table where they had dragged it in the night, and the house around her is just a house. Forty-two degrees and getting no colder. The furnace running. The refrigerator coming on with its small ordinary shudder. She listens to that and it is only itself.

"It's Wednesday," Mikko had said, and now he is asleep, gone all at once the way exhausted men go, sitting up against the arm of the couch with the quilt half off him and his mouth open. She does not wake him. She lets him have it.

She drinks her coffee. It is bad and cold and she drinks it anyway. The light comes up gray in the window over the sink, no sun in it yet, just the thinning of the dark into something she could read a page by, and she watches it the way she has watched it every morning since she came, the slow return of the yard out of the black — the snow, the tree line, the slope she cannot see the bottom of from here.

At six-fifteen she goes to the back door and unlocks it and opens it and looks out at the yard.

The snow has been disturbed in a radius around the entire house — not footprints, not the small deliberate prints she'd grown used to, but something different, the snow pushed away from the foundation in every direction as though something had been pressing against the house from outside all night. She looks at it for a long time.

She closes the door. She locks it again.

---

She has things to do before Louise comes.

She does them slowly. There is no hurry in her, and she is too tired for hurry anyway, the kind of tired that has gone past wanting sleep and out the other side into a flat clear patience, and she moves through the cold house the way you move through a thing you are doing for the last time and want to do right.

She gets the box first. There is a wooden one in the basement workshop, on the floor under the bench, a crate her father built or her grandfather did — dovetailed at the corners, the joints tight, the wood gone the color of weak tea. It is empty but for a coil of wire and a tin of nails, and she sets those on the bench and takes the box and it is light in her hands, light enough that she knows what she is going to do is mostly fill it. The workshop is the coldest room in the house except the one she has not gone into yet. The pegboard with the tools she finally has names for. The chisels in their graduated order. The mallet worn smooth in the one place. She does not look at them long. She carries the box out.

The root cellar is behind the furnace on the north wall, where the warmth off the metal stops a foot short of the stone, and she turns sideways to get back into the dark there and fits the iron key into the forged plate. It turns with the single dry sound it made the first time. The cold comes out at her before the door is all the way open — lower than the basement's cold, with the wet edge to it, the smell riding out on it of earth and stone-iron and under those the sourness that is almost sweet. She has been here before. She knows what it costs to stand in it. She stands in it.

She had wrapped the documents back in the oilcloth and tied the cord and set them where they had been, on the center shelf, because they were not hers to keep and not hers to throw away. She had told herself that. She takes them down now. The land claim. The letters to and from the county, formal and dry. The list of names in two hands, Eino's and her father's, four columns, her own name last on the Hietala column with nothing under it. She does not unwrap it here in the cold to look at it. She knows what it says. She sets the bundle in the box and it goes down with a small weight that is more than paper should have.

The jars she leaves. The tin box that rattles she leaves. The small wrapped figure at the back of the stone shelf she leaves where it stands, and she does not touch it, because it is not hers and she does not know what it is and she has decided to let it go on being something she does not know. She backs out and pulls the door to and turns the iron key the other way, the bolt sliding back into the stone, and she stands a moment with her hand flat on the old close-grained boards. Then she takes her hand away.

Toivo's ledgers are on the basement shelf where they have always been, four of them, the cloth covers soft at the corners. She takes them down one at a time and stacks them in the box on top of the oilcloth. *Perhaps he will not have to carry this.* She does not open them. She knows the lines that matter and she does not need to see them in his hand again this morning. The box is heavier now when she lifts it, and she carries it up the basement stairs with both arms and sets it on the kitchen floor by the door and stands a moment getting her breath.

Mikko sleeps. The light in the window is grayer, nearer to morning.

There is one more.

---

She goes down the hill in the gray pre-dawn with her coat on and her flashlight in her pocket and she does not bring the box, because what she is going for she wants to carry up in her hand.

The path is the same. The birches first and then the pines, the packed trail her father kept clear over sixty years of going down to sit in the dark and watch the water. The cold is different out here than in the house — older, with the lake in it. The sauna comes up out of the trees ahead of her, the dark lower walls and the cedar her father put in the last summer she was home, the whole structure stepping out over the ice on its heavy pilings. She fits the padlock key. The hasp opens.

Inside, the changing room is the temperature of the outdoors and then some, the cold dense and close. Her father's worn sandals under the bench where she left them. The stiff towel on its hook. She goes through to the sauna proper and the stove is dead — no fire in the kiuas since she let the last one go, the stones up top gone to the same gray cold as everything else, the firebox below shut and silent. The band of carved marks runs level around the room at the height it has always run, the same in the steam and out of it, and there is no steam now. There is nothing now. It is the coldest room she has been in this morning and the stillest, and the stillness has the lake under it.

She kneels in front of the lower bench. She finds the seam with her fingers, the rectangular section set into the floor of the platform, cut so fine it disappears into the plank lines, and she does not need the crowbar this time; she knows where to lift. The section comes up. Below it the lockbox, bolted to the framing, the combination lock cold enough on the hasp that it stings her fingers when she turns the dial. Her grandmother Aili's birthday, two days before her own, the cake they shared when she was small. The lock opens.

Inside: Eino's notebook, older than the ledgers, the leather dark and dry. The key she'd found wrapped beside it the first time is gone — it is in the root cellar door now, it has been since she found what it opened. Only the notebook left. She takes it out and holds it and it is so cold from the box that it is almost wet to the touch, the way iron is in deep winter, and she thinks of Eino writing *kaunis* in it and underlining it, a man putting one true word down where no one would read it for a hundred years.

She closes the lockbox. She spins the dial. She lowers the section of bench back into the floor and presses it flush.

She knows it is the last time she will open that box. She lets herself know it, kneeling on the cold boards with the notebook against her chest. Her father knelt here. His father did. The whole line of them down to her, opening this box in the heat and the dark and closing it again, keeping the one word and the four ledgers and the list of names under the weight of the sauna and the weight of themselves. She is the one who gets to stop.

The hatch is over by the far wall, the rope handle flush in the planks, the floor under it nothing but a few feet of air and then the ice. She does not open it. She has no reason to and one good reason not to, and she has learned, this winter, to leave shut the things that ask to be left shut. She stands with the notebook in her hand and looks at the hatch and then she turns and goes out and locks the padlock behind her and starts up the hill.

---

Hank's ledgers she leaves in the basement.

Forty-two of them, her father's whole watch set down year on year, the cold even hand and at the very end the entries gone short — *Tired.* *She is gone. I sat in the sauna all night.* She had stood in front of that shelf with the box already heavy at her feet and her hand on the first of them and she had not been able to do it. The documents are not hers and the ledgers from before her father are not hers, but these are. These are the part of it she has not decided about. She is not sure whether they go in the box and down to the water, or whether they stay, whether there is something in keeping them that is not the same as keeping the secret. She does not know. She is not ready to know, and she has learned not to force a thing she is not ready for, and so she leaves them on the shelf in the order he left them and she goes up the stairs.

She sets Eino's notebook in the box on top of everything else. The box is full now, or full enough. She closes the lid.

She goes to wake Mikko. He is still on the couch in the warm kitchen, the quilt fallen, and she puts her hand on his shoulder. His shoulder is warm through the flannel, the one warm thing she has touched all morning, and she keeps her hand there a second longer than she needs to.

"They'll be here soon," she says. "We need to go down to the shore."

He opens his eyes. He looks at her, surfacing, putting the morning together. He looks at the gray window and the box by the door. He sits up.

"Wednesday," he says.

"Wednesday."

He pushes the quilt off and finds his boots. She turns the burners off, one by one, the small blue flames going out, and the kitchen is suddenly only as warm as it is going to stay. She picks up the box.

They go out the back, into the radius of pushed snow, and down the path.

---

The lake is wrong in the early light.

The cracks she'd seen the day before have spread overnight — the whole surface of the lake is webbed with them now, lines running in every direction from the point thirty feet out from the sauna, so that the ice looks less like ice than like something that has been holding its shape through effort and is close to stopping. The surface is a pale gray-white in the morning and it is moving, subtly, in a way ice does not move — a slow pulse, almost breathing, as though whatever is below it has been pressing up against it all night the way it pressed at the house.

The fish houses are gone. She'd thought their owners had pulled them off for the season. Looking at the ice now she is less certain.

She sets the box down at the edge of the shore, in the snow, where it will be ready when it is needed. She stands beside Mikko at the water's edge and she looks at the lake and she feels the presence of it the way she has felt it since the day she arrived — the weight and the cold and the attention of something very old and very tired and very angry — and she does not look away.

They wait.
