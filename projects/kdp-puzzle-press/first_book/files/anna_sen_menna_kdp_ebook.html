Anna Sen Mennä

Seven Martin

Chapter 1

The Drive Up

The radiator starts banging at four in the morning and that's the end of sleep. Aino lies there for twenty minutes listening to it, waiting for it to stop, and when it doesn't she gets up and makes coffee and stands at the kitchen window watching the parking lot fill with the kind of February dark that doesn't become light so much as it becomes a different shade of dark. She showers. She finishes packing the bag she'd started the night before and then abandoned. She carries everything down to the Subaru in two trips and by five-thirty she's on the road, an hour earlier than she'd planned, which is fine. Better than lying there. Better than listening to the radiator and thinking.

The interstate is quiet. She turns the radio on and off twice before leaving it off. There's nothing she wants to hear.

She'd stopped at the Pick 'n Save last night — peanut butter, apples, a sleeve of saltines, things that wouldn't go bad in her father's house because she didn't know what nineteen years did to a refrigerator and she didn't want to find out hungry. The bags are on the passenger seat. She'd eaten an apple for dinner in the parking lot before driving home and felt sorry for herself for approximately thirty seconds, which felt like enough.

Her father's name was Henrik Hietala, though no one had called him that. He'd been Hank since before she was born. He died six days ago in his kitchen in Kivijärvi, Michigan, at the age of seventy-seven, while making coffee. The neighbor found him in the afternoon when he didn't show up at the bar. The lawyer told her all of this on the phone while she sat at her desk at work with a half-finished form on her screen, a request for medical records that was due the next morning, and she'd put the lawyer on hold twice — once to finish with the Social Security office and once because she'd needed a minute, just one, before she came back on and said yes, she would come, and when was the funeral.

Friday. The funeral is Friday.

She'd finished the form before going home. She doesn't know why she'd done that. Habit, probably. The kind of habit that holds you together when nothing else will.

—

The snow starts around Wausau, fine and dry and not serious yet, just enough to make her turn on the wipers. The man on the radio — she's tried it again, settled on a basketball game because at least basketball has a score, something definite happening — is calling the game in that voice that sports men have, the big unhurried authority of it. She's not following. It's just a voice in the car. She's grateful for it.

She thinks about the coffee maker.

This is what her brain keeps doing. Not the big things, not the funeral or the house or what happens after, but the coffee maker — whether her father had still been using the old percolator or whether at some point he'd bought one of the machines with the pods. She doesn't know why this matters to her. It doesn't matter. But she'd been in his kitchen a hundred times as a girl and the percolator had always been there, the same dented aluminum one, and she wants to know if it was still there when he died and she can't ask anyone because it would be a strange thing to ask.

She'd been nineteen the last time she was in that kitchen. Nineteen. Her father would have been forty-eight, which seems impossible now. He'd made her instant Folgers because she'd asked for instant Folgers, which she'd done because she was nineteen and contrary and she doesn't even like instant Folgers and she has thought about that cup of coffee with a regularity that is embarrassing, off and on, for nineteen years.

The truck in front of her slows. She slows. The snow is heavier now, the wipers earning their keep, and the road is going gray and soft at the shoulders. She backs off to forty-five and settles in.

She has not cried yet. She keeps waiting to and it keeps not happening. She cried at a commercial last month — a dog, a soldier coming home, deeply manipulative, she'd known it was manipulative while it was happening — but she has driven two hours toward her dead father's house and her eyes are dry and she doesn't know what to make of herself.

—

She crosses into Michigan at four-eighteen. The sun is already going — February at this latitude doesn't fool around — and the sky has that bruised, purple-gray look of a winter evening on the range. She knows this sky. She'd forgotten that she knew it.

The trees change when she crosses the state line, or maybe she just starts noticing them. The pines up here are different from the ones in Wisconsin — older, denser, crowding close to the road like they've decided the right-of-way is an inconvenience they've been tolerating for a hundred years. Her father used to tell her that the white pines along this stretch had been here when his great-grandfather stepped off the boat. They were already big then, he'd said. Eino told his wife he thought he'd come to the wrong country. She'd been in the back seat, eight years old maybe, and the trees had seemed enormous to her, cathedral-enormous, the light coming through them in columns. She'd believed every word.

The basketball game has ended. The same man is doing the postgame now, a detailed and apparently urgent discussion of what went wrong in the third quarter. She is not listening at all.

The radio cuts out.

Not static — silence. A clean, total silence, like someone reached over and turned it off, which no one did. The display still shows the station. The volume is still up. She glances at the signal bars and sees they're full, which they shouldn't be — there's never signal on this stretch, never has been, the hills or the trees or something kills it every time. She knows this road.

She doesn't touch the dial.

A dead spot, she thinks. They happen. You drop a station and pick up the silence between, and the display just hasn't caught up. Except the display has never caught up wrong before, not in nineteen years on this road, and the bars are full, and a dead spot is static, fuzz, the ghost of a station two counties over bleeding through — a dead spot is not nothing. The hills, then. The trees. Something between her and the tower, the dense pines swallowing it the way they swallow the light. But she'd lose the signal for that, lose the bars, and the bars are full.

She gives it the next reasonable thing. A fault at the station. A man at a board somewhere reaching for the wrong switch. That's allowed. That happens.

She drives. The silence holds. It is not the silence before she'd turned the radio on; that had been just quiet, the ordinary quiet of a car and a road. This is the other kind. The kind that is waiting for something. Like the quiet after someone asks a question — the room holding its breath for the answer. She counts. She doesn't mean to and then she's doing it anyway, the seconds, the way you count the gap between lightning and the thunder to know how far off the storm is. Four. Five. Six.

The man comes back mid-sentence: —the kind of decision that'll cost you in March. No static, no fade-in, just there again like he'd never been gone, like the gap had been hers and not his.

She exhales slowly. She hadn't realized she'd stopped breathing.

A sign comes up out of the snow, green and reflective, and her eyes go to it the way eyes do on an empty road, hungry for anything. WATERSMEET 11. She reads it twice. She has no business in Watersmeet — she's never had business in Watersmeet — but the name sits there a beat longer than it should, the way a name does when you've heard it somewhere and can't place where. Then the sign is behind her and the road is dark again. She's forty minutes past the exit before it occurs to her that she should have eaten something. She eats an apple without tasting it.

—

She's been in this part of the state before, obviously, but she hasn't been here in so long that it hits her strangely — familiar and not, the way a word looks wrong if you stare at it too long. She passes Bear's Bar, which she'd forgotten entirely until she sees it and then remembers it completely: the neon, the gravel lot, the smell of cigarettes that had hit her in the face the one time her father had taken her inside at age eleven to use the bathroom. She passes the mine pits, which she had not forgotten. They're everywhere up here, filled with green water in summer — her father had called them by their old company names, the Cleveland-Cliffs, the Mather B, the Pewabic, ghost names for ghost holes in the ground.

The sign for Kivijärvi comes up at six forty-one. Population 711 — the number on its own little panel bolted underneath the main sign, the same sign she'd read from the back seat as a child, only then it had said 1,400.

She slows to thirty-five and the town comes up around her, what's left of it. The Lutheran church. The Finnish Hall, dark. The diner. Hakala's with two trucks in the lot. The gas station. The cross street at the bottom of the hill that goes to the lake.

She doesn't turn. She goes the other way.

The gravel road has been plowed, snow piled high on both sides. She follows it half a mile until it bends and the house appears in her headlights: the porch, the peak of the roof, the big Norway pines around it, and her father's truck in the driveway where it has sat since Wednesday and where it will sit until she moves it.

She parks and sits in the car for a moment. She's not sure what she's waiting for.

Nothing, she decides. She's not waiting for anything.

She gets out. The cold hits her immediately — not the temperature, which is twenty and not even bad for here, but the quality of it, that specific U.P. dryness that she'd forgotten, like the air is made of something finer than regular air. Her breath comes out in a cloud and disappears. The pines are perfectly still.

And then, from behind the house — down the slope, from the direction of the lake she can't see but knows is there, knows the way she knows her own name — there's a sound. A long, low crack, deep and resonant, rolling out across the ice.

Just the ice, she tells herself. The ice does that. She's heard it a thousand times.

She gets her bag from the back seat and goes to the porch. The light above the door is on — the neighbor, or the lawyer, somebody left it on for her — and as she reaches the bottom step it flickers. Once. A pause, long enough that she thinks it's done. Then again. Another pause, longer, the dark coming back and sitting there. Then a third time, and this time she's stopped on the step watching it, her bag in her hand, the cold coming up through her boots. Then it holds steady, the way it must have been holding steady all along.

Old wiring, she thinks. Old house.

She lets herself in.

The smell of him is everywhere, coffee and sawdust and the particular cold of a house that's been sitting for a week with no one in it. And underneath all that something else, something she can't name, something like the smell of a basement or a lake at the end of winter — dark and cold and patient.

She sets her bag down. She looks at the kitchen counter.

The percolator is there. The same one, the dented aluminum one, forty years old if it's a day. She crosses the kitchen and puts her hand on it. Long cold.

She hadn't known until this moment that she'd been hoping it would still be there. She doesn't know what it means that it is.

—

She sleeps badly, which she'd expected. The house makes noises. She wakes twice thinking she's heard something and both times it's nothing, just a settling board or the furnace, and she lies in her childhood bed under the quilt her grandmother made her and stares at the ceiling and doesn't sleep.

The third time she wakes it's nearly three. And this time it isn't nothing.

She lies still and listens. It's coming from outside — from the direction of the lake, half a mile through the trees. A sound she can't quite name. Rhythmic, with gaps. Not mechanical. She waits for it to make sense. It doesn't. After a few minutes it stops.

She gets out of bed and goes to the kitchen window. She doesn't turn on the light.

The moon is up and the yard is bright with it, the snow lit almost blue. The pines at the far edge are black. The yard between the house and the trees is smooth and unbroken except —

Except for the footprints.

They come from the tree line, single file, and they cross the yard and they end at the back porch. Small prints. Evenly spaced. Too small for an adult, too deliberate for an animal.

Aino stands at the window in the dark and looks at them for a long time.

Animal, she thinks. Has to be. Some animal she's not thinking of right now because it's three in the morning and she's exhausted and has been driving for thirteen hours and her father is dead.

She goes back to bed. She pulls the quilt up. She listens to the house.

She doesn't sleep.

Chapter 2

Hakala's

She wakes at seven-fifteen to gray light through curtains she made from a bedsheet when she was fourteen and which are apparently still doing the job. For a moment she doesn't know where she is — the ceiling is wrong, too low, the smell is wrong, too old — and then she does know and lies there with it for a minute before getting up.

The quilt goes back. Her feet find the cold floor.

In the kitchen she starts the percolator and while it heats she goes to the window. The yard is still bright from last night's moon, though the sun hasn't cleared the pines yet and won't for another hour. She looks at the snow.

The footprints are still there.

She'd half-convinced herself in the thin hours of early morning that she'd imagined them, or misread them, or that a wind had come up and softened them into something else. But there they are, crisp as they were at three a.m., a single line crossing the yard from the tree line to the porch. Small. Patient-looking, somehow, which is a strange thing to think about footprints but she thinks it anyway.

She stands there until the percolator is done and then she pours a cup and stands there some more.

An animal, she decides again. Some animal she's not thinking of. She is not going to stand in her dead father's kitchen catastrophizing about footprints in the snow when she has a funeral home appointment in Iron Mountain at ten o'clock.

She drinks the coffee. It's not as good as she remembered. It's not bad either.

—

The drive to Iron Mountain takes forty-five minutes on roads that haven't been plowed since yesterday, and she follows a salt truck for the first twenty miles and doesn't mind. The landscape out here in daylight is both exactly what she expected and strange to her — the pines, the sky, the flat white fields that open up between stretches of woods, the occasional farmhouse set back from the road with a light in one window. She had forgotten how far apart everything is. In Milwaukee you can feel the city around you even when you can't see it. Out here you can't feel anything around you at all.

The funeral home is on the main street in Iron Mountain, a brick building with a discreet sign and a parking lot that's been carefully shoveled. The man who meets her is named Gerald, fifties, the kind of careful gentle that people in his profession either come by naturally or learn so thoroughly it becomes the same thing. He has a folder with her father's name on it. He offers her coffee and she says no thank you and they sit across a desk from each other and go through the folder.

He says her father's name the right way. That's the thing she notices first, sitting down. Hietala — the ie short and clipped, the way her father said it, the way the old men in the township say it, not Hy-tay-la the way the bank and the school and the State of Wisconsin have said it her whole life. People who learn the name learn it wrong. Gerald didn't learn it. He has it. She files that away without knowing where to put it and lets him keep talking.

Her father had, it turns out, pre-arranged most of this. Left a list of preferences — no viewing, Lutheran service, cremation after. The plot in the church cemetery had been in the family since his grandfather's time; that part at least required nothing from her. Gerald says all of this with a kind of respectful approval, as though her father had done everyone a favor by being so organized about his own death, which Aino supposes he had.

The folder, though. The folder is wrong, or not wrong, but old. The intake form on top is dated last week, crisp, the toner still sharp. But underneath it there's an older sheet, the paper gone soft and faintly yellow at the edge the way paper goes when it's sat in a drawer for years, and the typeface on it is the gray, slightly broken gray of a machine that needed a new ribbon a long time ago. She can't read it upside down and doesn't try. Pre-arranged, she reminds herself. He set this up years ago; of course there's old paper in here. People plan ahead up here. People who've watched their parents die in February plan very far ahead. That's all it is. She signs where Gerald indicates and doesn't look at the old sheet again, and not looking at it costs her something small she'd rather not price.

She picks up a small envelope of information about what happens next, what she'll need to bring to the service, who will contact whom. Gerald is very thorough. She is grateful for his thoroughness because it means she does not have to think.

She is in the parking lot by eleven-fifteen, earlier than she'd expected. She sits in the car for a moment. Iron Mountain feels enormous after Kivijärvi — a grocery store, a hardware store, two gas stations visible from where she's parked, people walking on cleared sidewalks. A woman in a red coat walks past with a dog. Ordinary world going on.

She drives to the grocery store and buys actual food. Soup, bread, eggs, a rotisserie chicken she's going to eat most of in the car. She eats the first leg in the parking lot before she's even started the engine and does not feel sorry for herself about it.

—

By the time she gets back to Kivijärvi it's early afternoon and the light is already thinning. She puts the groceries away in her father's kitchen, which is the strangest thing she's done in recent memory — opening his cabinets, learning where he kept things, finding that the soup bowls are exactly where they always were but the mugs have moved to the other side of the sink. Small adjustments. Nineteen years of small adjustments without her.

She makes herself eat a bowl of soup and then doesn't know what to do with herself.

The house is very quiet. She'd forgotten this kind of quiet — no traffic, no neighbors, no ambient hum of a city doing its business. Just the furnace and the wind starting up outside and, very faintly, the creak of the pines. She cleans the kitchen, which takes ten minutes. She brings in more wood from the stack on the porch for the fireplace her father apparently never used anymore, judging by the cold ash in it. She makes a fire. She sits in front of it in her father's chair and lasts about twenty minutes before the silence gets to be too much and she needs to be somewhere that has other people in it.

Hakala's is four minutes away.

—

The bar hasn't changed in any way she can detect. Same neon Leinenkugel's sign in the window. Same wood paneling the color of old tobacco. Same low ceiling, same eight or ten stools at the bar, same four booths along the wall. The smell is exactly what she remembered — beer and woodsmoke and the ghost of fifty years of cigarettes, back when you could smoke in a place like this.

She sits at the bar. There are three other people in the room: a man and a woman in the far booth who appear to be having a quiet argument, and an old man at the end of the bar who she clocks immediately and then looks away from.

Paavo Maki. He has to be eighty-something now. He was always thin and he is thinner, the way very old men get — papery, like the wind could rearrange him. He is drinking what looks like a brandy old fashioned and watching the television mounted in the corner, which is showing a hockey game with the sound off.

The bartender comes over. He's maybe thirty-five, dark-haired, a beard. She doesn't recognize him.

"What can I get you."

"Leinenkugel's," she says. "And a burger if you're still doing food."

"Kitchen closes at eight." He gets her the beer. "You just get in?"

"Last night. I'm Hank Hietala's daughter."

Something crosses his face — the particular expression people have in small towns when they're calibrating how much they knew the person and how sorry to be. "I heard about your dad," he says. "I'm sorry. He was in here a lot. Good guy."

"Yeah," she says. "He was."

She doesn't actually know if this is true. She knew her father for nineteen years and then she left, and the man who came into this bar regularly for the last nineteen years is someone she didn't know at all. But it seems like the right thing to say.

The burger comes. She eats half of it before she becomes aware that the old man at the end of the bar has turned on his stool and is looking at her. When she glances up he doesn't look away.

"You're Hank's girl," he says.

"Aino," she says.

"I know who you are." He says it without heat, just as a fact. "I found him, you know. Your father. I'm Paavo Maki."

"I know," she says. "The lawyer told me. Thank you for — " she's not sure how to finish that sentence. Thank you for checking on him. Thank you for going in when he didn't show up. Thank you for not leaving him there longer than you had to.

"He didn't suffer," Paavo says. "If that's something you want to know. It was fast."

"Thank you," she says again.

He nods. He picks up his drink and she thinks that's the end of it, that he'll turn back to the hockey game, and she goes back to her burger. But after a minute he speaks again.

"How long are you here for?"

"Through the weekend. Maybe a little longer. I need to get the house sorted out."

He nods slowly, looking at his glass. "You going to stay at the house?"

"That's the plan."

Another pause. The couple in the booth have stopped arguing and are now eating in silence, which seems worse somehow. The hockey game goes on soundlessly overhead.

"Don't go out on the lake," Paavo says.

She looks up. "Sorry?"

"The ice." He doesn't look at her. "This time of year. It's not safe."

She almost smiles. She's been coming up here since she was born, or came up here, and she knows what the ice is like in February. "Okay," she says.

"I mean it." Now he does look at her, and his eyes are sharper than she'd expected, sharper than an eighty-something man sitting alone with a brandy old fashioned at five in the afternoon has any right to be. "Stay off the lake."

She holds his gaze for a moment. He means it, that much is obvious — but it's a strange emphasis to put on it, a strange way to say what he's saying. Not the ice is thin or it hasn't frozen solid yet. Just: don't go out on the lake. Like the ice is the least of it.

"All right," she says.

He turns back to the hockey game.

She finishes her burger. She has another beer. The couple in the booth pays and leaves. The bartender wipes down the bar and puts some glasses away and she can tell he's the kind of bartender who's comfortable with silence, which she appreciates. At some point Paavo Maki finishes his brandy and puts on his coat and goes out without saying anything else, and the door lets in a cold blast when it opens and swings shut.

She sits with her second beer for a while after he's gone. The bar is empty now except for her and the bartender and the hockey game. She watches the players move around the ice without the sound, which makes it look like something she doesn't have a word for. Purposeful and silent and far away. The puck goes somewhere she can't see and they all turn at once and skate after it. From up here, with the sound off, they don't look like men playing a game. They look like something moving on ice for a reason she isn't allowed to know.

Stay off the lake.

She means to think about it the way she thinks about everything — turn it over, find the rational shape of it, set it down. He's old. Old men in towns like this carry a list of things that aren't safe and they hand the list to anyone who'll hold still. The ice is thin in February. People drown. He found her father; he's allowed to be careful about her. There. That's the explanation, sitting there reasonable on the bar in front of her, and it doesn't take.

It doesn't take because she can still feel where the second one landed. Not in her ear, where the first one had gone, the polite warning she'd half-smiled at. The second one — stay off the lake, his eyes finding hers, the brandy untouched in front of him — that one had gone in lower, under the sternum, and it's still there now, a small cold weight she can't talk herself out of, the way you can't talk yourself out of having swallowed something. Her body believed him before she'd decided whether to. Her body is still believing him.

She thinks about the footprints in the snow this morning. Small and evenly spaced and unhurried. Coming from the trees, crossing the open yard, ending at the back porch. Going to the porch. Not coming from it. She hadn't let herself finish that thought this morning and she doesn't finish it now, but it's in the room with her, the way the warning is in her ribs, and the two of them seem to know each other.

An animal, she thinks. The ice is thin. She pays her tab and tips well and goes out into the cold.

The drive back to the house takes four minutes. There are two ways. The short way drops down the hill and crosses at the bottom, the cross street that runs along the shore, the one she'd grown up taking because it was simply how you went — past the boat launch, past the dark water you could see in summer between the houses. The long way climbs back through town and comes around on the plowed gravel road through the pines, the high road, the one that never sees the lake at all.

At the bottom of the hill she comes to the cross street and her hands are already turning the wheel before she's chosen anything, the long way, up and around, away from the water. It adds maybe ninety seconds. The headlights swing across snowbanks and the black wall of pines and there's no lake out there to see in the dark anyway, no reason on earth not to take the short way, and she takes the long way, and she keeps her eyes on the gravel where it unrolls white in the high beams and does not once look toward the shore she can't see.

It's the faster way home. She tells herself that, getting the words ready in case she needs them.

She doesn't need them. There's no one in the car to say them to. There's only the small cold weight under her sternum, riding along, and the gravel, and the pines going by, and the turn for the house coming up where it always has.

She doesn't ask herself whether the fast way is true. She knows it isn't. She drives the long way and lets that be the thing she doesn't examine instead.

Chapter 3

The House

She gives herself the morning.

This is what she decides over her second cup of coffee, standing at the kitchen window again, the footprints still there in the yard, the sun finally clearing the pines and throwing long blue shadows across the snow. She has nowhere to be until the funeral tomorrow. The lawyer's office isn't until Monday. She has a whole day in this house with nothing required of her and she is going to use it the way she uses days off in Milwaukee, which is to say systematically, starting with the thing she least wants to do and working toward the thing she least minds.

What she least wants to do is go through her father's bedroom.

She does it anyway.

It doesn't take long. He was not a man who accumulated. The closet holds work clothes and two dress shirts still in their dry-cleaning bags and a pair of boots so old the leather has gone gray. The dresser holds what dressers hold. On the nightstand: a glass of water gone flat, a library book two weeks overdue about the iron mining history of the U.P., a pair of reading glasses with a cracked left lens that he had apparently been wearing anyway. She sets the glasses down carefully. She doesn't know why she's being careful with them. He's not going to need them.

She strips the bed and puts the sheets in the washer because it seems like the right thing to do and because it gives her something to do with her hands.

Then she goes back to the kitchen and makes more coffee and looks at the rest of the house and thinks about where to start. The living room is fine — dusty, sparse, the couch and the chair and the television and a bookshelf full of paperback westerns and U.P. history and one incongruous copy of a Larry McMurtry novel she doesn't remember him ever mentioning. The bathroom is fine. The small second bedroom that was hers is fine, sealed in 1987 the way childhood bedrooms are, the Indigo Girls poster she'd tacked up at sixteen still on the wall, slightly crooked, the same way it had been slightly crooked when she put it up.

That leaves the basement.

—

The door to the basement is off the kitchen, next to the back door. She's walked past it a dozen times since she arrived and not opened it. This is not intentional. She tells herself this is not intentional.

She opens it now.

The smell comes up first — sawdust and machine oil and something under that, something mineral, like wet stone. She finds the light switch and goes down.

The basement is two rooms. The front half is storage — the furnace, the water heater, shelves of paint cans and mason jars and the kind of accumulated household miscellany that takes forty years to build up and an afternoon to throw away. The back half is the workshop, and this is where her father spent his time.

She can tell because of the tools.

She knows tools. She grew up in a house where things got fixed rather than replaced, where her father could look at a broken thing and understand it the way some people understand language. But the tools on the pegboard back here are not tools she recognizes. Some of them she can name — a drawknife, a froe, a spokeshave, hand tools for working wood, old ones, older than her father probably. But others she cannot place. Long-handled things with heads she doesn't have names for. A series of chisels of graduated sizes hung in precise order. A mallet with a head of dense, dark wood, the handle worn smooth in a very specific way, the way handles get when the same hand has closed around them ten thousand times.

She picks up the mallet. It's heavier than it looks.

On the workbench: a half-finished piece she can't identify, clamped in a vise. Cedar, she thinks — the smell is right. Whatever it is, it's carved rather than built, shaped from a single piece of wood rather than assembled. She can see where the chisel marks are still rough, where he hadn't finished. He had been in the middle of something when he died.

She puts the mallet down. She looks at the walls.

He's covered them — not covered, that's the wrong word, decorated isn't right either. Marked them. Small carvings in the wood of the wall studs, in the exposed framing between the drywall panels. She has to get close to see them. Not letters, not exactly, though some of them remind her of something. Runes, she thinks, or what she imagines runes look like, though she doesn't actually know what runes look like.

She reaches out and traces one of them with her finger, following the cut of it, the way you'd read a word in a language you don't have. The wood is cold. Not cool, the way wood is in a basement in February — cold, the way the percolator was cold, the way a thing is cold when it has been holding the cold for a long time and means to go on holding it. She takes her hand back. The furnace is six feet away and running. She puts her finger against the stud beside it, the plain one, the uncarved one, and it's only wood, only the ordinary chill of the room. She tries the carving again. Cold. She tells herself the difference is in her, that a fingertip is a poor thermometer, that she's been down here twenty minutes in a basement and her hands have given up their heat unevenly the way hands do. It's a good explanation. She files it. It doesn't quite cover the part where she keeps her hand off the wall after that.

She takes out her phone and takes pictures of several of them.

She doesn't know why she takes the pictures. She just does.

On a shelf above the workbench, lined up with a precision that is out of keeping with the rest of the basement's organized clutter: forty-six leather-bound notebooks. She counts them without meaning to. The spines are unmarked. They're all the same size, the same worn brown leather, as though he bought them in bulk or made them himself. The one on the far right end looks newer than the others, the leather less cracked.

She reaches for it.

She stops.

She doesn't know why she stops. The notebooks are her father's and her father is dead and she is his heir and there is no reason she cannot open one. She picks up the newest one and opens it to the last entry.

January 8, it says at the top. Three days before he died.

Cold snap holding. Ice at 24 inches on the north end, softer toward the inlet. Quiet last night, quiet this morning. She came up around 2 but did not knock. Stayed below. I sat until four. Went back to the house.

Tired.

She reads it again.

She reads it a third time.

She came up around 2 but did not knock. The basement is cold. It was cold when she came down and she hadn't thought anything of it, basements are cold, but she's aware of it now, a flat even cold with no edge to it, and her breath is going out of her in a thin visible thread that hangs there longer than breath should before it lets go. She's holding the notebook with both hands. She makes herself notice that — both hands, the way you hold a thing you've decided not to drop — and she sets one hand flat on the workbench instead, on the wood, which is the same temperature as everything else down here and tells her nothing. She. Not a name. Not the woman from the bar, not a neighbor, not anyone he'd have written down as anyone. Just she, the way you write a thing you don't have to explain to yourself because you already know what it is. Stayed below. Aino's heart is going faster than the moment calls for and she stands there and lets it, because there is nothing in this entry, nothing at all, an old man's ice measurements and a note that he was tired three days before he died of being seventy-seven in his kitchen, and her body has read it as something else and gotten there ahead of her, the way it got there ahead of her at the bar, under the sternum, the small cold weight settling in beside the one already there.

She closes the notebook and puts it back exactly where it was. She goes upstairs. She sits at the kitchen table for a while without doing anything.

Then she gets up and puts her coat on and goes outside, around the side of the house, down the slope through the pines toward the lake.

—

She's not going to the sauna. She just wants to see the lake.

This is what she tells herself, walking down through the snow, the cold finding the gap at her collar, the pines closing overhead. She just wants to see it. She grew up on this lake. She swam in it every summer until she was eighteen. She knows this lake.

The sauna is visible before the lake is — the dark shape of it at the shoreline, built on stones at the water's edge, the lower half extending out over the ice. It's bigger than she remembered. Or she remembered it wrong. It has the look of a thing added to over many years, the roofline not quite consistent, a newer section visible on the east side where the wood is a slightly different color even under the weathering. Her great-grandfather built the first version of it. Her grandfather expanded it. Her father replaced the interior the last summer she was home, and she knows this because she had helped him carry cedar planks down the hill before everything went wrong.

She stops at the tree line. She does not go to the sauna.

The lake is frozen solid, or looks it — flat white stretching out to the far shore, the pines on the other side a dark line against the pale sky. A couple of fish houses are visible in the middle distance, other people's, small dark rectangles on the white. The ice is quiet. No cracking, no groaning, just the wind moving across the surface and lifting a fine spray of snow.

The wind is at her back. It comes down the slope behind her, through the pines, and it should carry out across the lake and away, the way wind does over open ice, and it doesn't. It dies at the shoreline. She can feel it pushing at her shoulders and she can see, twenty feet out, where it stops — the snow lifting and turning and settling, lifting and settling, going nowhere, as though the wind has reached the edge of something and can go no further onto it. The far shore is a mile off and perfectly still. Nothing moves out there. But it isn't the stillness of water frozen and left alone. It's the stillness of a surface that has turned its attention shoreward, that has gone quiet the way a thing goes quiet when it knows it is being looked at and is looking back. She has the clear and stupid sense that if she takes one step down onto the ice the whole white surface of it will know.

She doesn't take the step. She tells herself she doesn't take it because the ice along the shore is rotten this time of year, undercut where the inlet runs, and she has better sense than to test it in city boots with no one knowing where she is. It's a good reason. It's even true. It is not why her feet have stopped where the snow ends.

She stands there for a few minutes. The cold is working its way through her coat now, finding her shoulders, her ears.

There is nothing wrong with the lake. It is a lake in February. It is exactly what a lake in February looks like. She says it to herself in those words, plainly, the way you read a label aloud to settle an argument, and the lake goes on attending to her, patient, the snow lifting and settling at the edge of it, and does not agree.

She goes back to the house.

—

That evening she finds the photograph.

She's been going through the living room more carefully, looking for documents her father might have filed in random places — the lawyer had mentioned a deed, an insurance policy, a few things she should locate before Monday. She's found nothing useful, only the usual drawer-junk of a man who lived alone: takeout menus for restaurants in Iron Mountain, a hardware store loyalty card, a pocket knife, batteries of every size, a small spiral notepad with a grocery list in his handwriting that she cannot throw away and so sets on the kitchen counter with the reading glasses.

The photograph is on the wall in the hallway, between the bathroom and the bedroom door. She's walked past it three times without looking at it. She looks at it now.

Three men standing on a frozen lake in front of a sauna. The sauna in the photograph is smaller than the one at the shore, and different — older, cruder, not the same structure at all really, though the placement is the same, the same stones at the water's edge. The men are wearing the heavy work clothes of an earlier time. The photograph is black and white and slightly overexposed and whoever took it was standing on the ice looking toward the shore.

The man on the left is tall and broad-shouldered and looks at the camera with an expression she can't read. The man in the middle is shorter, heavier, and is not looking at the camera at all — he's looking at something to his left, off-frame. The figure on the right is a boy.

She looks closer. Her father. Fourteen years old maybe, slight in his winter coat, his face not yet settled into the one she knew. But her father without question — the jaw, the way he stands, slightly turned as though he was about to walk away when someone called him back for the picture.

The tall man on the left, then. Her grandfather Toivo. And the man in the middle — her great-grandfather Eino, she supposes, though she has never seen a photograph of Eino and cannot say for certain.

Three generations of Hietala men standing in front of the sauna on the ice. One of them still a boy.

She thinks about the notebook entry. She came up around 2 but did not knock. Stayed below.

She thinks about the mallet in the basement, worn smooth by ten thousand uses.

She thinks about Paavo Maki at the bar last night, the sharpness in his eyes. Stay off the lake.

She takes the photograph off the wall. She turns it over. On the back, in a handwriting she doesn't recognize, someone has written three names and a date.

Eino. Toivo. Henrik. February 1962.

February 1962. Her grandfather Toivo drowned in that lake four months later, in June of 1962, when her father was fourteen years old.

She hangs the photograph back on the wall exactly as she found it.

She goes to bed early. She lies in the dark under her grandmother's quilt and listens to the house.

The funeral is tomorrow.

She thinks about her father at fourteen, standing on the ice in February, being called back for a picture he didn't particularly want to be in. The way he's turned slightly, like he was already leaving. She knows that posture. She has seen it on herself in photographs her whole adult life and never once thought about where she got it from.

Outside, the lake is quiet. The house is quiet. Eventually, somewhere in the small hours, she sleeps.

Chapter 4

The Funeral

The church is on the main street, same as it always was, a white clapboard building with a modest steeple and a parking lot that was full when she was a girl and is half-empty now. She gets there early and sits in the Subaru for a few minutes watching people arrive. Old people, mostly. A few she recognizes and most she doesn't, which makes sense — she left at nineteen and the town has been slowly replacing itself with older versions of itself ever since.

The pastor is young. This surprises her, though it shouldn't. She doesn't know what she expected — the pastor from her childhood was a barrel-chested Norwegian man named Virtanen who had seemed ancient to her at eight years old and is presumably actually ancient now or dead. This pastor is maybe thirty-five, slight, with the careful manner of someone who has learned to move through other people's grief without disturbing it too much. He shakes her hand at the door and tells her he is sorry for her loss and that her father was a faithful member of the congregation. She thanks him. She doesn't ask how well he knew him because she can already tell the answer is not very.

Inside, the church smells the way Lutheran churches smell — wood polish and candle wax and the particular cold of a stone-floored room that never quite warms up no matter how hard the furnace works. The light comes in thin and gray through the plain windows; there is no stained glass here, never was, just clear panes and white walls and a wooden cross at the front, the Finnish Lutheran taste for plainness that her grandmother used to call honest. She sits in the front pew. The pew is the same hard varnished oak it always was, the seat too shallow and the back too straight, built by people who did not believe comfort was the point. The hymnal in the rack in front of her is the green one, the same green one. Behind her she can hear people settling, the soft percussion of coats and whispers and the cough of an old man two rows back. She is alone in the front pew, which she had not thought about until she is sitting in it — that there is no one to sit beside her, no family left to fill the rest of the bench. She doesn't turn around.

The service is not long. The pastor speaks about her father in the general terms of a man working from notes rather than memory — faithful, hardworking, a member of this community for his entire life. He reads from Psalms. The congregation sings a hymn she knows without having thought about it in twenty years, the words coming back to her the way things come back from somewhere below the place where you stopped looking for them. Her voice comes out thin and she stops after the first verse and just listens to the old people around her sing it the way people sing hymns they have been singing since childhood, with a kind of low, unselfconscious authority.

Her father's casket is closed. This was his preference, per the list he left Gerald. She finds she is glad of it. She is not sure she could have looked at him in there and then stood up and sung a hymn.

She does not cry during the service. She had thought she might, finally, here — that the church and the hymn and the closed casket would be the thing that broke it loose. But she sits through all of it dry-eyed and a little numb and when it's over she stands and shakes the pastor's hand again and accepts condolences from people whose names she has to read off their expressions rather than actually remember.

Paavo Maki is there, near the back. He catches her eye across the room and nods once and she nods back. He doesn't come over.

—

The reception is in the church basement, the same basement she ate a hundred after-service lunches in as a girl. The stairs down to it are the same stairs, narrow and steep, the handrail worn pale at the turn, and her feet find the spacing of them without consulting her, the way they used to when she came down too fast in patent shoes and her grandmother told her to slow down. The room at the bottom hasn't changed in any way that matters. The long folding tables. The stacking metal chairs that are always slightly cold to sit on. The percolator the size of a small engine on the counter, the same one or its identical twin, breathing its steady industrial gurgle into the room. The low ceiling with the foam tiles and the fluorescent light that makes everyone look like they have been up all night, which some of them have. The smell of coffee and something baked and, under it, the church-basement smell itself, which is its own thing — floor wax and old hymnals and the cool of a foundation that holds the cold the way the percolator in her father's kitchen holds it, the way everything up here seems to hold it.

The women of the congregation have provided, the way they do. There is a sheet cake from the IGA with white frosting and a blue piped border. There are two coffee cakes under foil, and a glass dish of bar cookies, and a crockpot of meatballs plugged in at the end of the table with a hand-lettered card that says SWEDISH propped against it. And there is a plate of something she doesn't recognize until she gets closer and realizes it is pulla — the Finnish cardamom bread, braided in three strands and brushed with egg and scattered with pearl sugar, golden and split a little along the seams the way it splits when the braid is good. So specific to her grandmother's kitchen, to a particular Sunday smell of it, that she has to look away from it for a moment. When she looks back she takes a piece, because not taking one would be its own kind of statement, and she eats it standing up, and it is exactly right, the cardamom blooming the way it did when she was six, and it undoes her a little more than the hymn did. She doesn't let it show. She wipes the sugar off her fingers with a paper napkin and folds the napkin into a square and keeps it closed in her fist.

She gets coffee in a styrofoam cup and stands near the wall and lets people come to her, which they do, in the patient procession of small-town funerals. The first ones she half-knows. A heavyset man who shakes her hand and says he worked the second shift with Hank at the mine for eleven years, before it closed, and she says thank you and cannot find his name though she's fairly sure she once knew it. A couple her father's age whose faces she has clearly seen at this church a thousand mornings and whose names are simply gone. She has gotten good, over three days, at reading the answer off the face — the way they lead with the name when they know she won't have it, so she can borrow it back from them in the next sentence. She does this several times. She thanks people for coming. She accepts the casseroles that are pressed on her, the foil pans she will not eat and cannot refuse, and stacks them on the end of the table by the meatballs.

Then there are the women. Three or four of them, the old ones, the ones in the good black, who do not so much come to her as gather near her in a loose orbit and watch her with frank, unembarrassed attention. They knew Aili. They say so — I knew your grandmother, each of them, like a credential — and they tell her things in the patient declarative way of people who have decided she ought to have them. That Aili made the best pulla in the parish and would never give the recipe straight, always left a step out so yours came out wrong. That she sang alto. That she could clean a fish faster than any man and did it on the back step with a cigarette in her mouth. They tell her she has Aili's eyes. They are kind to her, and they are curious about her, in the way of people who have been watching a situation develop for nineteen years and are finally getting to see how it ends — the daughter who left, come back at last, and only for this. She doesn't begrudge them the curiosity. She's curious herself. She answers their questions about Milwaukee and her work and whether she's staying long with the same short answers she's been giving since she arrived, and they take the shortness for grief, which is generous of them, and not entirely wrong.

She notices the old woman before the old woman comes to her. Has noticed her, in fact, for a while — across the basement, near the coffee table, a small sharp-featured woman in her mid-eighties at least, dressed in the kind of black that suggests she has attended a great many funerals and keeps appropriate clothing ready. She has not joined the orbit of the other women. She has stood a little apart from them with a cup of coffee in one hand and she has been watching Aino steadily, without pretense, the way the others watched but more so, longer, without the courtesy of looking away when Aino's eyes happen across her. The first time their eyes meet the woman does not nod or smile or do any of the things people do to dissolve a caught look. She simply holds it. Then she lets it go and watches the room and waits, and Aino feels the held look go on sitting on her like a hand laid flat between the shoulder blades, even after it has stopped.

She comes to her last, after most of the others have drifted away in twos and threes, coats on, heading out into the cold. She comes across the basement slowly but without hesitation, the coffee cup steady in her hand, and she is looking at Aino the way people look at something they've been waiting to see for a long time.

She stops in front of her and takes Aino's free hand in both of hers. Her grip is firm.

"Aino," she says.

"Yes," Aino says. "Thank you for coming. I'm sorry, I don't —"

"Helmi Korhonen." The woman says it as though the name should mean something, and it does, distantly — she has a memory of this name, her grandmother saying it, her father saying it once, a long time ago. "I knew your grandmother Aili. We came over the same year, 1956. Different boats." A faint smile. "She was my closest friend for forty years."

"I didn't know that," Aino says. "I'm sorry. She died when I was fifteen."

"I know when she died." Helmi says it without unkindness, just as a fact. She is still holding Aino's hand. "You look like her. Around the eyes."

"People have said that."

Helmi looks at her for a moment with an expression Aino can't quite read — not quite sorrow, not quite relief, something between the two. Then she says, in Finnish: "Sinä tulit takaisin."

Aino understands it without having to translate it. You came back.

"Kyllä," she says. Yes. The word comes out before she decides to say it, in Finnish before she decides to use Finnish.

Something shifts in Helmi's expression. She glances around the basement — the last few people pulling on coats, the pastor stacking chairs at the far end, nobody within easy earshot. She looks back at Aino and lowers her voice.

"Anna sen mennä," she says. Let it go.

Aino waits.

"The sauna. Whatever is in it, whatever he kept in it. Lock the door and leave it. Let it rot. Let the woods take it back in fifty years if they want it." Helmi's grip on her hand tightens slightly. "Your father did enough. His father did enough. It has been enough."

"I don't know what you mean," Aino says, which is not entirely true.

"You will." Helmi releases her hand. She picks up her coffee cup with the deliberateness of a woman who has said what she came to say and is finished. "Your grandmother knew. She never went near it either. That was the right choice." She pauses. "Aili was a smart woman. Smarter than the men she married into."

Aino thinks about the photograph. Her grandfather Toivo on the ice, four months before he drowned. Her grandmother Aili, who knew, who never went near the sauna, who lived another forty years after her husband went into the lake.

"What happened to my grandfather," she says. "Toivo. Was it an accident?"

Helmi looks at her for a long moment.

"He was a good man," she says finally. "He was not a strong one." She sets her coffee cup down on the nearest table. She picks up her coat from the back of a chair. "Go home, Aino. Sell the house. Leave the sauna locked and let the woods have it eventually. Go back to your life."

She buttons her coat with the careful attention of the very old and walks toward the stairs without looking back.

Aino stands in the emptying basement with her cup of cold coffee and watches her go.

—

She is one of the last to leave. She helps stack the remaining chairs because it gives her something to do with her hands and because the pastor looks tired and grateful when she offers. He asks if she's all right. She says yes. He says her father spoke of her sometimes. She asks what he said. The pastor smiles carefully and says that he was proud of her, that he mentioned her work, that he had a photograph of her on the refrigerator.

She drives home in the blue late-afternoon light. She goes in through the back door and stands in the kitchen and looks at the refrigerator.

There is a photograph on it, held by a magnet from a hardware store in Iron Mountain. A snapshot, slightly faded, the color of photographs from the mid-nineties. A girl of about eight in a swimsuit standing on a dock, squinting into the sun, holding up a fish with both hands and grinning the way children grin when they've done something they're proud of.

The lake is visible behind her. The sauna is at the edge of the frame.

She doesn't remember the day. She doesn't remember the fish. But that's her face, and that's this lake, and her father had this on his refrigerator when he died and apparently for a long time before that.

She takes it down and sits at the kitchen table and holds it for a while.

Outside the kitchen window the yard is going dark, the pines black against the last light. The snow is still unbroken except for the footprints, which have been there for two days now, and which she has stopped letting herself think about, mostly.

She puts the photograph in her coat pocket.

She makes dinner. She eats it. She washes the dishes and dries them and puts them away. Before she goes to bed she takes the photograph out of her coat pocket and props it against the percolator on the counter, where her father would have seen it every morning.

Chapter 5

What He Left Her

The lawyer's office is above the hardware store on the main street, up a narrow staircase that smells like the hardware store below it — machine oil and rubber and something metallic. The nameplate on the door says ERNEST AUTIO, ATTORNEY AT LAW. She knocks and a voice tells her to come in.

Ernest Autio is in his late sixties, compact and careful-looking, with the kind of deliberate stillness that suggests he has spent a long career letting other people fill silences. He has a white mustache and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and a desk covered in the organized chaos of a man who knows exactly where everything is. He stands when she comes in and shakes her hand and says he is sorry about her father.

"He was a client for thirty years," he says. "And a friend, I think, though he wasn't a man who used that word easily."

"No," she says. "He wasn't."

He gestures to the chair across from his desk and she sits. He opens a folder.

The will is not complicated. The house and land to Aino, free and clear. The savings account — he names a figure, thirty-one thousand and change — to Aino. Personal effects to Aino. A small bequest to the Lutheran church, two hundred dollars, which makes her think of the pastor stacking chairs and feel something she doesn't examine.

Ernest reads all of this in the measured tone of a man who has read a great many wills and understands that the people sitting across from him are not really listening to the legal language, they are listening for their name and for the shape of what they're being left. She listens for both.

Then he turns a page.

"There is one additional item," he says. "Your father was specific about how it should be handled, and he asked me to present it separately." He looks at her over his reading glasses. "The sauna and all its contents are left to you, with a condition attached. The estate cannot close — the deed cannot transfer, the accounts cannot be released — until you have, and I'm quoting your father here, made a decision about the sauna and acted on it. His language, not mine."

She looks at him. "What does that mean, legally?"

"It means the estate stays open until I'm satisfied the condition has been met. Your father trusted me to use judgment about what constitutes acting on it." Ernest takes his glasses off his forehead and sets them on the desk. "I'll be honest with you, Aino. He came in and revised this will eight months ago. He was very deliberate about this language. I asked him what he meant by it and he said I'd know it when I saw it." A pause. "I've been a lawyer for forty years. I've had stranger instructions."

She thinks about the ledgers in the basement. The forty-six of them, lined up in order.

"There's also an envelope," Ernest says. He opens his desk drawer and produces it — a standard white envelope, sealed, her name written on the front in her father's handwriting. Careful handwriting, the handwriting of a man who did not write often and took it seriously when he did. "He left this with me two years ago. He said to give it to you in person, not to mail it, not to leave it at the house."

She takes the envelope. It's light. Not much inside.

"Do you want a few minutes?" Ernest asks.

"No," she says. "I'll read it at home."

She puts it in her coat pocket, next to the photograph.

Ernest goes through the remaining paperwork — what she needs to sign, what happens next, the timeline for probate. She signs where he indicates. She asks a few questions about the deed and the accounts, the kind of questions her job has taught her to ask, and he answers them precisely and without condescension, which she appreciates.

At the door he shakes her hand again.

"Your father talked about you," he says. "He was proud of you. I want you to know that, since I'm not sure who else would tell you."

She thanks him. She goes down the stairs and out into the February cold.

—

She does not open the envelope right away.

She sits in the Subaru in the parking lot behind the hardware store with the engine running and the heat going and the envelope on the passenger seat where she set it when she got in. The lot is small, gravel and packed snow, a loading dock at the back of the store with a dumpster and a stack of wooden pallets going gray under a tarp. A man in a Carhartt coat carries a length of pipe out to a truck and goes back in. The light is the flat midday light of February up here, no shadows in it, the kind of light that makes everything look like a photograph of itself.

She turns the heat down a notch. She checks the gas gauge, which is fine. She watches the man come out again with another length of pipe and load it and drive away. She tells herself she is letting the car warm up, which is not true; it is warm. She tells herself she will read it at home, which is what she told Ernest, and then she does not put the car in gear.

Her hands are on the wheel. They are her mother's hands, people used to say, though she has no memory of her mother's hands to check it against. The knuckles are a little chapped from the cold. There is a paper cut on the side of one thumb from the probate forms that she had not noticed until now.

The envelope is light. She knew that already, standing in the office. Light enough that there is almost nothing in it, which is somehow worse than if it had been thick, because a thick envelope is a man explaining himself and a thin one is a man who has decided exactly how much he is going to say.

She picks it up. She holds it for a while longer. Then she works a finger under the flap and opens it.

Inside: a key, small and old, the kind that fits a padlock rather than a door. A folded piece of paper, which is a hand-drawn map of Lake Kivijärvi and the surrounding property, her father's land marked with a simple rectangle for the house and a smaller shape at the shoreline for the sauna. An X on the lake, maybe thirty feet out from the sauna — she can't tell if it marks something on the surface or something below it.

And a note. One page, in his handwriting.

Aino —

I am sorry I did not tell you this myself. I tried several times. I could not find the words and then I ran out of time, which is what happens when you wait too long for the right moment.

The key is for the lockbox under the bench in the sauna. You will need the small crowbar in the basement workshop, third peg from the left, to get the bench up. Inside the lockbox is what you need to understand what I have been doing and what my father did before me and his father before him.

Sit in the sauna for a while before you open the floor. Light the stove. Let it get warm. I know this sounds strange. It matters.

Watch the water through the opening when you do open it. Do not put your hand in. Do not lean over it. Watch, and wait, and you will understand.

I am sorry about the summer you were nineteen. I was afraid for you and I handled it wrong. I have thought about it many times.

The thing in the water is not evil. I need you to know that. It is something that was wronged a long time ago by people in our family and it has been waiting a long time for someone to do something other than watch it. I did not know what to do. Maybe you will.

I love you. I should have said so more.

Dad

She reads it twice. She folds it carefully and puts it back in the envelope with the key and the map. She sits in the parking lot with the envelope in her lap.

The thing in the water is not evil.

She thinks about the lake at age seven.

She was lying on her stomach on the end of the dock with her arm in the water to the elbow, the way you do at that age, trailing your fingers and watching the light come apart around them. The water was cold the way it is even in July up here, cold a few inches down. She remembers the boards warm against her belly and the cold of the water and the smell of the lake, which is its own smell, weed and stone and something underneath that. And she remembers the hand. Coming up through the green toward hers. Pale, and slower than a hand should move, and reaching, the fingers spread.

She did not have time to be afraid of it. That came later. What came first was her father.

She did not hear him on the hill. She heard him when he was already most of the way down it, the heavy fast crash of a grown man running on uneven ground, and then he had her — not gently, not the way he picked her up other times, but by the back of the swimsuit and one arm, hard enough that it left marks, hauling her up and back off the dock so fast her chin caught the board and her teeth came together on her tongue and she tasted blood. He held her against him with his heart going like something trying to get out. She could feel it through his shirt. He was looking at the water. Not at her, at the water, at the exact place her hand had been, and his face was a face she had never seen on him, before or after, the face of a man looking at a thing he already knew was there.

Just a reflection, he said, after. Just the light. There's nothing in the lake, Aino. And his voice was steady when he said it, which is how she knew, even at seven, that it was not true, because his voice was steady and his hands were not.

She believed him anyway. For thirty years she believed him.

She starts the Subaru. She drives back to the house.

She does not go to the sauna that afternoon. She makes coffee and sits at the kitchen table and looks at the photograph of herself on the dock, eight years old and squinting and holding up the fish, the lake behind her and the sauna at the edge of the frame, and she thinks about how fast he came down that hill.

He had not gone looking. He had known where to look.

Chapter 6

Night Two

She doesn't go to the sauna that day, or the next.

She tells herself she is being practical. There are things to do — the house to go through, decisions to make about furniture and clothes and the accumulated objects of a life lived in one place for forty years. She fills three garbage bags with things that are simply garbage, worn-out things, expired things, things with no use and no meaning. She fills two boxes with things she thinks might matter — old photographs, her father's watch, a hand-carved wooden box she finds in the bedroom closet that she can't bring herself to open yet.

She calls her office. She extends her leave through the end of the month. Her supervisor says of course, take the time you need, and she can hear in his voice both genuine sympathy and the faint stress of a man recalculating his caseload.

She calls her mother in Phoenix. Lila answers on the second ring, which means she was expecting the call or dreading it, probably both. They talk for twenty minutes in the way they always talk — on the surface of things, in the register of logistics and weather and how are you doing, fine. Lila says she's sorry about Hank. She says it the way she says most things about Aino's father, with a care that suggests she is aware of how many ways she could say it wrong. Aino tells her the funeral was small but good. Tells her about the church, the pulla in the basement, the pastor who didn't know him well. She does not tell her about Helmi Korhonen or the envelope or the note.

At the end of the call Lila says: "Are you all right up there? Alone?"

"I'm fine," Aino says. "It's quiet."

"It was always quiet," her mother says, and something in her voice when she says it makes Aino want to ask a question she doesn't know how to form yet. But Lila has already moved on, asking about the drive back, when she's thinking of leaving, and the moment closes.

She cooks. She reads one of the paperback westerns from her father's shelf, which is not very good but requires nothing from her. She goes to bed at a reasonable hour and lies in the dark and listens.

The sound comes back on the third night.

—

She's been asleep — actually asleep, deeper than she's managed since she arrived — when it pulls her up. The same sound as before, rhythmic and faint, coming from the direction of the lake. She lies still and listens and it goes on longer this time, five minutes maybe, before it stops.

She does not get up. She tells herself there is nothing to get up for. The quilt is warm. The room is cold the way the room is always cold, the furnace cycling on and off below her, and she lies under her grandmother's stitching and stares at the ceiling she stared at as a child and waits for sleep to come back.

It does, a little. She surfaces again sometime later — she doesn't reach for her phone to check, doesn't want the number — and lies there listening to nothing, to the house, to the small settling sounds it makes that mean nothing, that she has spent a week learning to tell from the sounds that mean something. There is a difference. She knows that now in a way she didn't a week ago, and she is not sure when she learned it.

The third time she comes up the sound is going again. She lies still through all of it this time. She counts the way she counted in the car, the count starting on its own the way it did on the highway, except that this time it does not resolve — she loses it somewhere past a hundred, and it is still going when she gives up, and then it stops between one breath and the next, the way it stopped before, like a thing that has decided it is done rather than a thing that has run out.

She gets up. She gets up the way you get up when you have already lost the argument with yourself, knowing she will not sleep until she has looked, knowing also that looking has not once made anything better. She crosses the cold floor in her socks. She does not turn on a light.

She goes to the kitchen window.

The yard is darker tonight — clouds over the moon, the snow a flat gray rather than the bright blue-white of the first night. The footprints are still there, the original ones, more than a week old now and softened by a light snowfall two days ago, the edges gone blurry. Around them, fresh ones.

She leans closer to the glass.

The fresh prints don't follow the same path as the old ones. These come from a different direction — not from the tree line at the back of the property but from the side, from the slope that leads down toward the lake. Single file, same size as before. They cross the yard at an angle and go to the back porch and stop. Or they almost stop. She follows them with her eyes and sees that one more print continues past the porch — just one, pressed into the snow right at the edge of the porch steps, pointed toward the back door.

Pointed toward where she is standing.

She steps back from the window. She is aware of her own breathing.

She stands in the dark kitchen for a long time. The house is completely still. Whatever made those prints is not on the porch now — there's no sound, no movement, the door is locked. She checked the locks before bed. She checks them every night.

After a while she turns on the kitchen light. The yard looks ordinary under the artificial light, just snow and trees and the dark beyond. She fills a glass of water at the sink and drinks it standing up and tells herself to think clearly.

The prints are small. No animal she can think of makes prints that shape and that size. She got her phone and she looked it up two nights ago — she'd spent an hour with her phone going through every animal in the U.P., comparing paw prints and hoof prints and claw marks, and nothing matched. She had put the phone away and not looked it up again.

She goes back to bed. She does not sleep.

In the morning she gets up early and goes outside before she loses her nerve. She does not bother with coffee first. She pulls on her coat and her boots over bare feet and opens the back door and the cold comes in at her like a hand on the chest.

She has forgotten her gloves. She is halfway across the porch before she registers it, her hands already aching at the knuckles, and she does not go back for them. Going back is its own kind of admitting.

She crouches beside the fresh prints. The snow gives under her knee, slow and then all at once, the crust breaking and her knee sinking until the cold of it soaks through the denim and into the bone. She doesn't shift off it. The early light is flat and gray across the yard, no shadows in it, the kind of light that comes at every hour up here in winter, and it does the prints no favors and tells her no lies.

She looks at them closely. She has looked at them from the window, from above, from inside, and it is not the same as this, down at their level with her bare hand a few inches from the nearest one and the cold coming up off the snow into her face. There is no claw at the front. No splay, no dewclaw, no drag of a tail or a heel. No fur has touched the edges. The print is small and the print is deliberate and it presses straight down and lifts straight up, the way a thing steps when it has somewhere to be and means to get there. She holds her hand over it, not into it, the way her father wrote do not put your hand in about a thing he meant something else by entirely, and her hand is bigger than the print and that is somehow the worst of it.

They are not an animal's. She knew this but she needed to see it again in daylight, down close, with the cold in her knee and her hands going stiff, where she could not tell herself it was the window or the dark or the angle.

She straightens up. Her knee is wet and her hands won't quite close. She looks at the slope down toward the lake, the direction the prints came from. The birches along the slope are bare. The pines beyond are still. Nothing moves, and nothing has moved, and the not-moving is exactly what it was the first night and the night her father came down that same slope at a dead run.

She goes back inside and makes coffee and stands at the counter next to the percolator and looks at the photograph of herself at eight years old. She drinks the coffee with both hands around the mug because her hands are still cold, the warmth of it going into her palms before it goes anywhere else.

The envelope is on the kitchen table. It has been on the kitchen table for the better part of a week, since the parking lot behind the hardware store, since she read the note twice and folded it and told herself she would deal with it. She has eaten three meals a day at that table with the envelope a foot from her plate. She has moved it once, to wipe the table, and set it back. She has not opened it again.

She sits down across from it. She does not pick it up yet.

She could leave. That is the thing she lets herself think, sitting there with the coffee going cold. She could fill the rest of the boxes and load the Subaru and lock the house and drive south, and the estate would stay open and Ernest Autio would wait, and her father's condition would go unmet, and some lawyer years from now would do whatever lawyers do with a house no one came back for. She could be in Watersmeet by noon and out of the U.P. by dark and home — the apartment, the radiator, the dog commercials — by the time the sound started again tonight for someone who was no longer here to hear it. No one would make her stay. The prints would go on being made in a yard no one was standing at the window of.

She thinks about how fast he came down that hill.

That is the weight, the one that has been settling in under the sternum since the parking lot, beside the older one she carried up here, the small cold one that is not grief and is not fear and is the thing that comes when you understand that a door has been left open specifically for you and that closing it is also a choice you will have to live with. He left it open on purpose. He waited two years to hand her the envelope and eight months to write the condition into the will and forty-six ledgers' worth of his life arranging the matter so that she would have to decide, and the cruelty of it and the trust of it are the same thing and she cannot get them apart.

She reaches across the table and picks up the envelope. It is still light. She works a finger under the flap, though the flap is already open, and takes out the key — small and old, for a padlock and not a door — and leaves the map and the note inside. She does not need the map. She knows where the sauna is. She has known where the sauna is her whole life.

She puts on her coat.

She goes to the sauna.

Chapter 7

The Sauna

The path down to the lake is through the birches first and then the pines, a slope of maybe a hundred feet, and someone — her father, presumably, over many years — has kept it clear enough to walk. The snow is packed down in a rough trail between the trees. She follows it.

The sauna comes into view before the lake does. She'd seen it from the tree line over a week ago and turned back. Up close it's different. Larger than she'd registered from a distance, the structure extending out over the frozen surface of the lake on heavy timber pilings, the lower walls dark with age and moisture, the upper walls newer, the cedar her father put in the last summer she was home, weathered now but still holding its color. Three distinct sections visible from outside — the changing room, the washing room, the sauna proper — each addition a slightly different vintage of construction, the roofline stepping up and back like something grown rather than built.

The door to the changing room faces the shore. There is a hasp on it with a padlock.

She fits the key. The padlock opens.

Inside, the changing room is small — bench along one wall, hooks above it, a wooden mat on the floor. A pair of her father's rubber-soled sandals are under the bench, the kind you wear in a sauna, worn down at the heel. She leaves them where they are. On the hooks: a towel that has been there long enough to go stiff, a canvas bag she doesn't look inside, a birch whisk bundle so dry it crumbles when she touches it.

The door to the washing room. The door to the sauna proper.

She opens that one last.

The heat is gone, obviously — hasn't been a fire in the stove since before her father died, two weeks now, and the temperature in here is the same as outside, ten degrees maybe, the cold dense and close in the small space. The stove is in the corner, a cast-iron kiuas packed with stones on top, the firebox below. Beside it a metal bucket and a long-handled ladle for the löyly. On the shelf above the stove: a clay pot, a small glass bottle with something dark in it, a folded piece of cloth. Against the opposite wall the lauteet, the tiered bench platform, two levels, the lower level where you sit at lower heat and the upper where it gets serious.

She looks at it all. She looks at the walls.

The carvings are here too, the same as in the basement — marks cut into the wood of the wall studs, into the frame around the door, into the underside of the upper bench. More of them than in the basement. Some she recognizes from her photographs of the basement walls and some she doesn't. They cover every surface at a certain height, a line of them running around the room like a border, and below that line the walls are plain and above that line they are plain, just that one band of marks all the way around.

Her father's handwriting in the ledger: Sat until four. Went back to the house.

She looks at the kiuas.

Her father's note: Light the stove. Let it get warm. I know this sounds strange. It matters.

She looks around for wood. There's a box beside the stove, she'd missed it in the dimness — a wooden crate, and in it birch logs split to the right size, dry and ready, as though her father had prepared them in advance. Which he had, she realizes. He had stocked the wood before he died. He'd known someone would be coming in here after him.

She kneels and opens the firebox. She builds a fire the way her father taught her when she was six — paper at the bottom, kindling crossed over it, the first log on last, not too tight. She has matches in her coat pocket. She lights it.

She sits on the lower bench with her coat on and waits for it to take.

—

It takes a long time for the sauna to warm, as it always does — you can't hurry a kiuas, her father used to say, it heats at its own pace and if you try to rush it you'll crack the stones. She sits on the bench and watches the fire through the small vent in the firebox door and listens to the wood shift and catch. The cold retreats slowly. After half an hour she can feel the heat beginning to build in the stones above the firebox, a dry radiant warmth that is different from any other heat she knows.

She takes her coat off.

She has not been in a sauna in twenty years. She'd forgotten what it does to your body, the particular permission of it — the way the heat insists on your attention, pulls you out of your head and into your skin. She sits on the lower bench and breathes the smell of hot cedar and old wood and something underneath that, something mineral, and she watches the steam begin to rise from the stones when she ladles water over them, the löyly filling the upper half of the room and making the carved marks on the walls briefly visible and then obscuring them.

She moves to the upper bench. It's hot up here. She can feel it on her scalp, the back of her hands, the tops of her ears. She sits with it.

She thinks about her father sitting here. Not once but hundreds of times, thousands maybe, over sixty years of coming down to this sauna and lighting the stove and sitting in exactly this heat. What he thought about. Whether he prayed. Whether it got easier or harder with time.

She thinks about the note. The thing in the water is not evil.

She thinks about Helmi Korhonen. He was a good man. He was not a strong one.

After a while she climbs down from the upper bench and kneels in front of the lower one. She runs her hand along the underside of the platform. The wood is smooth and dry from decades of heat. She finds the edge — a seam, not obvious, cut with precision so it blends with the plank lines — and follows it with her fingers. A rectangular section, maybe two feet by three, set into the floor of the bench platform.

She goes out to the changing room and digs in the canvas bag she didn't look in earlier. Inside: the small crowbar her father mentioned in his note.

She comes back in. She works the crowbar into the seam.

The section lifts.

Below it: the lockbox, a heavy metal box bolted to the framing of the platform, a combination lock on the hasp. She stares at it for a moment. He hadn't mentioned a combination.

She tries his birthday. Nothing. She tries her birthday. Nothing. She tries her grandfather Toivo's, the date she knows the way you know the birthdays of the drowned, and the lock holds. She sits back on her heels. Then she tries her grandmother Aili's birthday, which she knows because it was two days before her own and they used to share a cake when she was small.

The lock opens.

Inside the lockbox: a notebook, older than the ones on the shelf in the basement, the leather dark and dry. And underneath it, wrapped in cloth, a key she doesn't recognize, larger than the one she used on the padlock, heavy, the kind of key that belongs to something she hasn't found yet.

She takes the notebook. She leaves the key for now.

She replaces the section of bench and stands up and goes to the hatch.

She knows the hatch is there — her father's note had told her, don't open the floor — but she doesn't know exactly where until she finds the rope handle set flush into the floor planks near the far wall, directly over the water. She stands over it for a moment.

She opens it.

The cold comes up immediately, a breath of it, the air from below the sauna where it extends over the lake. Through the opening she can see the ice — the sauna sits close enough to the surface that the ice is only a few feet below, pale gray in the dim light filtering through the gaps in the pilings.

She watches it. She waits.

For a long time nothing happens. The fire ticks in the kiuas. The steam drifts. She sits cross-legged on the floor beside the hatch and watches the ice through the opening and thinks about her father doing this same thing, week after week, year after year, and she starts to understand, viscerally, in her body rather than her mind, how long this has gone on. How long the family has been coming down here and sitting in this heat and watching this ice.

Then something moves below the surface.

Not on the ice — below it, in the water under it, a darkening, a shift, the way a cloud shadow moves across a field. She watches it. It is not a fish. It is not a current. It moves with a kind of deliberateness that she can feel rather than see, and she feels it in her chest before she fully processes it with her eyes, a pressure, a presence, something that knows she is here.

She does not put her hand in. She does not lean over.

She watches.

After a moment — she cannot say how long, time moves strangely in the heat — the darkness recedes. The ice is just ice again, pale and still. Nothing moves.

She closes the hatch.

She sits back on the lower bench with the old notebook in her lap and her heart going hard in her chest and the kiuas ticking down around her, and she thinks: so that is what he was watching.

She does not get up right away. The fire is past its height now and settling, the logs gone to a low orange seam in the firebox, and the heat in the stones is the long even kind that comes after the flames are done, the kind that will hold for another hour and then let go all at once. She sits in it. She lets the löyly thin out and does not ladle more. The carved marks come back out of the steam as the air clears, the band of them running level around the room, and she does not look at them either.

What she thinks about is small. She does not try to think about the thing in the water, which is past her, which her mind keeps sliding off of the way a hand slides off ice. She thinks instead about the number of times she has been told, by people who meant it kindly, that her father was a private man. That he kept to himself. That whatever he did down at the lake was his own business and harmed no one. She had let herself believe it the way you believe a thing you have not tested, and the believing had cost her nothing, and now it has cost her everything it was holding back.

She breathes the hot cedar and the mineral smell under it. Her body has changed in the last hour and she can locate the change without naming it: she will not, after this, be able to stand at the kitchen window at night and tell herself the prints are an animal's. The lie is gone the way a tooth is gone, a smooth socket where it was, the tongue going back to it. She is not afraid, exactly, sitting here. Fear would be easier. What she has instead is the flat certainty of someone who has read the bottom line of a page and cannot unread it, and the heat works on her the way it must have worked on him, ten thousand times, holding the body still while the rest of it caught up.

When the seam in the firebox has gone from orange to a dull red she stands. Her legs are unsteady from the heat and she waits for them. Then she banks the stove, closes the firebox vent, puts her coat back on over a body that is still warm and will be cold by the top of the hill, and walks back up to the house with the notebook under her arm.

She doesn't look back at the lake.

She almost doesn't.

Chapter 8

The Ledgers

The notebook from the lockbox is not her father's.

She can tell before she opens it — the leather is a different color, a darker brown, and older, dried out in the way of things that have been kept in a cold place for a long time. When she opens the cover she finds the handwriting inside is not her father's either. Smaller, more careful, the handwriting of someone for whom writing in English was a learned rather than a native act. The ink has faded to a pale brown.

At the top of the first page, a date: October 14, 1919.

She sits at the kitchen table with a fresh cup of coffee and reads.

—

The notebook is Eino's.

It doesn't read the way she expected — she had imagined, if she'd imagined anything, something like confession, something like explanation. But Eino does not explain himself. He records. The same terse daily-entry style as her father's ledgers, the same attention to weather and temperature and what the lake is doing, but underneath that a different kind of entry, one that appears without warning and then disappears again for days or weeks at a time.

October 14. First snow. Ice forming at the inlet. The woman's cabin is gone. I have moved the stones.

She reads that entry three times. The woman's cabin is gone. She thinks about what Helmi told her — or didn't tell her, what Helmi refused to say. She thinks about what Ernest Autio's folder contained and what it didn't contain.

She keeps reading.

October 29. Ice at four inches on the north end. She has not been still. Risto says the same. I have begun the foundation.

November 3. She came up last night for the first time. I sat with the stove until morning. She did not come through.

November 17. The sauna is framed. I told Aino it is for the family. She looked at me for a long time and did not speak.

The first Aino. Her great-grandmother. She looked at him for a long time and did not speak, and he wrote it down, and kept building.

She closes the notebook. She keeps one hand flat on the cover, the way you keep a hand on a thing you might have to deal with again, and she becomes aware that her hands are cold. The kitchen is not cold. The furnace is running; she can hear it. But her fingers have gone stiff around the leather and there is the coffee, the fresh cup she'd poured to read by, and when she lifts it to drink it is cold too, a skin already forming on it, and she does not remember letting it go that long. She does not know how long she has been sitting here. The light through the window has moved.

In the quiet she can hear the house. The furnace, the tick of the baseboard heat, the refrigerator cycling — and under all of it, faintly, a sound she had not noticed until now, a settling somewhere below, a single small knock of wood against wood that could be the house adjusting to the cold or could be nothing or could be the floor over the basement. She holds still and waits to hear it again. It does not come again. She files it the way she files things and the filing does not work.

These are not strangers in this book. The careful hand, the stones moved, the woman's cabin gone. The man who built a sauna over it and told his wife it was for the family. They are hers. She has his jaw, her father had his jaw, it is in the photograph in the hall. The thing the book is recording, page by terrible page in a man's even bookkeeping, is what her family did and went on doing, and she is holding it in two cold hands in her father's kitchen.

She opens it again.

The entries go on through the winter of 1919 and into 1920, the same pattern — weather, ice depth, and then occasionally the other kind of entry, the ones that don't sound like a man talking about ice. She was restless last night. Three times she came up, three times I sat with it. The names again — she knows them all. And later: Risto's boy saw something at the inlet. We told him it was a beaver. He is seven. He believed us.

Risto's boy. The Mäkelä line, extinct by 1947 according to what they knew. A seven-year-old boy in 1920 who saw something at the inlet and was told it was a beaver.

She reads through the winter and into the spring of 1920, and in April there is an entry that stops her:

April 9. The ice is gone. She is more free now. I do not sleep well. Pastor came on Sunday and I wanted to tell him and I did not. There is no telling this to a pastor. There is no telling it to anyone. We have done what we have done and it cannot be undone and the only thing is to watch.

She reads it again. We have done what we have done.

He knew. Of course he knew. He had always known exactly what he'd done and he had decided, in the autumn of 1919, that the only available response was to build something over it and watch it for the rest of his life. And his son after him. And his grandson after him.

She goes to the basement and gets the second-oldest ledger from the shelf — her grandfather Toivo's, beginning in 1948, the year her father was born. She takes it upstairs and reads it alongside Eino's notebook, the two of them making a kind of call and response across thirty years: Eino's careful faded entries and Toivo's larger, more hurried handwriting, a man who had grown up knowing about this and had been handed the watch like an inheritance he couldn't refuse.

Toivo's early entries are steadier. He'd learned from his father, she can see it — the same records of ice and weather, the same pattern of the other kind of entry. But as the years go on something changes. The handwriting gets less steady. The entries come less regularly and then in clusters, as though he'd been away and was catching up. And then, in the entries from the spring of 1962:

March 3. She has been loud this week. Every night. I am so tired.

March 19. I told Aili I need to go to the sauna again and she looked at me the way she looks at me now, like she is trying to find the man she married under the one I have become.

Aino has been reading without paying close attention to names. She goes back through the earlier pages. In the entries from 1948, 1949, the early years of Toivo's watch, a different name appears — Eeva. She'd missed it on the first read. Toivo's first wife. She'd died in 1953, when her father would have been five. Aili had been the second wife, the war bride from Finland who'd come over in 1956 and never gone near the sauna and who had once told Aino — she'd been about twelve — that the lake was not for swimming, in a tone that brooked no follow-up.

She reads on.

April 14. I have been thinking. There must be another way. The watching is not enough. Eino watched his whole life and it did not change anything. I have watched and it does not change anything. She is there. She is always there. We put her there and watching her does not undo that.

May 20. I have been to see the pastor. I asked him — carefully, without saying what I was asking — whether a wrong done cannot be undone, only watched. He gave me the answer I expected, about forgiveness and grace and the mercy of God. I did not find it useful.

June 1. Hank turned fourteen last month. He is a good boy. He does not know yet. I have been thinking about whether to tell him. I have been thinking that perhaps if I tell him there is another way — if we could find another way — he would not have to carry this.

That is the last entry. The notebook ends there, in the middle of a page, June 1st 1962. The rest of the page is blank. The rest of Toivo's pages are blank.

But Eino's notebook is not finished. She had stopped reading it at the spring of 1920, the entry that stopped her, and gone to the basement for Toivo's; she had read the two of them side by side after that and let Eino's lie open and forgotten under her elbow. She picks it up again now and turns to the back, to see how it ends, because Eino lived a long time — he died old, in his sleep, late that same year, an old man of eighty-two — and the careful faded hand goes on, thinner and shakier in the later pages but the same hand, the same weather and ice and the other kind of entry, all the way to the end.

She finds it near the back. The date is in June. The ink is darker than the pages around it, pressed harder into the paper, the only place in the whole notebook where the old man's hand is not even.

June 22. They have brought my son up out of the water. He went out two days ago on the morning ice at the inlet, where it had not yet gone. He took no rod. He took the boat to the open water and then he left the boat and walked out onto the ice that was left and it would not hold a man and he knew it would not hold a man, he had walked that ice every spring for thirty years and knew to the inch what it would do. The sheriff has called it an accident. A man checks the ice in June and the ice gives way. I have signed the paper that says so. Aili does not believe it and I do not contradict her and I do not agree with her. He had said to me a week before that the watching was not enough and there must be another way and I told him there is no other way, there is only the watching, and he looked at me the way Aino looked at me in the autumn of 1919 and did not speak. I do not know what was in his mind on the ice. I do not think he knew. A man can walk out onto rotten ice meaning to test it and meaning the other thing both at once and not be able to say which, and then it does not matter what he meant. She was loud the night he died. She is quiet now. The boy is fourteen. He does not know yet. I am too old for this and there is no one else.

She reads it twice. She sets the notebook down very flat on the table.

She sits at the kitchen table for a long time with both notebooks in front of her. Outside the window the light is changing — late afternoon, the sky going gold and orange through the pines, the snow on the yard turning pink.

Her grandfather had been trying to find another way. He'd gone to a pastor who hadn't understood the question. He'd gone to his own father and been told there was no other way. And then on a June morning he had taken the boat out and left it and walked out onto ice he knew to the inch, and whatever was in his mind out there, his father had not been able to name it and neither could she.

She picks up her father's newest ledger, the one she'd read in the basement the first day. She finds the entry from the summer she was nineteen. She'd known it would be there. She reads it now knowing what she knows:

July 12. A. [Aino] found one of the early books today. I took it from her. I handled it badly. She left. I don't know if she will come back.

July 13. She didn't come back.

July 14. She is gone. I sat in the sauna all night. She was very loud.

She. Her father had gone to the sauna the night Aino left. Had sat there alone. Had done his watching in the worst night of his life.

Aino closes the notebooks. She stacks them carefully on the table. She gets up and goes to the window and stands there for a while watching the light go out of the sky.

Then she picks up her phone.

It is an absurd object to be holding in this kitchen. The notebooks on the table go back to 1919, leather and faded ink and a hand that learned English the hard way, and she is standing over them with a slab of glass that connects, when it feels like it, to everything. The bars in the corner of the screen come and go. She has to stand by the window to keep them.

She doesn't know how to search for what she wants. There is no clean way to type it. She tries the name of the lake, the county, the word Ojibwe, the word removed, and reads her way down through real estate listings and a historical society page and a PDF of a county plat map until she has, slowly, the outline of it — the band, the dates, the ground that this lake sits on the edge of and used to sit deeper inside of. She finds the name of the band itself and writes it on the back of her father's envelope: Lac Vieux Desert Band of Lake Superior Chippewa. Then she finds the tribal offices, and a phone number, and a page of names and titles, and she reads the names until one of them is a person whose work might plausibly have anything to do with a wrong done on this lake in 1919, and she writes that name down too.

It feels like nothing. It feels like the strangest thing she has done since she got here, stranger than the cold wood, stranger than the sauna — this, standing in her dead father's kitchen at dusk with a phone in her hand, looking up the people her great-grandfather wrote about as she and the names and the stones, as though the answer to a hundred years of it were a number she could call during business hours.

She doesn't call tonight. It is too late in the day and she is not ready. But she has the name, and tomorrow she will be.

She makes dinner. She sits at the table with the notebooks and her father's ledgers spread around her like a flood. She eats without tasting much.

After dinner she puts the notebooks and ledgers in order — Eino's first, then Toivo's four, then her father's forty-two — and carries them carefully down to the basement and puts them back on the shelf exactly as she found them.

She goes back upstairs. She washes her hands at the kitchen sink.

She looks at the photograph on the counter, herself at eight with the fish and the lake behind her.

She had been happy that day. She is almost certain of it.

Chapter 9

Old Boyfriend

She goes to the gas station in the morning for coffee because the percolator is doing something wrong — not broken, just off, the coffee coming out thin and pale, which might be the grind or the water or something she's doing differently from her father — and she is standing at the counter waiting for the attendant to ring her up when someone says her name.

She turns. A man about her age, maybe a year or two older, coming in from outside with the cold still on him. Tall, broad through the shoulders, wearing a canvas work jacket and boots that have actually been worked in. Dark blond hair going gray at the sides, a face she knows.

"Mikko," she says.

Mikko Aaltonen. She has not thought about him in years. She has thought about him occasionally, in the way you think about people who were important to you at seventeen and whom you've reduced, over time, to a single representative image: in his case the front seat of a truck, winter, the heater running, a conversation she can no longer reconstruct but remembers the feeling of. Important, at the time. She'd left and he hadn't and that had been that.

"I heard you were in town," he says. He looks at her with the careful expression of a man who has rehearsed this moment. "I'm sorry about your dad."

"Thank you. He was — " she stops. "He was good to you, I think? You two stayed close?"

"He hired my crew a few times over the years. Siding, a few years back. Roof repairs." He takes a cup from the coffee station, fills it. He doesn't look at her while he does this. "He talked about you."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Then it's probably true." He looks at her now. Something in his face that she can't read, or isn't trying hard enough to read. "I was going to head over to the diner for breakfast if you want to —"

"Yeah," she says. "I do."

—

The diner is warm and smells like bacon and the particular coffee of a place that has been making it the same way since 1974. They take a booth by the window. The waitress knows Mikko and looks at Aino with the frank curiosity of someone who has been briefed. Aino orders eggs and toast and is aware that this will be talked about before she reaches the end of the block.

They talk about easy things first, the way people do who have history and are being careful with it. She asks about his business. He runs a small construction outfit, he and two other guys, mostly residential work around the range. He asks about Milwaukee. She tells him about the job, the apartment, the outline of a life that sounds, as she describes it, more provisional than she'd thought of it.

The waitress brings the eggs and Mikko's coffee gets refilled without his asking. There is a silence then, not uncomfortable, that goes on a beat longer than a silence between strangers would. He turns his cup a quarter turn on the table.

"You look good," he says. "I'd have known you anywhere."

"You too." It's true. He has weathered the way the men up here weather, into something that looks like the country they live in. "You married a Niemi girl. Somebody told me. Years ago."

"Laura. We're divorced." He says it plainly, no weight on it. "Eight years now. She's in Marquette. It was — " He stops, and she watches him decide not to finish the sentence, watches him fold it back up. "What about you."

"Divorced too. No kids." She drinks her coffee. "We're a pair."

He smiles at that, briefly. Then the smile goes and something more careful comes in behind it, and she knows, the way you know weather coming, that they are both standing at the edge of the same thing. The truck. The winter. The year she was nineteen and got in her car and he didn't ask her not to and she didn't ask him to come.

"I always figured," he starts, and the sentence has the shape of something he's said to himself before, in the cab of his truck, alone. I always figured you'd write. I always figured I'd hear. He doesn't say either one. He looks out the window a moment, then back. "It was a long time ago," he says instead, which is not what he meant, and they both let it be what he said.

She could tell him why she left. She has the words for it now in a way she did not have them at nineteen — about the house and the lake and the thing under it she didn't have a name for then, about a father who took a book out of her hands and made her understand, without saying anything, that there were rooms in him she would never be let into. She could say all of that. She doesn't. It isn't his to carry, and anyway the leaving had only ever been half about him, and the half it was about him she doesn't trust herself to get right across a diner table at eight in the morning.

"It was," she agrees. A long time ago.

"You ever think about coming back?" he asks.

"No," she says, without hesitation, and then immediately thinks of the notebooks spread across the kitchen table last night, her grandfather's last entry, she would not have to carry this. "I don't know," she says. "Maybe."

He looks at her. "How long are you staying?"

"I'm not sure anymore."

He nods like this is the answer he expected. He wraps both hands around his coffee cup. Outside the window the main street of Kivijärvi is quiet, a pickup truck going past, a woman walking a dog. The ordinary world.

"The sauna," he says, and her attention sharpens.

She waits.

"I saw the smoke yesterday," he says. "From the road. I wasn't going to say anything." He is looking at his coffee. "I knew your dad. Not the way you knew him, but — enough. And I know what the sauna meant."

"What do you know about it?" She keeps her voice even.

He is quiet for a moment. "My grandfather knew your great-grandfather. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Eino and my grandfather were friends. They came over around the same time, same region. Worked the mine together." He pauses. "There are things my grandfather told my father and my father told me. Things you don't — " He stops. He looks out the window. "My grandfather said that what Eino did, that winter, was the worst thing he'd ever seen a man do. And that Eino spent the rest of his life trying to make it right by watching, and that watching wasn't making it right, and that by the time my grandfather understood that it was too late to do anything about it."

She stares at him. "Your grandfather was there."

"No," Mikko says. "He wasn't. But he knew the men who were. He knew what happened." He finally looks at her. "I've known my whole life. Everyone whose grandfather was around in 1919 knows, on some level, though most of them have worked very hard not to. It's a small town. It has always been a small town."

She thinks about Helmi Korhonen in the church basement, glancing around to see who was in earshot before she spoke.

"What happened when you went in?" he asks.

"I saw something in the water." She says it plainly, as a fact, the same way she would report something at work. This is what I observed. "Under the ice. I don't know what it was."

He lets out a breath. "Your father went every week. For thirty years."

"I know. I read the ledgers."

He nods. He looks out the window again. The pickup is long gone. The street is empty.

"What do you want to do?" he asks.

It's the right question, she thinks. Not what should she do, not what does she have to do. What does she want to do. She has not been asked that question since she got here. She has not asked it of herself.

"I don't know yet," she says. That's the honest part, so she starts there. "I keep — " She turns her fork over on the plate. "Three days ago I didn't know any of this existed. Now there are these books in the basement going back a hundred years and a thing in the water and a town full of people who all knew before I did, and I keep waiting to feel like it isn't mine to deal with. And it doesn't go away. It isn't going away. So." She hears herself trailing into nothing and makes herself stop, and sits with it a moment, the diner warm around them, the smell of bacon, a fork clinking somewhere behind her. What she wants is underneath all of that and she has to go down and get it. "I want to know what they did," she says, slower. "What was actually done. Not the version where it's a sauna and a family that keeps to itself. The real one." She looks at him. "I want to understand what happened in 1919. And I want to talk to someone who knows."

"Someone from the band," he says. Not a question.

"I have a name."

He nods slowly. "They may not want to talk to you."

"I know."

"But if they do — if they're willing —" He picks up his coffee cup. Sets it down again. "Whatever you decide to do about it, I'll help. If you want. That's all I wanted to say."

She looks at him across the table, this man she knew when she was seventeen, who has lived his whole life in the long shadow of something his grandfather knew and never spoke of properly, who is offering now, carefully and without drama, to help.

"Okay," she says.

They finish their breakfast. The conversation finds its way to easier things after that, old names, what became of people, who is still around. She laughs once, genuinely, about something she can't remember the moment after, and it surprises her — the laugh itself, the ease of it. It has been a long week.

Outside on the sidewalk they stand in the cold for a moment.

"Call me," he says. "When you know what you want to do."

She says she will. She watches him walk to his truck. She stands on the sidewalk in the February cold with her hands in her pockets and thinks that she has, without quite deciding to, found someone in this town who is not trying to talk her into leaving.

She drives back to the house. She finds the name she wrote on the back of her father's envelope.

She calls.

Chapter 10

What Comes Up

She goes back to the sauna that evening.

She knows she shouldn't, or knows that every person who has spoken to her since she arrived would say she shouldn't — Paavo at the bar, Helmi in the church basement, Mikko over eggs this morning. Don't go out on the lake. Leave it locked. Don't go to the sauna again. A chorus of it, all saying the same thing in different registers.

But she's read the ledgers now. She knows what her father was doing and she knows what his father was doing before him, and she has started to understand that the watching — the hundred-year watch the Hietala men kept — was not a solution. It was a containment. And containments are not permanent. They require someone to maintain them, and her father is dead, and she is the last Hietala, and the thing in the water has been coming up to the surface every night for two weeks now and making prints in the snow outside the back door.

The watching won't stop it. But she needs to understand it before she can do anything else.

She brings wood down from the porch stack. She lights the kiuas. She sits on the lower bench in her coat while it heats and reads more of Eino's notebook by the light of a headlamp, the early entries, the ones from before the first winter — the way he describes arriving, the land, the lake. He had thought it was beautiful. He had written that word, kaunis, beautiful, and underlined it.

She moves to the upper bench when the heat is real.

She has brought a ladle of water and she pours it over the stones and the steam rises and the carved marks on the walls emerge and disappear in the vapor. She sits in the heat and lets it do what it does — pulls her out of her head, insists on her body, makes thought slow and simple. She understands why her father came here. Even with everything she knows about this sauna and what it sits over, there is something about the heat that makes sense, that feels like the right place to think about hard things.

After a while she climbs down from the upper bench. She stands in front of the hatch.

She opens it.

The cold air comes up as before. She settles cross-legged on the floor and looks down through the opening at the ice, pale and still in the dim light.

She waits longer this time. Ten minutes. Twenty. The fire ticks in the stove. The steam thins as the heat from the last löyly dissipates. She is aware of the cold coming up from the hatch in contrast to the heat still radiating from the stones, two temperatures at once, and she understands in some bodily way why her father always stoked the fire before he sat here — the heat above and the cold below, the threshold between them, the hatch as the seam.

Then it comes.

Slower this time, or she is more prepared for it — the darkening below the surface, the movement that is not current and not a fish and not anything she has a name for. She watches it. It moves toward the opening from somewhere she can't see, gathering, becoming more itself as it approaches, and she realizes that what she is seeing is not one thing but many — not a shape exactly but a presence, the way you can sense a person in a dark room before you see them.

It stops below the opening.

She does not put her hand in. She does not lean over.

But she leans closer than she did the first time — close enough to see, in the pale gray ice below, something that resolves slowly out of the pattern of frozen water. A face. Or the suggestion of a face. Or her own mind, pattern-seeking in the way minds do, finding a face in the random structure of ice the way you find shapes in clouds.

She doesn't know which. She doesn't look away.

The temperature in the sauna has dropped. She realizes it gradually — the kiuas is still hot, the stones still radiating, but the cold coming up through the hatch has intensified, spreading out across the floor and climbing, and her breath is visible now in a way it wasn't five minutes ago. She can feel it on her feet, on her hands.

And then the knocking.

Three knocks, from below. Not on the ice — through it, or from under it, a sound that comes up through the floor of the sauna itself, through the pilings, three distinct knocks with a pause between each one that is exactly the same length.

She goes very still.

She does not move. She does not breathe in a way she would call breathing. The fire ticks. The stones tick as they give up their heat. She counts the seconds without meaning to — she counts everything now, she has started to notice, the silences most of all — and it goes on longer than she expects, long enough that she begins to think it was the only set, that it was over.

The cold comes up through the hatch.

Three knocks. Said the names. Her father's ledger. Quiet by morning.

After a long silence there are three more.

The same three. The same pause between them, measured out to the same length, as though something below were keeping the time exactly and would keep it exactly as long as it took. She does not say anything yet. She lets the sound finish and lets the room go quiet around where it had been, and she stays where she is, kneeling, until the quiet has held long enough that she trusts it.

She becomes aware that she is holding her breath. She lets it out slowly. Her hands on her knees are trembling — not from cold, or not only from cold.

She thinks of what her father wrote. It is not evil.

She thinks of what Eino wrote. She was restless last night.

She thinks of her grandfather Toivo. Perhaps if I tell him there is another way.

She does not know the names. She doesn't know what names her father said, what Toivo said, what Eino said — the ledgers record the saying of names but not what names, as though that part were too private to write down, or too dangerous. She doesn't know the name of the woman in the water. She only knows she is there.

She kneels at the edge of the hatch and looks down at the ice, at the suggestion of a face, and she says, because it is the only true thing she knows to say:

"I'm sorry. I don't know your name yet. But I know what my family did. And I'm going to find out how to make it right."

Nothing happens. The ice is ice. The sauna is warm and dim. The kiuas ticks.

She closes the hatch.

She sits on the lower bench for a long time afterward, until the fire burns low and the heat begins to fade and the cold starts to reclaim the room. Then she puts on her coat and banks the stove and walks back up the hill to the house in the dark.

Inside, she washes her hands and face at the kitchen sink and makes tea and sits at the table. She is not shaking anymore. She feels — she doesn't have a word for it. Not calm exactly. Something older than calm. The feeling of having looked at a thing directly that you have been looking away from for a long time.

Her phone shows a missed call. The number she doesn't recognize and then does — she'd written it on the envelope this afternoon, the tribal offices, a woman named Sandra who'd said she would pass along the message. The missed call is from a different number. There is a voicemail.

She listens to it.

A woman's voice, older, unhurried: This is Louise Swifthawk. I understand you've been trying to reach someone about the lake property in Kivijärvi. I knew your father. I think it's time we talked. Call me back when you're ready.

She looks at the timestamp under the message. The call came in at 8:47. She does the arithmetic without deciding to. She was down at the sauna at 8:47 — on the lower bench, or kneeling at the edge of the open hatch, or walking back up the hill in the dark, the phone in her coat pocket where she never felt it, never heard it. It means nothing. People call in the evening. She looks at the number again, and then at the time again.

Aino sets the phone down on the table, face down. She wraps both hands around her mug of tea.

She will call back tomorrow. Tonight she sits in her father's kitchen with the photograph of herself at eight on the counter and the sound of three knocks still present somewhere in her body, below thought, below language, and she does not feel afraid.

She feels, for the first time since she drove north on a February Tuesday with bags of peanut butter and apples on the passenger seat, like she is in the right place.

Chapter 11

Helmi Korhonen

She calls Louise Swifthawk in the morning and they talk for twenty minutes. Louise's voice is measured and careful, a woman who has thought about this conversation before having it. She asks Aino a few questions — how long she's been in Kivijärvi, what she knows about the property, whether she's been to the sauna. When Aino says yes, she's been to the sauna, there is a pause.

"What happened?" Louise asks.

Aino tells her, plainly, the way she would report something at work. The darkness under the ice. The knocking. What she said into the hatch.

Another pause, longer this time.

"I'd like to meet with you in person," Louise says. "Not on the phone. Can you come to Watersmeet?"

They agree on a day, two days from now. Louise gives her an address. She says: "Come alone," which Aino had intended to do anyway, and then: "Don't go back to the sauna until after we talk." A pause. "Please."

Aino says she won't. She means it.

She puts the phone down and sits for a moment with the strangeness of it — that she has just made an appointment to discuss what is in the lake under her family's sauna with a woman whose family was put there by her great-grandfather. That this phone call was ordinary in its logistics, a time and an address, and impossible in every other way.

She needs to do something with her hands. She decides to go see Helmi.

—

Helmi Korhonen lives on the north end of town in a small white house with a covered porch and a birch tree in the front yard that is, in Aino's estimation, approximately the age of the town. She'd gotten the address from the Lutheran church directory, which was in a kitchen drawer at her father's house, which tells her something about the kind of man her father was — he kept a church directory in a kitchen drawer even though he never, as far as she could tell, used it for anything.

She knocks. Helmi answers after a minute, dressed for the day despite it being nine-thirty in the morning, a small woman in a cardigan and wool pants with reading glasses pushed up on her forehead and an expression that is not quite surprised to see her.

"I thought you might come," she says. She steps back to let her in.

The house is warm and orderly and smells like coffee and something baked. The front room is full of photographs — family, she assumes, and among them, on the shelf beside the window, a photograph she recognizes: two women, young, on a ship's deck, squinting into sun. One of them is a younger Helmi. The other one she doesn't know for a moment and then does. Her grandmother Aili.

"Sit down," Helmi says. "I'll get coffee."

—

They sit across from each other at the kitchen table with coffee and a plate of cookies that Helmi produces without being asked. Helmi waits. She is, Aino has noticed, a woman who is very comfortable with silence, who uses it the way some people use small talk, to let the air settle.

"I went to the sauna," Aino says.

"I know," Helmi says.

"Mikko Aaltonen told you?"

"Mikko's grandmother told me forty years ago that when the time came, someone would go into that sauna who wasn't going to just sit and watch. I have been waiting to see who it was." She picks up her coffee. "I hoped it would be your father. He wasn't able."

"He wrote, at the end. That he thought there might be another way. That he didn't know what it was."

Helmi nods. "Toivo thought the same. He went looking for it in the wrong places." She sets her cup down. "The church," she says, with a flatness that is not quite contempt. "As though the church had anything useful to say about this."

Aino thinks about Toivo's ledger entry. The pastor gave me the answer I expected. I did not find it useful.

"What do you know?" Aino asks. "What did my grandmother tell you?"

Helmi is quiet for a moment. "Aili knew from the beginning. Eino told her — not what he'd done, not exactly, but enough. She understood the sauna. She understood what the watching was and what it wasn't."

"How could she stay?"

"Where would she go." Helmi says it without a question mark, the way you state a fact you've already turned over for fifty years. "She was twenty-four. She had two children by then and a third coming and no English and a husband who had done a thing she could not undo by leaving. She was furious with him for the rest of his life and for the long years after. She kept it quiet because she had children and a life and because what else do you do." A pause. "Aili was a smart woman. Smarter than the men she married into. Smart enough to know that knowing a thing and being able to fix it are not the same. She told me, when we were old enough to be telling each other the truth. She told me what she knew."

"Which was?"

"That a woman was killed. That her husband and three other men did it. That they buried it — her, and what they'd done — under the sauna, and that it hadn't stayed buried." Helmi's voice is even, the voice of someone reciting a history they have long since moved past grief about and arrived at something colder. "She told me the woman's name. Mindemoyen. She had heard Eino say it in his sleep."

Aino is quiet. Then: "But what is it. What's down there. You said you understood what the watching was. So tell me what it's watching."

Helmi looks at her with something that is almost amusement and is not amusement.

"You think because I am old and I am Finnish I have a word for it." She turns her cup a quarter-turn on the table. "We had words. That was the trouble. We came over with a whole language for it."

"What words?"

Helmi is quiet long enough that Aino thinks she isn't going to answer. The coffee maker on the counter clicks off. Outside a car goes by and is gone.

"In the old country," Helmi says, "everything had its väki. You know this word? No. Your father wouldn't have taught you." She says it without reproach. "It is hard to put in English. A force. A power that lives in a place, in a thing. The forest has its väki. Fire has its väki — you don't whistle near the stove, you don't speak ill of it, because it is awake and it has a temper. Water has the most of all. Veden väki." She turns the word over carefully, an object she hasn't held in some time. "Where there was deep water, a lake like this one, the old people said there was a vetehinen. A water-being. It lived under the surface. It could be angry, it could pull a man down, it could be — paid off, almost. You gave the first fish back to it. You did not foul the water. You were polite to it, the way you are polite to a neighbor with a long memory and a short fuse."

"And that's what's in the lake."

"No." Helmi says it flatly, and then, more quietly, "That is the whole mistake. That is what I am trying to tell you." She sets the cup down. "They came here, Eino and the others, with these words in their mouths. Väki. Vetehinen. When the lake started — doing what it does — they reached for what they had. A man understands a thing he has a name for. So they told themselves: it is the water-spirit, it is angry, we will manage it the way our grandfathers managed the lakes at home. Watch it. Keep it fed. Keep it quiet." She shakes her head, once. "But a vetehinen is a Finnish thing. It belongs to Finnish water. The men brought the word across the ocean in a trunk like a coffee grinder and tried to set it down on a lake that already had — " she stops.

Aino waits.

"That already had whatever it had," Helmi finishes. "Which was not theirs. Which was not a vetehinen and never was. It was a woman, first of all, a real woman with a name, and what she became after they did what they did — that is not a thing I have a word for. The Finnish word was always too small. It was a borrowed coat that didn't fit and they kept trying to button it." She looks at the window. "Vetehinen is what they could say. It is not what was true."

"Then what's true."

"I don't know." Helmi says it without apology. "And the part I do know is not mine to hand out. There are people across the lake whose grandmothers knew what this water was before any Finn ever saw it, and they did not call it by my words, and they did not understand it the way four frightened men with a thing to hide understood it. If there is a true name for it, it is theirs, and you will not hear it from me, because I would only get it wrong the way Eino got it wrong, and getting it wrong is the entire history of this place." She picks up her coffee again. "That is why you are going to Watersmeet. That is the only sensible thing anyone in your family has thought of in a hundred years."

Aino turns this over. "But Aili understood it. You said that. She knew what the watching was and wasn't, when Eino didn't."

"She understood it could not be named and managed. That is most of what there was to know, and it was more than the men ever learned." A pause. "What none of us knew — Aili, me, your father — is what to do about it. The women always knew more than the men but the men were the ones going into the sauna and we were not." A pause. "I told you to leave it. I meant it. I still mean it. But I also knew when I said it that you probably wouldn't."

"I called the tribal offices," Aino says. "I'm going to Watersmeet in two days."

Helmi looks at her for a long moment.

"Good," she says. "That is the right thing." She picks up her coffee. "Whatever they tell you, whatever they ask of you — listen. Don't argue. Don't explain. Listen."

Aino nods.

"And take Mikko with you," Helmi says.

"She said to come alone."

"She said that because she doesn't know Mikko." Helmi sets her cup down with a small decisive click. "His grandmother was a good woman. She spent her whole life trying to make up for what her husband knew and didn't stop. If you tell Louise Swifthawk whose grandson he is, she will know his grandmother's name, and that will matter." She pauses. "This is not only a Hietala problem. It was never only a Hietala problem. Take Mikko."

Aino thinks about Mikko at the diner. Whatever you decide to do, I'll help.

"All right," she says.

They drink their coffee. Helmi refills both cups without asking. Outside the kitchen window the birch tree in the front yard is very still in the cold air, the white bark bright against the gray sky.

"My grandmother," Aino says. "Did she ever forgive him? Eino?"

Helmi thinks about this for a longer time than Aino expects.

"She loved him," she says finally. "That's not the same thing. She loved him and she knew what he was and she lived with both of those things for forty years and I don't think she resolved them, no. I don't think she was able to." A pause. "She told me once that the hardest part wasn't forgiving him. The hardest part was that she understood how it had happened. She could follow the chain of it — the land pressure, the mining company, the Indian agent, the other men, the drinking, the dark, the fear. She could follow every link of that chain and still arrive at the same place. And understanding it didn't make it right. It just made it more terrible."

Aino thinks about Eino's notebook. Kaunis. Beautiful. The man who thought the lake was beautiful and then did what he did in it.

She does not have anything to say to that. Helmi doesn't seem to expect anything. The clock on the wall above the stove moves its minute hand with a small dry sound. Aino lets the question be over and finds that it isn't, quite — that it has left something in the room that wants a little more air.

"Did you forgive her?" she asks. "For staying. For keeping it quiet."

Helmi looks at her, and for the first time the stillness in her face is not the comfortable kind.

"There was nothing to forgive," she says. "She did what a woman with three children does. She survived it and she did not let it eat her." She turns her cup again. "I used to think — when we were younger, before I understood anything — that she should have done more. Said something. To someone. I had ideas about what a person ought to do." A small breath that is not quite a laugh. "Then I got old enough to understand what would have happened to her, and to the children, and to nothing else. She was not the one who needed to be forgiven. She spent forty years carrying a thing she didn't put down there. That is its own kind of penance, carrying what isn't yours."

She is quiet for a moment.

"She made me promise I would tell whoever came after. That is all she could do, at the end. Hand it forward to someone with fewer reasons to be afraid." Helmi looks at her steadily. "So now I have told you. And you have fewer reasons than she did. Don't waste them."

Aino nods. She finds she can't say anything. Helmi doesn't ask her to.

They sit in the kitchen and drink their coffee and outside the birch tree stands in the cold without moving, white and bare and patient.

Chapter 12

The Drive to Watersmeet

She calls Mikko that afternoon. She tells him what Helmi said. He is quiet for a moment and then says: "My grandmother's name was Rauha. Louise Swifthawk may know it."

"Will you come?"

"Yes," he says, without hesitation.

She calls Louise Swifthawk and tells her she'd like to bring someone, explains who, explains whose grandson. There is a pause on the line.

"Rauha Aaltonen," Louise says. "Yes. I know that name." Another pause. "Bring him."

—

They drive to Watersmeet on a Thursday morning, Mikko's truck because the roads are better with the higher clearance. The sky is low and white. The heater works on one setting, which is too hot, so they run it in bursts and then crack the windows, and the cab fills with the smell of exhaust and cold pine and the coffee in the two travel mugs in the console, going lukewarm.

The landscape between Kivijärvi and Watersmeet is forty miles of the same country. Pines and birch and the occasional glimpse of a frozen lake through the trees, a gas station, a shuttered bait shop, a church with a plowed lot and no cars in it. The road runs straight for long stretches and then bends for no reason she can see, around some property line drawn a century ago by men who are all dead now. Mikko drives the way he does everything, without hurry, both hands on the wheel, easing off well before the curves.

They drive most of it without talking. Earlier in their acquaintance she had read his silences as the comfortable kind, a U.P. quality, or maybe just his — a man who does not feel the need to fill the air. This silence is not that. This one has a shape. There is a conversation they are not having and it sits between them on the bench seat like a third passenger, and she is aware of it the way you are aware of a sound that has stopped: the absence of it is louder than the thing would be.

She could ask him what his grandmother told him. Whether Rauha ever said the name out loud, in the kitchen, the way Aili said it in her sleep. Whether he has known, all the years he ran his crew and married a Niemi girl and divorced her, that the road they are on now was always going to be in front of him. She does not ask. He could ask her what she is going to say to Louise Swifthawk, whether she has it rehearsed, what she imagines comes after. He does not ask either. They carry their separate halves of it down the straight white road and the not-asking is its own kind of weather in the cab, pressing.

Past a county-line sign the trees open onto a long frozen bay and she looks at it without meaning to, the flat gray ice with the snow scoured off it in places, and then makes herself look back at the road. Forty miles. Every lake between here and there is the same lake, in a way she would not have understood a week ago.

"Are you nervous?" he asks, somewhere around the halfway point.

"Yes," she says.

"Me too." He says it to the windshield. His hands do not move on the wheel. After a while he reaches over and turns the heater off entirely, and they drive the rest of the way cold, which is better.

The address Louise gave her is a house on a side road off the main highway, a small ranch house with a satellite dish and a stack of firewood on the porch and a late-model truck in the driveway. There is a mat outside the front door that says WELCOME in plain letters, no embellishment.

She knocks. Mikko stands slightly behind her.

The woman who answers is in her late sixties, compact and solid, with close-cropped gray hair and eyes that assess them both in a single efficient look. She is wearing jeans and a flannel shirt and reading glasses on a cord around her neck. She looks like what she is, which is a person who has a great deal to do and has set some of it aside for this conversation.

"Come in," she says.

—

The living room is warm and ordinary — a couch, chairs, a television, a bookshelf, family photographs on the walls. She offers coffee and they both say yes and she goes to the kitchen and comes back with three mugs and a plate of fry bread and sits across from them in the armchair.

She looks at them both for a moment.

"I'll tell you what I'm going to tell you and what I'm not," she says. "There are things about Mindemoyen that belong to us and I won't be sharing them with you. Her name you already know. What she was to us, what we believe about what happened to her, what we call what she has become — that's not for this conversation." She looks at Aino specifically. "I want to be clear about that before we start."

"I understand," Aino says.

"What I'll tell you is the history, because the history is yours too and you should know it. And I'll tell you what I think you can do, which is not much, but it's something." She picks up her coffee. "Your great-grandfather acquired that land through a federal homestead claim in 1913. The land had been used by my family for generations before that. The Indian agent had been telling us for years that our claims were not recognized. Mindemoyen was the last one living on the property your great-grandfather claimed. She was elderly and alone and she refused to leave." She says all of this with the flatness of someone reading from a document they have read many times. "In the fall of 1919, four men — your great-grandfather and three others — came to her cabin. She died. Whatever exactly happened, she died, and her remains were put in the lake."

Aino has the mug in both hands and she keeps it there. She has come prepared, she had thought, to receive this. She has read the ledgers. She has read Eino's notebook in his careful learned-English hand, the woman's cabin is gone, I have moved the stones, and she had believed, sitting at the kitchen table with the leather cover under her palm, that she had already taken in the worst of it. She had filed it. That was the work she does, the work she is good at, taking a hard fact and setting it where it goes.

It does not go anywhere now. Louise says four men and she was elderly and alone and she refused to leave in the same even voice she used for the homestead claim and the date, and Aino feels each one land somewhere below the level she does her thinking on. An elderly woman, alone, who would not leave. The cabin gone. The stones moved. Her great-grandfather's jaw, which is her father's jaw, which is in the photograph in the hall, which is, when she looks down at the backs of her own hands around the mug, partly hers.

Her face holds. She is aware of holding it the way you are aware of bracing against a cold she had thought she'd dressed for. But the heat has gone out of her hands. The mug is warm and her hands are not taking it, and there is a pressure low in her chest, under the breastbone, dense and specific, that has nothing to do with the warm ordinary room. She died. The plainness of it is the worst part. Louise will not dress it up and Aino understands, dimly, that the refusal to dress it up is a courtesy and also a kind of judgment, and that both are correct. The fry bread sits on the plate between them. Somewhere in the house a clock or a heater makes a small sound. Outside, nothing.

She wants, for one bad moment, to say that she didn't do it. That she was born in 1971, in Milwaukee, that she never met the man. The wanting embarrasses her even as it rises and she does not say it, because it is beside the point, and it would only be Aino asking to be let off something no one has tried to put on her. She keeps the mug in her hands. She makes herself stay in the chair and in the fact. Her remains were put in the lake. Put there by men whose name she carries on the other side, the Hietala side, the side that built the sauna and kept the watch and called it for a hundred years a family that keeps to itself.

When she finds she can move again she does the only thing there is to do, which is small and inadequate and the whole of what is asked of her in this moment. She looks up. She holds the older woman's eyes. She nods, once — not agreement, there is nothing to agree to, only that she has heard it and is not going to look away from it.

Louise looks at her steadily. Aino meets her eyes.

"We knew," Louise says. "We knew within weeks. We had no recourse — no legal recourse, nothing that would have been heard. We grieved her. We have grieved her for a hundred years." A pause. "We also knew, within a few years, that something remained. That she had not — moved on, let's say. We could feel it in the lake. We left it alone. It was not ours to interfere with."

"Because of the sauna," Aino says.

"Because of the sauna," Louise agrees. "Your family put themselves between us and her. Every generation. We could not reach her, and honestly —" she pauses, something shifting in her expression, "we were not certain what reaching her would mean. She was changed by what happened to her. What she wanted, what she needed — we don't know. We don't presume to know."

The room is quiet. Outside, a car passes on the road.

"What my father wrote," Aino says carefully. "In his note to me. He said she's not evil. That she was wronged and has been waiting for someone to do something other than watch."

Louise looks at her for a long moment. "Your father was more perceptive than his father and his grandfather. I think he was right, as far as he understood it." She sets her coffee down. "The watching — what your family has been doing — it was containment. It kept things from getting worse. It also kept things from getting better. She has been held in place for a hundred years by your family's attention. Watched but not acknowledged. Known but not named. That is its own kind of wrong, added to the original."

Mikko speaks for the first time. His voice is careful and quiet. "What would acknowledgment look like?"

Louise looks at him. "Your grandmother was Rauha Mäkinen before she married?"

"Aaltonen," he says. "Her family was Mäkinen."

Something moves through Louise's expression — not quite surprise, not quite its absence. "The Mäkelä line," she says.

"Yes."

She is quiet for a moment. She is looking at Mikko now the way she looked at Aino when she said four men, placing him, fitting the man in the flannel shirt on her couch into a history that had a slot for him before he was born.

"Your great-grandfather was there that night."

She does not say it as small talk and Aino, beside him, feels the difference. It is not new information to anyone in the room. Mikko has known his whole life; he said so at the diner, everyone whose grandfather was around in 1919 knows. But knowing a thing in your own head and hearing it said across a coffee table by the granddaughter of the woman it was done to are not the same knowing, and she watches it reach him.

"I know," he says.

His voice is level but something has gone out of it. He sets his mug down on the side table with the deliberate care of a man who has just noticed his hands. He does not look at Aino. He looks at the middle distance, at the family photographs on Louise Swifthawk's wall — other people's grandmothers, other people's children — and his jaw works once and is still.

The word there sits in the room. Not by the lake, not in the town, not in the long careful margin where Eino kept his ledgers and Toivo kept his after him. There. At the cabin. In the fall, in 1919, one of the four. Louise does not elaborate and Mikko does not ask her to, and Aino understands that the not-asking is the only thing he can offer it — that to ask for detail would be to ask Louise to hand over what is hers, and that Mikko, who has carried the shape of this for fifty years without the words, has decided in the space of a breath not to do that.

The silence holds a moment longer than is comfortable. Outside the car has long since passed. Then Louise lets it go, and turns, and continues.

"What would acknowledgment look like," she repeats, as though returning to a question from a different angle. "It would look like stopping. The watching, the sitting, the containing. It would look like your family removing itself from between us and her. And it would look like giving back — not the land, that's done and it would take courts and years and that's a different conversation. But what your family took from us in terms of — being able to grieve properly. Being able to say her name without your family's guilt standing in the way of it." She looks at Aino. "The things your father kept in that sauna. The notebooks, the tools, the records. Those belong with us. Not because they're ours legally, but because they're her history, and that is ours."

Aino thinks about the forty-two ledgers. The notebooks. The carved marks on the walls that she'd photographed and couldn't read.

"I'll bring them," she says.

"Not today," Louise says. "I need to talk to my family about what we want and how we want to receive it. This is not something I can decide alone." She looks at both of them. "I'll call you. It may take a week, may take two. When we're ready, we'll tell you what we want."

Aino nods. "And the sauna itself?"

Louise is quiet for a moment. "What were you thinking?"

"I don't know exactly. Taking it down, maybe. Or — I thought about fire. It's a sauna. They burn."

Louise's expression doesn't change. "That's your decision to make," she says. "Not mine. What happens to your family's structure on your family's land is not something I can tell you." A pause. "What I will say is that whatever you do with it, do it after we've had a chance to — prepare. There are things we may want to do first. On our side. Our own way." She picks up her coffee. "Can you give us two weeks?"

"Yes," Aino says.

Louise nods. She looks at them both again, that efficient assessing look. "You did the right thing coming here," she says. It is not a warm statement but it is a genuine one. "Most people wouldn't have."

"My father wanted to," Aino says. "He just didn't know how to start."

Louise considers this. "He kept watch for thirty years," she says. "That's something. It's not enough, but it's something." She stands, which is the signal that the conversation is done. They stand too. At the door she shakes both their hands.

Outside, in the cold, walking to the truck, Mikko doesn't say anything for a moment. He unlocks her door first, the way he was raised to, and goes around, and gets in, and sits for a second with the key in the ignition not turned, both hands on the bottom of the wheel, looking at the WELCOME mat and the firewood on Louise Swifthawk's porch.

Then: "Mäkelä," he says quietly. Not to her, exactly. To himself, or to his grandfather, or to the name in the air.

"Yeah," she says.

He starts the truck. He does not turn the heater on.

They drive back to Kivijärvi through the white pines with the low sky pressing down on everything. The light has not changed all day; it is the same flat white at two in the afternoon as it was at nine. The same gas station, the same shuttered bait shop, the same churches and the same long frozen bay sliding past on the passenger side, and Aino watches it go and does not look away from it this time.

The silence in the truck is different from the silence on the way there. That one had a conversation buried in it, the things they were each not saying held separate, his half and her half. This one is the same silence on both sides. It is heavier, and it is more honest, because there is nothing in it that wants to be said and isn't — only the thing itself, which neither of them has words equal to, which they are both now carrying in the same direction.

Mikko drives the way he did this morning, without hurry, but his shoulders have come down from where they were on Louise's couch and his face has the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he already knows the answer to. There. At the cabin. One of the four. He keeps both hands on the wheel and at one point he lets out a long breath through his nose, slowly, the way you do when you are deciding not to say something, and she lets him not say it.

She has her hands in her lap. She is aware of them — that she does not know what to do with them, that they have been cold since the living room and have not warmed up — and she finds, somewhere past the county line, that she has been turning her father's house key over and over between her fingers without deciding to, the small repetitive motion of a woman with nowhere to put what she is holding. She makes herself stop. She presses both palms flat against the tops of her thighs and leaves them there, and watches the road, and the cab is cold, and the quiet between them is exactly the right amount of quiet for what they are both carrying, and she finds that she can sit in it without difficulty, which is not the same as comfort and is better than comfort.

Chapter 13

Going Home

She rides back from Watersmeet in Mikko's passenger seat, watching the pines go by in the fading light. He drops her at the house and she stands in the cold driveway a moment watching his taillights disappear down the gravel road before going inside.

The sky outside the kitchen window is the dull pewter of a day that never quite found the sun. She puts the kettle on and stands at the counter thinking about what Louise said.

The watching — it was containment. It also kept things from getting better.

She has been thinking about her father's life, since she got here, in terms of what he failed to do. What he didn't tell her, what he didn't fix, what he couldn't bring himself to start. But there is another way to look at it, she thinks now. He sat in that sauna for thirty years. Every week, sometimes more. He went down the hill in the dark, in the cold, in every season, and he lit the stove and he opened the hatch and he watched. That is thirty years of keeping a promise he never made, inherited from a man who made the wrong one.

It is not enough. Louise said so and she already knew it. But it is not nothing, either.

She thinks about the first Aino. Her great-grandmother, who looked at Eino for a long time and did not speak, and then lived in that house for forty-three years with the thing he'd done and the thing he'd built over it and never went near the sauna. She wonders what the first Aino thought about in the night. Whether she talked to anyone. Whether there was a Helmi Korhonen for her, someone to tell the true version to.

She thinks there probably wasn't. 1919 was a different kind of alone.

The kettle starts to go and she takes it off before it whistles. She does not make the tea. She stands holding the kettle with the steam coming off the spout and finds that she does not want to be in the house.

—

She puts her coat on over the sweater and her father's chore gloves from the hook by the door, the leather ones gone stiff with cold, and goes out the back.

The light is nearly gone. The snow on the slope down to the water holds it longer than the sky does, a flat blue-white going gray, and her boots break the crust at every step. She has not been down to the shore since the second day. She told herself then it was the rotten ice, the city boots, the better sense. She does not tell herself anything now. She just goes down, past the woodpile, past the dark shape of the sauna at the waterline, and stops at the tree line where the snow ends, where she stopped before.

The lake is the same flat white it was three weeks ago. The fish houses are still out there, small and dark in the last of the light. Nothing has changed and everything has. She knows the history now. She knows what is in the water and how it came to be there and the name of the woman whose remains her great-grandfather moved with his own hands, and she stands at the edge of it in the cold and waits to feel something large.

What she feels instead is small and exact. The lake takes her in. There is no other word she can put to it that is honest. The cold off the ice comes up the slope and finds her face the way cold does, but underneath the cold there is a second thing, slower, that has nothing to do with weather — a settling of the whole white plain toward the place where she is standing, a leaning-in she feels along the front of her body and not her back. It is not the wind. The wind is at her back, going nowhere, dying at the shoreline the way it did before. This is the lake itself, gathered and aware, taking the measure of her the way you take the measure of a debt.

She does not look away from it. That is the only thing she has to offer, and she offers it. She stands until the cold has worked all the way through the chore gloves and her feet have gone numb in the city boots and the light is fully gone and the fish houses are only a darker dark on the dark, and the lake holds its attention on her the whole time, patient, and she lets it.

Then she goes back up the hill. Her own prints going down are already softening at the edges. She knocks the snow off her boots on the step and goes in and the house is warm and ordinary and she stands in it for a moment getting the feeling back in her hands.

—

She makes soup from what's in the refrigerator — there's not much, she needs to go back to the grocery store — and eats standing at the counter, which she does sometimes in Milwaukee and which in her father's kitchen feels different, feels like something he would have done, and she lets herself stand there and eat his soup and be his daughter for a few minutes without that being complicated.

Then she washes the bowl and sits at the kitchen table with a cup of tea and thinks clearly, as clearly as she has since she got here.

The ledgers need to go to Louise. All of them, or at least the ones from before her father — Eino's notebook, Toivo's ledgers. Her father's forty-two she is less certain about. They are his handwriting, his life, his thirty years of watching. She doesn't know if they belong with Louise or with her or nowhere, buried with her father. That question can wait.

The sauna itself. She has been avoiding thinking about this directly, but she makes herself think about it now. The structure has been there since 1919 in one form or another. Her father put new cedar in it the last summer she was home. He kept wood stacked and ready for the stove. He maintained it the way you maintain something important, something that has a function you cannot abandon.

Now she is the one deciding its function.

The things in the lockbox — the key she hasn't tried yet, whatever door it opens. The carvings on the walls. The tools in the basement whose purpose she still doesn't fully understand. All of this is information she doesn't have yet, and she needs it before she decides anything.

She gets out a piece of paper and makes a list, because she is a paralegal and lists are how she thinks:

What Louise's family wants and when.

What the key in the lockbox opens.

What the carvings mean and who to ask.

What to do with father's ledgers.

What to do with the sauna.

What to do with the house.

What to do after.

She looks at the last item. What to do after. It is the kind of line item she writes a dozen times a week, the catchall at the bottom of a matter, the placeholder for the part you cannot scope yet. At work it means the closing, the distribution, the file sent to storage. Here she does not know what it means and she has been letting the not-knowing pass for the same kind of clean professional deferral, a thing to be handled when the upstream items resolve.

It is not that. She makes herself see that it is not that.

The list is honest about the lake and dishonest about her. Every item above the line is a thing she does and then leaves. Give Louise the ledgers and drive away. Open the lockbox, learn the carvings, decide the sauna, list the house with a realtor in Marquette, and drive away. The whole document is built on the assumption that there is an after she returns to, that Kivijärvi is a matter she closes and Milwaukee is where she lives, and she is not sure anymore that the assumption is true, and that is the thing she has not written down because she does not have the handwriting for it.

Because here is the part under the part. She is not afraid of the lake. She has been afraid of the lake and the fear has changed into something she does not have a word for, down there at the tree line in the dark. What she is afraid of, sitting at her father's table with the tea going cold, is that she could stay. That the pull she feels is not grief and not duty and not the percolator. That her father went down that hill in the dark every week for thirty years because something in this place had hold of him, and that the same thing — older than her father, older than the homestead claim, set in the water in 1919 — has been quietly fitting her into the slot it kept for him since the night she drove up Highway 51 with the radio cutting out. That she would not be choosing it. That it would feel like choosing.

She does not write that down either. There is no clean line for it. But she stops pretending the last item is logistics.

She thinks about Mikko saying: Are you ever thinking about coming back?

She thinks about the way the cold hit her when she got out of the car that first night, the quality of it, the specific U.P. dryness she'd forgotten. She thinks about the percolator. The photograph. The pines outside the window making the light different from any light in Milwaukee.

She thinks about the drive north on Highway 51, the trees closing in, the radio cutting out on that stretch of highway where it always cuts out, the silence in the car that wasn't just silence.

She has been here for nearly three weeks. She has not thought about Milwaukee in several of them.

She folds the list and puts it in her coat pocket with the envelope and the photograph of herself at eight. She goes to bed at a reasonable hour and lies in the dark and listens to the house, which is quiet tonight — no sound from the lake, no knocking, just the furnace and the pines.

She said something into the hatch and the knocking stopped.

She doesn't know what that means. She doesn't know if it means anything. But she is aware, lying in the dark of her childhood bedroom under her grandmother's quilt, that the house feels different than it did when she arrived. Not less strange. But differently strange. Her father watched the lake for thirty years and the lake stayed where it was put; that was the whole of what the watching did, she understands now — it held the door, it did not open it, and a held door is still a closed one with a person standing guard. What she felt at the tree line tonight was not a thing held down. It was a thing turned, the way a head turns toward a sound, toward the slope and the house and the lit kitchen window and her, the only one left who carries the name and has gone down the hill and stood at the edge and not looked away.

She lies under the quilt and the furnace cycles on and off and somewhere below the cold floor and the frozen yard and the dark trees the water keeps its slow attention on the house, and she is the one in the house, and she finds — and this is the part she will not be able to explain to anyone, not Mikko, not Louise, not the version of herself with the apartment in Milwaukee — that she is not afraid, and that lying here in her father's house with a hundred years of it underneath her, she feels something she can only call being in the right place. Which is exactly what she is afraid of.

She sleeps. She dreams of nothing she can remember in the morning.

When she wakes the sun is coming through the bedsheet curtains and the yard outside is empty. No new prints in the snow.

She makes coffee. She looks at her list.

She starts with the key.

Chapter 14

Mikko Tells Her

The key from the lockbox is larger and older than the padlock key — iron rather than brass, the kind of key that belongs to something built before the age of standard hardware. She turns it over in her hand at the kitchen table trying to think what in this house would have a lock that old.

Not the house itself — the doors have modern deadbolts, her father's doing at some point. Not the basement — there's no lock on the basement door. She goes through the house methodically, room by room, opening closets and drawers, looking for something she's missed.

She finds it in the basement.

She'd walked past it twice without registering it — a section of the fieldstone foundation wall on the north side, behind the furnace, where the shadows are deep and she'd only glanced. She has to turn sideways to get back there, between the furnace's flank and the cold stone, and the warmth off the metal stops a foot in, the way it does, and then there is the wall. A wooden panel set into the stone, about three feet square, the boards dark and close-grained and older than the framing in the rest of the basement. A keyhole in the middle of it, in a plate of black iron that was not stamped out of a sheet by a machine. Someone forged it. The edges are uneven, hammered, the metal thicker at the corners, and there is a maker's roughness to it that her eye, after twelve years of mortgage paperwork and chain-of-title, has no category for. It is the kind of thing that would be a root cellar door in an older house. Which is exactly what it is.

She fits the key. The iron is heavy enough in her hand that she has to use her wrist. It turns with a single dry sound, no give to it, the bolt a thing the size of her finger sliding out of the stone.

The door swings inward on hinges that have been recently oiled — her father's maintenance, she can tell, the same patient keeping-up that marks everything in this house. He had kept this. Whatever else he had let go of, he had come down here with an oil can.

The cold comes out at her before she has the door all the way open. Not the basement's cold, which is the cold of a room above frozen ground. This is lower than that, and it has a wet edge to it, and a smell rides on it — earth, and the iron of the stone, and under those a sourness that is almost sweet, the smell of things kept a long time in the dark and slowly going to nothing. She stands in it a moment with her breath hanging in front of her. The space beyond is small, maybe six feet deep, the walls bare earth shored with timber, a single shelf of fieldstone running along one side and rough plank shelving along the other. No bulb. She uses her phone for light.

On the plank shelves: mason jars, the oldest she has ever seen outside a museum, the glass gone faintly violet the way old glass goes, the zinc lids dull and pocked. Hand-labeled. She holds the light close and reads the handwriting and knows it before she has read a word of it — Eino's, the same cramped slanted hand from the documents, from the oldest of the ledgers. The labels are in Finnish. Some of the words she has, from a childhood of half-listening: piharatamo, plantain. Siankärsämö, yarrow. Mesiangervo. Others she does not have at all, and a few are not plant names she thinks but something else, shorter, with a date after them. She does not open anything. The contents have gone dark and settled and she does not want to know how they smell.

On the stone shelf, a tin box that rattles when she lifts it and that she sets back down without opening, because her hands are cold and because some part of her has begun, down here, to ask permission of things before she touches them, and she notices that about herself and does not examine it.

And at the back of the stone shelf, pushed to the wall, a small figure carved from wood. It is wrapped in a piece of cloth, gone gray, tied at what she takes to be the neck with a length of the same cord that ties the oilcloth bundle. She can make out the shape of a head under the cloth, the suggestion of shoulders. It fits in her palm. She picks it up and the wood is colder than the air, colder than the stone, the particular cold she had felt in the carved studs in the workshop on the far side of the furnace, and she sets it back exactly where it stood and does not unwrap it. It is not hers to unwrap. She does not know what it is. She lets it go on being something she does not know.

On the center shelf, taking up most of the space: a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord, about the size of a large book. This she takes.

She takes the oilcloth bundle upstairs. She unties it carefully at the kitchen table.

Inside: documents. Old ones, the paper fragile and browned at the edges. The top sheet is a land claim document, her great-grandfather's name in official typescript, the property described in legal language she can parse easily enough after twelve years of reading legal documents. Below it: correspondence, a series of letters between Eino and the county land office, formal and dry. Below that, a folded sheet of paper in Eino's handwriting that is not a letter to anyone official.

She reads it.

It is a list of names. Four columns — one for each of the four families. Under each family name, a list of people, beginning with the original four men and working forward through the generations, names and dates of birth and, for the ones who had died, dates of death. Her own name is at the bottom of the Hietala column, with her date of birth. Her father's handwriting, not Eino's — he had updated this, adding names as they were born, removing nothing.

She is the last name on the Hietala list.

Under the Mäkelä column, the last entry reads: Risto Mäkelä Jr., b. 1912, d. 1947. No children. The end of that line.

Under the Lehtinen column, three names below the original Lehtinen man: living descendants, she presumes, still in the area.

Under the fourth column, in Eino's handwriting: the Saari family, four living descendants as of whenever Eino last updated this, and then her father's handwriting continuing the list — a Saari man still in Kivijärvi, she'd seen the name around town.

She sits with this list for a long time.

And while she is sitting with it the basement comes back to her — not the cellar, the workshop, the pegboard of tools she had handled her first morning and couldn't name. The long-handled things with heads she had no words for. The chisels hung in graduated order, smallest to largest, the way a man hangs them when he uses every one of them and knows where each belongs in the hand. The mallet of dark dense wood, worn smooth in the one spot, the handle shaped over time to a grip that was not hers and not her father's only. She had taken those for the ordinary tools of a man who fixed things, a drawknife and a froe among them, woodworking gone slightly past her. They are not that. They are the tools for the marks. For cutting the band of carved figures into the studs of the sauna and the framing of the basement walls and keeping them cut, deepening them as the wood weathered and the lines went shallow, year on year, the way you keep up a thing that has to hold. The cedar in the vise on the workbench, the half-finished piece her father was shaping when his heart stopped — she does not know what it was going to be and she understands now that this is the wrong question. It was going to be part of the structure. The tools and the marks and the sauna and the watch are one thing, and have been one thing since 1919, and her father spent his evenings down there not in a hobby but in maintenance, keeping the edges sharp on the only work that mattered, the way he oiled the cellar hinges, the way he stocked the firewood before he died.

She lets that settle. It does not frighten her the way the list does. It is almost the opposite. It is the first thing about her father in three weeks that she fully understands.

Before she calls Mikko, she calls her supervisor in Milwaukee. She has been gone nearly three weeks. Her extended leave runs out at the end of the month and she has been not letting herself think about what comes after.

Her supervisor picks up on the second ring. The connection is bad out here and his voice comes through with the underwater quality of a call routed through a tower thirty miles off, and she stands at the counter with the phone to her ear and looks at the kitchen while he talks. The grocery list in her father's hand, still on the counter where she'd set it the first day with the cracked reading glasses. The percolator. The window over the sink and the yard past it, the snow, the tree line and the slope down to the lake she cannot see from here. The kitchen of a man who has been dead three weeks, holding everything he left exactly where he left it, and her own life on the other end of the line, her caseload, the divorces and custody fights and the orderly grief of people whose dead stay dead. The two do not fit in the same room. She holds the phone and lets them not fit.

She tells him she needs more time — she doesn't know how much, another two to three weeks at minimum. There is a silence on the line that she reads accurately as the sound of a patient man running out of patience.

"Aino," he says. "I've been flexible with you. We have cases backing up."

"I know," she says. "I'm sorry. There are things here I have to finish."

Another silence. Then: "Two more weeks. After that I need to know something definitive."

She thanks him. She means it. She hangs up and stands a moment longer at the window — the job, Milwaukee, the life that has been on hold and going — and then she picks up the phone again.

She calls Mikko.

He comes over that afternoon, driving up the plowed gravel road in his truck, and she has coffee made and the documents spread on the kitchen table and she watches his face as he looks at them.

He stands over the table and reads the list a long time, the way she had read it, top to bottom, column by column, and she lets him. She does not narrate it for him. She has learned, this week, that there is nothing to say while someone reads their own name into a thing like this and that the saying would only be for her.

He finds his grandmother's name in the Mäkelä column. Rauha Mäkinen, b. 1921 — she'd been nine when they'd updated this, old enough to inherit the knowledge, young enough that it had shaped her whole life. Mikko's finger rests on her name for a moment. Then it moves up the column, to the original Mäkelä man at the top, one of the four, and back down again, the whole line of them, four generations of his own people set down in Eino's hand and then her father's, and then it stops at the bottom, where the Mäkelä column ends and hers does not.

"She never talked about it directly," he says. "She just — made sure I knew, in pieces. A thing here, a thing there. By the time I was old enough to put it together I already knew the whole shape of it."

"Did your father know?"

"Yes. He moved away when I was twelve. Different response to the same inheritance." He looks up from the document. "How many other people in this town know?"

"I don't know. Helmi knows. Paavo — I'm not sure how much." She pauses. "You said that everyone whose grandfather was around in 1919 knows on some level."

He nods. He sits down across from her and wraps his hands around his coffee mug. "What did Louise say about the Lehtinens? And the Saaris?"

"She didn't. We didn't get that far."

He is quiet. She watches him thinking. The afternoon light is in the kitchen now, the low slanting kind that comes this far north in winter for an hour before it goes, and it lies across the documents on the table and across the backs of his hands. Neither of them moves the papers. The list sits between them face up. She has the sense, sitting here, that this is the first time in a long while that two people have looked at it together in the same room — that Eino kept it and her father kept it and each of them carried it alone, folded into oilcloth in the cold, and that the simple fact of two coffee cups and two sets of eyes on it is a thing that has not happened, maybe, in a hundred years. It is not much. It does not change what is in the lake. But the paper is less heavy with two of them looking at it and she registers that, the small physical fact of it, the way the weight of a thing changes when a second hand takes a corner.

She does not say any of this. He would understand it and the saying would still be unnecessary. They sit with the list a while in the failing light.

"There's a Saari man on the north end of the lake," he says. "Retired. He's in his seventies. His grandfather was the one — " He stops. "I don't know exactly what his grandfather did. But he was one of the four."

"I know."

"He knows about the sauna. About what it is. I've had — not a conversation, but a moment, a few years ago, when we both understood we were talking around the same thing without saying it." He pauses. "He's not going to want this opened up."

"I know that too."

He looks at her. "What are you going to do?"

She thinks about what she told Louise. Taking it down. Or fire. Louise's response: That's your decision to make. And then: Can you give us two weeks.

"I'm going to wait for Louise to tell me what she wants," she says. "And then I'm going to do it. And I think — " She pauses. "I think the other families need to know. The Lehtinens and the Saaris. They've been carrying this too. Not the same way the Hietalas have, but — "

"You want to tell them."

"I think they should know what's happening. What I'm going to do." She looks at the list of names on the table. "They can do what they want with it. But I'm not going to make a decision that affects all of them without telling them first."

Mikko is quiet for a long moment. "I can talk to the Saari man," he says. "Erik Saari. I know him better than you do. It might go better coming from me."

"Would you?"

"Yes." He picks up his coffee. Sets it down. "This is going to be — not everyone is going to want this."

"I know."

"Some people are going to be angry."

"I know that too."

He looks at her with an expression she is starting to recognize — not quite admiration, something more complicated than that, something that has to do with having watched this property and this family from a distance his whole life and now watching the last Hietala decide to do what none of the others were able to. "Your father would have wanted this," he says. "I think. From what I knew of him."

She thinks of the last ledger entry. Tired. And before it, the entry from the summer she was nineteen: She is gone. I sat in the sauna all night.

"He would have," she says. "He just couldn't get there himself."

They sit at the kitchen table with the documents between them and the afternoon light fading outside the window, and she thinks that she has been in this town for nearly three weeks and has somehow, without planning to, found a person to carry this with her.

That is more than her father had.

That is more than Toivo had.

She gathers the documents carefully and puts them back in the oilcloth and ties the cord. She will return them to the root cellar for now. They are not hers to keep and not hers to throw away.

"Stay for dinner," she says.

Chapter 15

Spring Coming

It has been five weeks since her father died in his kitchen making coffee, and in those five weeks nobody has kept the watch.

She has been in the sauna twice — once to understand it, once to speak a name into the dark — but she has not done what her father did. Has not come down the hill every week, lit the stove, opened the hatch, sat in the heat and let whatever is below know that someone was still paying attention. The watch her great-grandfather started in 1919 and her grandfather maintained and her father kept for thirty years ended the morning Hank Hietala didn't show up at Hakala's and Paavo Maki let himself in through the unlocked back door.

She has felt the difference. The footprints every night. The knocking. The cold in the floor.

Whatever patience Mindemoyen had, a hundred years of it, is running out.

The forecast comes in on a Saturday — unseasonable warmth pushing up from the south, temperatures climbing into the high thirties by the middle of the week, possibly touching forty by Thursday. The meteorologist on the Iron Mountain station says it's the warmest March stretch on record for the region, the earliest thaw they've seen in forty years. He seems pleased about this.

She watches the forecast on her father's television and feels something cold move through her that has nothing to do with the temperature outside.

She goes to the basement and pulls the last five years of her father's ledgers from the shelf. She sits at the kitchen table and reads the March entries across those years, looking for what she expects to find.

It's there. Every year, in the entries from the third week of March onward, a note on ice thickness. The numbers decline across the years. The ice that ran twenty-four, twenty-six inches through early March is running eighteen, sixteen, fourteen. Last March he'd recorded twelve inches at the deep end and noted in the margin: thinner than I have ever seen it this time of year.

This March, if the forecast holds, there may not be safe ice at all by the end of the week.

She calls Mikko first.

"Have you seen the forecast?" she asks when he picks up.

A pause. "Yeah," he says. "I was going to call you."

"The ice is going to go early. Maybe by the weekend."

"I know." She can hear him thinking. "You need to call Louise."

She calls Louise. She tells her about the forecast, about the ledger entries, about the pattern she's seen across five years of her father's careful records. Louise listens without interrupting.

"Wednesday," Louise says when she's done. "We'll come Wednesday. That's as early as I can arrange it."

Four days.

She thinks four days is manageable.

She is wrong.

—

The first night — Saturday into Sunday — the knocking starts at midnight and does not stop.

Three knocks. A pause of exactly the same length each time. Three knocks again. She lies in the dark in her childhood bed and counts them. After fifty sets she gets up and goes to the kitchen and writes the number down and keeps counting. Three knocks, pause, three knocks, pause. She times the pause — seven seconds, exactly, every time. The precision of it is more frightening than randomness would be. This is not a sound the house is making. This is not ice. This is something counting, something that knows she is listening and wants her to understand it is not going to stop.

By three in the morning she has counted one hundred and twelve sets of three. She stops counting. She sits at the kitchen table with all the lights on and her coat over her shoulders and waits for dawn.

The knocking does not stop until the sky begins to lighten, sometime after six. Then it stops, mid-sequence, between the second and third knock of a set, as though something had been interrupted rather than finished.

She makes coffee. Her hands are steady. She is surprised by this.

She calls Louise.

"It's escalating," she says, when Louise picks up. "The knocking. All night. It didn't stop."

A silence on the line. "How many nights until Wednesday?"

"Three."

Another silence. "I'll see what we can do about moving it up."

"Can you?"

"I don't know. I'll try." Louise's voice is measured but underneath the measure there is something Aino hasn't heard from her before. Something that might be urgency. "Don't go to the sauna. Don't go near the water. Keep the lights on at night if you need to."

"Okay."

"Call Mikko. Don't be alone."

She calls Mikko. He comes over and they sit at the kitchen table and drink coffee and she tells him about the counting and he listens without telling her she imagined it. He goes home at noon and comes back at dusk and she is glad to see him both times in a way she doesn't try to qualify.

That night the knocking comes again at midnight, and again it does not stop, and again she counts. One hundred and forty-four sets of three before she stops counting. She and Mikko sit in the kitchen with the lights blazing and she tells him about the ledgers and he tells her about his grandmother and they talk until nearly four in the morning because talking keeps the knocking at a remove, keeps it from being the only thing in the room.

It stops at six-seventeen. Between the second and third knock again.

—

Sunday into Monday the knocking comes earlier. Ten-thirty. She is not yet in bed. She is sitting at the kitchen table when it starts and there is something about hearing it while upright, while awake and functional, that is worse than hearing it from bed — it removes the last possibility that she is dreaming it or half-dreaming it or in some altered state that would explain it. She is sitting at the kitchen table with her coffee and the knock comes once, twice, the pause, once, twice, and then she realizes it is not coming from the floor of the kitchen.

It is coming from the back door.

She goes very still.

Three knocks on the back door. The pause. Three more. She is eight feet from the door. She can see the door. It is locked — she checked the locks an hour ago, she checks them every night now — and there is nothing on the other side of the door's small window except the dark porch and the dark yard.

She does not go to the door.

She calls Mikko. He picks up on the second ring and she can tell from his voice that he was awake. "It's at the back door," she says.

He is there in twelve minutes. He does not go to the door either. They sit in the kitchen and the knocking continues at the door, three and pause and three, until nearly two in the morning. Then it moves.

She hears it move — a shift, a transition, and then it is coming from the floor again, from below, from the hatch in the sauna that she can feel even from here, the distance between the kitchen and the lake collapsing in her mind until the house and the water feel like the same place.

She thinks about her father timing it in the dark. Thirty years of this, alone. She had thought the watching was a kind of vigilance. She understands now that it was also a kind of endurance that she has no reference point for, no framework, no word for what it costs a person to do that, season after season, decade after decade, without anyone to call at midnight.

She reaches across the table and grips Mikko's arm. He puts his hand over hers.

They wait for dawn.

—

Monday the temperature climbs to thirty-one and the snow on the south side of the house begins to melt in earnest, running off the roof in a steady drip that she can hear from the kitchen. She goes down to the shore in the afternoon — not to the sauna, she hasn't been back since the burning decision, she stays well clear of it — and looks at the ice.

It is changing.

There are cracks running along the surface that weren't there three days ago, not at the inlet where she'd expected them but in the main body of the lake, in a pattern that doesn't look like natural ice movement. Natural cracks run with the prevailing stress of the surface, with the wind and the temperature gradients, in roughly parallel lines. These cracks run in a different pattern — radial, from a point she can't quite identify, about thirty feet out from the sauna. Thirty feet out. The X on her father's hand-drawn map.

She stands at the shore and looks at the cracks.

The fish houses that were visible from the shore when she arrived — three of them, belonging to people from neighboring towns — are gone. She doesn't know when they were moved. She hopes they were moved by their owners and not by something else. She does not let herself think about this too carefully.

The lake is making sounds. Not the deep resonant crack she heard her first night, the natural sound of ice under thermal stress. These are different. Closer to the surface. A series of smaller sounds, almost rhythmic, almost deliberate, coming from under the ice near the sauna. She listens to them for a long time before she makes herself go back to the house.

She calls Louise again.

"Wednesday is as early as I can do it," Louise says. "We'll be there at first light."

"Something is happening to the ice. The cracks —"

"I know." A pause. "It knows what's coming. It has been waiting for this for a long time and it knows." Another pause. "That's why you need to hold on until Wednesday. Can you do that?"

"Yes," she says.

She says it with more certainty than she feels, but she says it, and as she says it she finds she means it more than she did a moment ago. She is the last Hietala. She is not going to be the one who breaks before Wednesday.

—

Monday night the cold comes inside.

She notices it at eleven — a drop in temperature that shouldn't be possible because the furnace is running, she can hear it, the familiar kick and blow of it. But the floor of the back bedroom, the room that faces the lake, is cold in a way that has nothing to do with the furnace — a cold that comes up through the floorboards, through her wool socks, settling in her feet and climbing. She goes and gets the small thermometer from the bathroom cabinet and she walks through the house with it.

Front room: sixty-eight degrees. Hallway: sixty-four. Her bedroom: sixty-one. The back bedroom: fifty-two. The kitchen: fifty-eight and dropping as she stands there. The back door: she holds the thermometer near the floor at the base of the door and watches the number fall. Forty-seven. Forty-four.

She goes back to the front room and calls Mikko and tells him about the thermometer.

"Come to my place," he says immediately.

"I'm not leaving."

A pause. "Aino —"

"I'm the last Hietala," she says. "I'm not leaving the house." She is surprised by how clearly she means this. "Come here instead."

He comes. He brings a sleeping bag and his own thermometer and they compare readings from different rooms through the night. The cold is worst between midnight and three — the back bedroom drops to forty-one degrees, cold enough to see their breath, cold enough that the window develops a rim of ice on the inside of the glass. The furnace runs continuously and makes no difference.

The knocking is in the floor all night, from the direction of the lake, and the cold is in the floor all night too, and she sits in the front room wrapped in her grandmother's quilt with Mikko beside her and she does not leave and she counts the hours until Wednesday.

—

Tuesday. The snowmobiler.

Mikko gets the call just after eight. A man named Dale Numminen from the next township went out on his snowmobile Monday night and didn't come home. His machine was found at the inlet, engine still running, no sign of the rider.

She knows before Mikko tells her the rest. She knows the way she knows other things now, below thought, below language.

"The inlet ice," she says.

"They're saying it was an accident. Thin ice at the inlet, unstable. He didn't know the lake." Mikko's voice is careful and flat. "He didn't know any of it."

She sits with this. Dale Numminen. A name she will carry now, the way the Hietalas have been carrying Mindemoyen's name without knowing it for a hundred years. An innocent person caught in the gravity of something ancient and furious and aimed at specific targets but not precise enough, not after a hundred years of pressure building with nowhere to go.

"She's not aiming for him," Aino says. "She's not aiming for anyone now. She's just — " She stops. "It's been a hundred years. She's been in that lake for a hundred years and the watch is broken and she's coming up and she's not — she doesn't know how to — "

She stops again. She looks out the window at the yard, the snow heavy and wet in the warming air.

"She's in pain," she says. "That's what this is. She's in pain and anger is the only shape it has anymore."

Mikko doesn't say anything. He doesn't disagree.

She goes to the sauna that afternoon. Not inside — she stands outside the door with her hand flat against the cedar wall. The wood is cold. Below her feet, through the frozen ground, she can feel something she has no word for except attention — the sense of being known and observed and counted, of being the last name on a list a hundred years old.

"Tomorrow," she says to the wall. "Hold on until tomorrow."

She doesn't know if it helps. She doesn't know if anything helps. But she needs to say it, to acknowledge it, to refuse the hundred-year silence one more time.

She goes back to the house. She and Mikko sit up again, wrapped in the quilt together in the front room, the cold at bay for now, the knocking below them steady as a clock.

She does not sleep.

Tomorrow Louise comes.

Chapter 16

The Other Two Families

She and Mikko go to the Lehtinen house Sunday afternoon, while the knocking from the night before is still present in her body, in the specific way that a sound you've heard all night doesn't leave when it stops but settles into you and keeps going at a frequency only you can feel.

Marta Lehtinen answers the door. A woman in her sixties, heavyset, careful eyes. Her brother Juhani is at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee and the expression of a man who has been told something he didn't want to know and is still deciding how to hold it.

Marta makes coffee. They sit around the kitchen table and Aino tells them everything. Not the careful version, not the version she'd told before that left out the worst of it — she tells them about the knocking, about the cold in the floor, about the cracks in the ice. She tells it plainly because there is no time left for careful.

When she finishes the kitchen is very quiet.

Juhani sets his cup down. "What are you asking us to do."

"Stand at the shore on Wednesday. That's all."

He looks at her and she can see him deciding something, running calculations she isn't privy to. "My grandfather is dead," he says. "He's been dead for thirty years. He never told me what he did — my father told me. My grandfather died without ever saying a word about it to anyone." He pauses. "I've spent thirty years being angry at a dead man. Which is a very particular kind of useless."

"Juhani," Marta says quietly.

"I'm not refusing," he says. To his sister and then to Aino. "I'm just saying." He picks up his coffee again. "Wednesday. We'll be there."

—

The Saari property she leaves until Tuesday morning, when Mikko comes by with the news about Dale Numminen and the conversation she has been dreading is now urgent. Mikko drives. On the way he tells her what he knows about Erik Saari — seventies, retired, lived alone since his wife died four years ago, keeps mostly to himself, has a way of looking at the lake that Mikko has always understood without having the words for it.

Erik is outside splitting wood when they pull up, in the methodical way of a man who has split a great many cords, and he watches them park with the same stillness. But when he sees Aino get out of the truck something changes in his face. Not fear exactly. More like recognition.

He has felt the cold too. She can see it.

He puts the axe down and they go inside and she tells it again, the full version, and he listens with his hands flat on his knees and his eyes on a point past her shoulder, and when she gets to the knocking he closes his eyes briefly and she knows he has heard it too from wherever he sleeps on the north end of this lake.

When she finishes he is quiet for a long time.

"No," he says.

She waits.

"I'm not going to stand on that shore," he says. "I'm not going to stand there and — perform something. My grandfather is dead. What he did is done. Standing on a shore doesn't undo it."

"Nobody said it undoes it," Mikko says carefully.

"Then what does it do?"

The room is very quiet. Outside the bare birches are perfectly still. On the lake, visible through the window, the ice surface is marked with the radial cracks she'd seen from her own shore — they run this far north too, further than she'd realized.

"A man died yesterday," Aino says. "Dale Numminen. From Tioga Township. He wasn't connected to any of this. He just — crossed the wrong water." She looks at Erik steadily. "She's not contained anymore. My father kept the watch for thirty years and now he's dead and nobody has been sitting in that sauna and she's been coming up every night and she is going to keep coming up. Wednesday is the only thing that stops it."

Erik looks at her. Then he looks out the window at the lake.

"She's been louder," he says quietly. "The past week. I can hear — something, from my side. I thought I was imagining it." He pauses. "I haven't slept well in a week."

"Neither have I," she says.

He looks at his hands. He is a big man, still solid despite his age, the kind of man who has dealt with hard things by dealing with them physically — the wood splitting, the maintained property, the life lived in deliberate order. She can see him trying to locate this thing in a framework that doesn't have room for it.

"What does it mean," he says slowly. "Standing there."

"It means not looking away," Mikko says. "It means your family was there and you're the one who's left and you show up. That's all. Whatever Louise Swifthawk's family does — that's theirs. We just stand there and we don't look away."

Erik is quiet for a very long time. Long enough that she thinks he's going to say no again, going to stand up and indicate the meeting is over.

Then he says: "What time."

"First light," she says. "Wednesday."

He nods once. It is not a warm nod. It is the nod of a man who has looked at something he's been avoiding for fifty years and decided, with great difficulty, to stop avoiding it.

"I'll be there," he says.

—

In the truck driving back the relief she expected doesn't come. Instead there is just the weight of Wednesday arriving — the warming air, the sound of melt running off every roof they pass, the sky pressing down low and gray. Somewhere in a funeral home in the next county someone is telling Dale Numminen's family what happened.

"One more night," Mikko says.

"One more night," she says.

She looks out the window at the lake as they pass along the western shore. The cracks in the ice are visible even from the road, running out from the center in lines that have no business being there in March. The surface of the lake has the look of something that has been still for too long and is about to stop being still.

She thinks about her father. All the nights he drove this road in the dark, going down to the sauna, going to sit in the heat above the water, keeping the thing below the surface aware of his presence, his name, his attendance. Thirty years of it. Alone.

She thinks: I am not alone.

She looks over at Mikko. He is watching the road.

She looks back at the lake.

One more night.

Chapter 17

The Last Night

Tuesday into Wednesday. The worst night.

The cold comes at nine o'clock, before she is even in bed, before the dark is fully settled. It comes through the floor the way it has the past two nights but stronger now, and it comes through the walls too, from the direction of the lake, and the thermometer in the back bedroom reads thirty-nine degrees with the furnace running full and the front room is fifty-one and dropping.

She and Mikko are in the kitchen when it starts. She watches him notice it — the slight stiffening, the look of a man registering something his body knows before his mind catches up. He looks at her. She looks at him.

"It knows tomorrow is Wednesday," she says.

The knocking begins at nine-fifteen. Not from the floor this time — from every direction. The floor and the walls and, for the first time, the ceiling, three knocks in the floor and then three from the east wall and then three from above, rotating, so that the house itself seems to be a drum being played from inside. She has not told anyone about the ceiling. She does not tell anyone now. She just turns another light on and puts the kettle on and stands in the kitchen and breathes.

At ten she calls Louise.

"Tonight is bad," she says. "It's in the walls. The ceiling."

Louise is quiet for a long moment. "She knows we're coming," she says finally. "This is the last time she can — " Louise stops. Starts again. "She has been waiting for a hundred years. What she's doing now is what she has always been doing. It's just that there's no one sitting in the sauna keeping the weight of it down."

"I know," Aino says.

"You need to stay in the house. Don't go outside. Don't go near the water. Whatever you hear — whatever you think you hear from outside — stay inside."

"Okay."

"First light," Louise says. "We'll be there."

At midnight the temperature in the kitchen is forty-six degrees and she and Mikko are wearing their coats inside the house. The knocking has been continuous for nearly three hours. She has stopped counting. She has stopped trying to think about it analytically. She just breathes and stays upright and waits.

At one in the morning something hits the back door.

Not a knock. A blow. A single impact that shakes the door in its frame and rattles the window above it and sends her two steps back before she knows she's moved. Mikko is on his feet. They stand in the kitchen looking at the door.

Silence. Then the knocking resumes, the measured three-and-pause, as though the blow had been an exception and the counting has resumed.

"She's angry," Aino says. Her voice is steadier than she expects. "She has every right to be angry."

"I know," Mikko says.

"Don't open the door."

"I know."

She stands in her kitchen at one in the morning in her coat in forty-six degree air with the knocking in the walls around her and she thinks about Eino writing kaunis in a notebook and underlining it and she thinks about Toivo's last ledger entry, perhaps he will not have to carry this, and she thinks about her father dialing the phone and hanging up before anyone answered, over and over, across the last year of his life.

"I should have come sooner," she says.

Mikko looks at her.

"Not just after he died. Years ago. I should have — " She stops. "He was waiting for me. He'd been waiting for years. And I was in Milwaukee not answering questions I hadn't let myself ask."

"You didn't know."

"I knew something was wrong. I've always known something was wrong with this lake and this house and this family. I just decided I was allowed to not know it officially." She looks at the back door. "Nineteen years."

She thinks: hold on.

The knocking in the walls continues but something has changed in it — the rhythm is the same but the quality is different, something she can't name, like a voice that has been shouting for hours and is starting to go hoarse. At five-thirty the knocking stops.

Between the second and third knock. Just like every morning.

She stands in the kitchen in the sudden silence and breathes.

Mikko says: "It's Wednesday."

"It's Wednesday," she says.

She drinks her coffee. She watches the light come. At six-fifteen she goes to the back door and unlocks it and opens it and looks out at the yard.

The snow has been disturbed in a radius around the entire house — not footprints, not the small deliberate prints she'd grown used to, but something different, the snow pushed away from the foundation in every direction as though something had been pressing against the house from outside all night. She looks at it for a long time.

She closes the door. She locks it again.

She has things to do before Louise comes. She goes through the house with a wooden box from the basement workshop. The documents from the root cellar — the land claim, the list of names, Eino's correspondence with the county. Toivo's four ledgers from the basement shelf. Down the hill in the gray pre-dawn, into the cold sauna, the lockbox under the bench, Eino's notebook. Back up the hill with the box growing heavier.

He opens his eyes. He looks at her. He sits up.

They go. She brings the box.

The lake is wrong in the early light. The cracks she'd seen the day before have spread overnight — the whole surface of the lake is webbed with them now, lines running in every direction from the point thirty feet out from the sauna, so that the ice looks less like ice and more like something that has been holding its shape through effort and is close to stopping. The surface is a pale gray-white in the morning light and it is moving, subtly, in a way ice does not move — a slow pulse, almost breathing, as though whatever is below it has been pressing against it all night.

She is not alone this time — there are two vehicles, and when they park and the people get out she counts seven, where last time there were four. She watches them come down the slope toward the shore, moving with a quiet purposefulness that tells her they have talked about this, prepared for this, know what they are here to do.

Louise comes to stand beside her at the water's edge. She looks at the ice. She looks at the sauna.

She says something in her language that Aino doesn't understand, quiet and brief.

Then she looks at Aino.

"Are you ready?" she asks.

Aino looks at the lake. At the cracked and breathing surface, the point thirty feet out, the place where her great-grandfather put stones and a body and a hundred years of silence.

"Yes," she says.

And it begins.

Chapter 18

The Ceremony

The Lehtinens arrive first, Marta and Juhani together, Marta's face tight and pale. Erik Saari comes down the slope alone, hands in his pockets, and takes a position a few feet from the others without speaking. They are eight people in the early light on the shore of a lake that is not behaving like a lake, and none of them speak for a moment.

Louise looks at the assembled group — at Mikko, at the Lehtinens, at Erik. Something moves through her expression. She says to them, quietly: "Thank you for being here."

Aino has a wooden box at her feet, brought down from the house. Eino's notebook from the basement shelf, where she'd put it back after reading it. Toivo's four ledgers. The documents from the root cellar — the land claim, the list of names, the correspondence with the county. Things her family kept that were never theirs to keep. She had not slept anyway. She had spent the hour before dawn going room to room, gathering them.

Louise looks at the box. She nods once. The younger man steps forward and picks it up and sets it carefully near the vehicles.

Then she turns to her family and speaks in her language and they move.

—

What they do Aino watches from twenty feet back, beside Mikko, and she watches it as carefully as she has watched anything in her life. She is not going to look away. She promised herself that in the kitchen at three in the morning while the temperature dropped and the door shook, and she keeps the promise now.

There are words spoken. There are things brought from the vehicles — she doesn't look at them directly, that feels like a trespass — and placed at the water's edge with care. There is a quality to what Louise's family is doing that she has no language for. It is not performance. It is not ritual in the theatrical sense. It is more like — she searches for it — it is more like the way her father sat in the sauna. The committed presence of it. The being-there that is its own form of speech.

The lake responds.

Not gradually. Immediately, the moment Louise's uncle begins to speak, the surface of the lake changes. The pulse she had noticed — the slow breathing quality of the ice — intensifies. The cracks spread visibly in the early light, branching outward from the center point, and the sound the ice makes is not the sound ice makes. It is deeper. More intentional. As though the lake itself is answering.

Then the cold comes off the water.

She has felt this cold for the past nights inside the house. Out here at the shore it is a different thing entirely — not temperature, not weather, but the cold of the lake bottom, the cold of deep water in early March, the cold of the place where stones were placed in 1919 and have not moved since. It comes off the surface in a wave and hits her full in the face and she takes an involuntary step back.

Mikko grabs her arm.

She takes the step back and then she takes a step forward. She plants her feet at the shore and she stands there.

The cold intensifies. It is in her chest now, the sensation of breathing underwater, her lungs registering the wrong element, and she knows this is not physical — she is standing on solid ground, she is breathing air, the temperature at the shore is twenty-eight degrees — but knowing this does not make it less real. She can feel the weight of the water above her as though she is thirty feet down, in the dark, with stones.

She is letting me feel it, Aino thinks. She is letting me feel what it was.

She doesn't run. She breathes through it. It is the worst thing she has ever felt and she breathes through it and keeps her feet at the shore.

Beside her she can hear Mikko breathing deliberately, the way you breathe when you are keeping yourself from reacting to something. She doesn't look at him. She keeps her eyes on the ice.

The ice is moving now in a way she can't explain and won't try to. The surface shifts and settles and the cracks open and close and the light on it does something the light isn't doing on anything else — a quality she has no word for, a depth in it, as though the ice is not two feet thick but two hundred, as though what she is looking at is not the surface but the bottom.

Louise's uncle speaks for a long time. His voice is low and steady and does not waver, and the lake responds to it, she can feel the response, the quality of the presence below the surface shifting incrementally from — she searches for the word — from focused to something more diffuse, as though the beam of something very concentrated is slowly widening.

Then it focuses again. On her.

She feels it before she sees anything — a shift in the pressure of the cold, a direction, the sense of something that has been distributed across the whole surface of the lake gathering itself toward the shore. Toward the sauna. Toward her.

She is the last Hietala. She is what is left of the family that did this. She is standing at the edge of the water that held a woman's body for a hundred years and she is the last person whose name is on that debt and the presence below the ice knows her the way it has always known her name and it is done waiting.

The cold hits her like a wall.

She goes to one knee. She doesn't decide to — her legs simply stop supporting her, the weight of the cold and the pressure and the sensation of water that is not there overwhelming her body's certainty that she is standing on land. Her knee hits the snow at the water's edge. She is eight inches from the ice.

Mikko has her by both arms. She can feel that. She can feel the warmth of his hands through her coat.

"Aino," he says. Quietly. Steadily.

She looks at the ice. Eight inches away. The cracks are open here, the surface no longer smooth, and through the gaps she can see the water below — dark and cold and moving, something moving in it that she can see clearly now without the distance of a hatch between them, closer than she has ever been.

She is looking at the shape of a woman.

Not clearly. Not in the way you see a person standing in a lit room. The way you see a figure through deep water, through a hundred years of cold, the outline of a person who was a person and has become something else but has not forgotten what she was. The shape of arms. The shape of a face turned upward.

Looking at her.

The weight is enormous. The cold is in her lungs, her hands, the back of her eyes. She is being held at the water's edge by Mikko's hands and she is also, she understands, being held at the water's edge by something else — a hundred years of family gravity, the debt that landed on her because she is the last, the weight of Eino and Toivo and her father all arriving simultaneously in her chest like a wave.

She opens her mouth.

"Mindemoyen," she says.

Her voice comes out wrong — thin, strained, the voice of someone who is very cold or very frightened or both. She doesn't try again from that register. She breathes. She feels Mikko's hands on her arms. She breathes again.

"Mindemoyen," she says again. Her own voice this time.

The pressure does not ease. But it — attends. The sense of something waiting.

She thinks about what she needs to say. Not what she has rehearsed, because she hasn't rehearsed this — she hadn't known this moment was coming in this form, on her knees at the water's edge with her hands going numb and a shape moving in the deep water below her. She thinks about what is true. She thinks about what has not been said in a hundred years.

"What my family did to you was wrong," she says. "What Eino did. What all four of them did. It was wrong and they knew it was wrong and they chose silence instead of — " She stops. She starts again. "My grandfather tried to find another way and didn't know how. My father tried to find another way and didn't know how. I don't know if I know how either. But Louise is here. Her family is here. And I am telling you — out loud, at this lake, so that whatever is left of you that can hear me hears me — that you deserved better than every single one of us."

The cold peaks.

For a moment that she will never be able to accurately describe she is completely under — not physically, she is on her knees in the snow, but in every other sense she is at the bottom of the lake in the dark with the stones and the hundred years and the cold is everything and there is no surface and there is no air — and then Mikko says her name again and the sound of it, the specific ordinary human sound of her own name in his voice, pulls her back like a rope.

She is on her knees at the shore. She is breathing. Her hands are on the ice — she has put her hands on the ice without knowing it, palms flat, and the cold is going through her palms and up her arms.

She lifts her hands. She sits back on her heels.

The pressure eases.

Not gone. She does not think it will ever be entirely gone, not today, not yet, perhaps not ever — these things don't resolve cleanly, Louise said so, she has always understood that. But it eases. The cold at the shore retreats to something that is merely the cold of a March morning. The sensation in her lungs becomes ordinary breath. The light on the ice returns to the ordinary pale gray of winter.

She is aware that Louise is standing beside her. She doesn't know when Louise moved from the water's edge to here.

"Can you stand up?" Louise asks.

She can. She does, with Mikko's help. She stands at the shore of the lake and her legs are shaking but they are holding and she looks at the ice.

The cracks are still there but they are not moving anymore. The surface of the lake is still. Whatever pulse had animated it is quieter now — not absent, she can still feel the weight of what is in the water, she thinks she will always be able to feel it — but quieter.

In the silence Louise says the words she said last time: "We see you. We have always seen you. We are here."

And then, this time, something Aino has not heard her say before. Quietly, in her own language, brief.

Aino doesn't understand the words. She understands what they mean.

You are not forgotten.

The ice does not crack open. The body does not rise. There is no dramatic resolution, no definitive end. There is a long silence at the shore, eight people standing in the March cold, and the lake, and whatever is in it, and the morning.

Then Louise says to Aino: "The sauna."

"Yes," Aino says. "Now."

Chapter 19

What Her Father Wrote at the End

She does not go inside the house first this time.

She goes directly to the sauna. She opens the door. She goes in.

The cold in here is the same cold as outside — the kiuas has been out for days, the cedar walls hold no memory of heat. She stands in the center of the room and looks at the carvings on the walls, the band of marks her great-grandfather began and her grandfather continued and her father maintained, running around the room at the same height, the height a seated man would be at, the height of someone sitting and watching.

She goes to the stove. She opens the firebox.

She thinks about her father at fourteen years old in the photograph, slightly turned, as though he was already leaving. Four months before his father drowned. A boy who did not ask to inherit this and inherited it anyway.

She thinks about the entry in March of last year: I am running out of time to look.

She thinks about the entry in May: I should have told her the summer she found the ledger and I screamed at her and she left. That was the worst thing I ever did.

She thinks about Father's Day. Eleven minutes. He timed every call. She wonders how many minutes there were across nineteen years of calls. She did the math once — if there were four calls a year and they averaged ten minutes, that was forty minutes a year, seven hundred and sixty minutes across nineteen years, twelve hours. Twelve hours of her father's voice in nineteen years.

She builds the fire.

Paper, kindling, the first log — the way he taught her when she was six, the way she has done it twice now in this sauna, the way she will never do again after this.

She lights it.

She leaves the firebox door open.

She does not leave.

She has told herself she would build the fire and walk away, and now that she is here she finds she cannot. She stands in the cold sauna and watches the fire catch and she thinks: I owe him this. Not sentimentality. Witness. He sat in this room for thirty years and no one watched him do it and she is going to stand here and watch the fire take the room he built until she can't stand there anymore.

The cedar burns fast. She knew it would — the wood is old and dry, forty years of kiuas heat drawn into the walls, and when the fire comes out of the firebox and finds the wall beside it the wall goes up like it has been waiting. She backs toward the door. The heat is immediate and serious, the real heat of a structure fire, nothing like the heat of the sauna in use, and she backs out through the door and stands outside and watches through the open door.

The carvings go first. The band of marks running around the room, her great-grandfather's hand and her grandfather's hand and her father's hand in the wood, three generations of witnessing in a language she never learned to read — the fire finds them and they are gone in under a minute, and she watches them go and she does not look away.

The lauteet goes. The bench where she sat, the platform above it where she'd lain in the heat, the floor below it where the hatch was — the hatch over the water, the hatch her father had told her not to open, the one she opened anyway. All of it goes, and the fire finds the gap and the cold air from the lake below comes up through the opening and feeds it, and the sauna burns from the inside out and from the bottom up simultaneously and the smoke rising is dense and white against the gray sky.

She is crying.

She doesn't know when she started. She becomes aware of it gradually, the way you become aware of rain — you are dry and then you are wet and you can't say when the transition happened. Her face is wet and the heat from the burning sauna is drying it as fast as it comes and she stands in the snow twelve feet from the fire and cries for her father, for thirty years of coming down this hill alone, for the coffee he made every morning in the same percolator, for the eleven minutes on Father's Day, for the ledger entry that said I am tired.

She cries for Toivo, who wanted to find another way and found the lake instead.

She cries for Eino, who wrote kaunis and underlined it and built something terrible over something he couldn't face.

She cries for the first Aino, who looked at her husband for a long time and did not speak and carried it for forty-three years.

She cries for Mindemoyen. She cries for a woman she never met and never can meet and whose name she has only been carrying for a few weeks and whose family has been carrying it for a hundred years.

She does not cry for herself. She thinks maybe that comes later. She thinks she has a great deal of crying for herself still to do and today is not that day.

Mikko comes to stand beside her. He doesn't put his arm around her or say anything. He just stands there, the way he has been standing there through all of this, his presence its own form of speech.

The roof goes. The walls come down in stages. The stone foundation holds, the fieldstone that Eino set in 1919 and that is not going anywhere — it will stand at the water's edge after the snow melts and into the spring and beyond, just stones, just a rectangle of old mortared rock that the weather will slowly work on. That is fine. That is what stones do.

By the time the structure is down to coals and stone the sky has lightened fully and the morning is well underway and she has stopped crying and her face is dry and tight from the heat and the cold both.

Louise comes to stand beside her on the other side.

For a long time no one says anything.

Then Louise says: "Your father knew my name."

Aino looks at her. "He found it in the last year. He wrote it in his ledger. He tried to call."

"I know," Louise says. "I had a message. A voicemail, from a number I didn't recognize, no name, no words. Just breathing, for about twenty seconds, and then it hung up." She pauses. "I knew who it was. I didn't know how to call back." A pause. "I should have."

Aino looks at the coals. "He should have stayed on the line."

"Yes," Louise says. "He should have."

They stand together at the water's edge, at the ruined foundation of the thing that was between them, in the March cold, and the smoke rises and the lake is still and somewhere below the ice, in thirty feet of dark water, something has been heard after a hundred years of silence.

It is not over. Louise said so and Aino knows it.

But it has begun.

Chapter 20

The Lid Comes Off

The sauna burns for three hours.

It goes faster than she expected — the cedar is old and dry despite the moisture of its location, and the kiuas stones hold heat long after the wood around them has gone, and the structure comes down in stages, the roof first, then the walls, then the frame, until by noon there is nothing left but the stone foundation at the water's edge and a column of smoke rising into the gray sky.

Louise and the others had come back to watch. They stood at the shore, not far from where they'd stood before, and they watched the fire without speaking. At some point Louise's uncle said something quietly to the younger man and the younger man nodded. At some point Marta Lehtinen cried, briefly, and Juhani put his arm around her.

At some point Erik Saari walked down to stand at the edge of the foundation, as close to the fire as the heat would allow, and stood there for a long time with his hands at his sides. She didn't know what he was doing. She thought maybe he didn't either.

By the time it's down to coals and stone it's mid-afternoon and the temperature has climbed to thirty-four degrees, the warmest it's been since she arrived. Melt is running from the roof of the house, a steady drip from the eaves. The snow in the yard is heavy and wet.

Louise comes to find her at the kitchen window.

"We're going to go," she says. "We'll come back. There are other things we need to do here, at the shore. Not today."

"Whenever you want," Aino says. "I'll leave a key with Mikko for when I'm not here."

"Eventually. I don't know exactly when."

"He did what he could," she says.

"Yes," Louise says. "That's usually how it goes."

—

"How do you feel?" Mikko asks.

She thinks about it honestly. "Empty," she says. "Not bad empty. Just — like something that was full isn't anymore."

She thinks about this house. Forty years of her father's life in it. The soup bowls in the right place and the mugs moved. The percolator on the counter.

"I need to figure out what's next," she says.

"Yeah," Mikko says. He is looking at his coffee cup. "Take your time with it."

She looks at him. He doesn't look up. She thinks there are things he wants to say and is choosing not to, and she appreciates the choosing.

She hopes, without knowing if hope is the right word for it, that whatever is in the lake is freer than it was this morning.

She thinks of the silence after Louise said we see you, the quality of the air in that long quiet moment, and she thinks: yes. Something changed. She can't prove it and she won't try to.

Some things you just carry.

She gets up and puts the kettle on for tea and the afternoon settles into evening around them and the house is warm and the smoke is gone and the lake outside is still.

Chapter 21

After

She gives herself a week.

The house needs to be gone through properly — not the frantic triage of the first few days but the real work, the kind that requires decision rather than just sorting. What goes with her, what goes to the Lutheran church thrift store, what goes in the dumpster she rents from a place in Iron Mountain that delivers it on a Thursday morning without comment.

The furniture she leaves for the estate sale, which Ernest Autio arranges through a woman in Marquette who does this for a living. The woman comes on a Friday and walks through the house with a clipboard, assigning values to things Aino has been looking at for five weeks without quite seeing. Her father's chair. The bookshelf of westerns. The kitchen table where she has eaten most of her meals since she arrived. The woman says the table is worth something, it's old, good wood. Aino tells her to sell it.

She keeps three things from the main house. The quilt her grandmother made her, which she folds and puts in the back seat of the Subaru. The photograph of herself at eight with the fish, which she wraps in a dish towel. And the percolator, which she wraps in a sweater and nestles into a box of kitchen things and then at the last minute takes out of the box and puts on the passenger seat, where she can see it.

The forty-two ledgers she goes back and forth on for days.

She reads through more of them — not all, she doesn't have time for all, but enough to understand the full shape of what they contain. Thirty years of her father's life in those pages. Thirty years of weather and ice and the other kind of entry, the ones that sound like a man talking to something he can't name. They are, she thinks, the most honest thing he ever wrote. More honest than the phone calls, more honest than the note he left in the envelope, because they were written without an audience, for no one, just a man and a lake and the thing in it and the words he used to describe the watching.

She wraps them carefully in plastic sheeting from the basement and packs them in a box and puts the box in the Subaru. She will figure out later whether they belong with Louise or with her or somewhere else. She's not ready to decide and she's learned, in the past few weeks, to trust that instinct.

The stone foundation at the water's edge she leaves alone. The ash and debris from the fire she leaves for weather and time. In spring, when the ice goes out, Louise and her family will come back. They said so. She has left a key with Mikko.

She has a conversation with Ernest about the house. She is selling it below market because she wants it gone quickly and because market in Kivijärvi in early spring is not a number that requires much deliberation. There is a couple from downstate — Marquette, which barely counts as downstate up here, but close enough — who have been looking for a lake property and are willing to move fast. They have not been told anything about the sauna or the foundation or what happened here. Aino has thought about this and decided that what they don't know won't hurt them. The foundation is just stones. The lake is just a lake.

She hopes this is true.

She thinks it probably is, mostly.

—

Mikko comes over the last evening. She makes dinner — the last of what's in the refrigerator, a cobbled-together thing involving eggs and the end of a loaf of bread and some cheese — and they eat at the kitchen table, which will be someone else's kitchen table by the end of the month.

"You could stay," he says. Not for the first time, but differently this time — quieter, without the expectation of being argued with.

"I have a job," she says. "An apartment."

"I know."

She looks at him across the table. He is looking at his plate with the expression of a man who has said what he wanted to say and is not going to belabor it. She has come, in these past weeks, to understand this about him — the saying of the thing once, cleanly, and then the letting it be. She finds it, against her expectations, one of the things she likes most about him.

"I don't know what I'm going back to," she says. "I mean I know what's there. I just don't know if it's — " She stops. She doesn't have the right word. "I've been here for six weeks and I feel more like myself than I have in years. I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it right now," he says.

"No."

They finish dinner. She washes the dishes and he dries them, which is something they've fallen into without discussing it, and she is aware of the specific domesticity of it, two people in a kitchen, the ordinary rhythm of it. She thinks about the apartment in Bay View. The radiator that will start banging at four in the morning as soon as the temperature drops. The building that looks indifferent when she leaves.

She thinks about this house. What it felt like to pull into the driveway that first night, in the dark, her father's truck sitting there. What it felt like to put her hand on the cold percolator.

"I'll come back," she says. She says it to the dish she's washing, not to him.

"Okay," he says.

She hands him the dish. Their hands don't quite touch and then they do, briefly, the way accidental things happen, and neither of them says anything about it.

He leaves at nine. She stands in the doorway and watches his taillights go down the gravel road for what might be the last time and might not be.

She goes back inside. The kitchen is clean. The house is nearly empty. The percolator sits on the counter and she looks at it for a moment and then leaves it where it is for tonight.

She goes to bed in her childhood bedroom under her grandmother's quilt and listens to the house — which is not quiet, exactly, it never is, the furnace and the pines and the particular language of an old house settling into cold — and she falls asleep faster than she has since she arrived.

She does not dream.

In the morning she loads the Subaru. The percolator goes on the passenger seat. She locks the house and puts the key in the lockbox Ernest installed on the front porch. She stands in the driveway for a moment.

The lake is not visible from here. She can feel it anyway, the way she always could, the weight of that much cold water at the edge of the property, the particular quality of the air that comes off it. She has been able to feel it her whole life, she thinks. She just never let herself know what she was feeling.

She gets in the car. She drives down the gravel road. At the end of it she turns south, toward the highway, toward Milwaukee, toward the life that is waiting for her to decide what to do with it.

She passes the cross street that goes down to the lake.

She doesn't stop.

But she slows.

Chapter 22

She Does Not Get Far

Highway 2 to 45 to 51. The same road in reverse, the trees opening up as she crosses back into Wisconsin, the right-of-way widening, the towns getting larger and farther apart in a different way — not the emptying-out of the iron range but the filling-in of the lower half of the state, more houses, more traffic, more of everything that means she is going south.

The percolator rides on the passenger seat. She has wrapped it in her fleece jacket and buckled the seatbelt around it, which she did without thinking about it and noticed only after, and left it.

The radio is on. The basketball season is still going — it is only March, there is still basketball to be played — and she finds a game and lets it run. The same big unhurried voice. Something definite happening.

She drives through Ironwood. She drives through Hurley. She crosses back into Wisconsin and the road straightens and the sky opens up and she has the clear, clean feeling of a highway in front of her for a long distance.

She thinks about the six weeks. She lines them up in her mind the way she would line up the facts of a case at work, in order, each one leading to the next. The drive up. Hakala's. The house. The funeral. The will. The sauna. The ledgers. Watersmeet. The shore. The fire. She can see the shape of it now from the outside, which she couldn't while she was in it — the way each thing opened the next, the way she kept going one step further than she'd planned, the way the book she'd thought she was reading turned out to be a different book entirely.

She thinks about Mikko. The way he dried dishes without being asked. The way he said I'll come back after she said it, quietly, as though he were testing the weight of it.

She thinks about her father's ledger. I am running out of time to look.

She is thirty-eight. She has time.

The question is what she wants to do with it.

—

She stops for gas outside Wausau, the same stretch of highway she'd been on when the lawyer called, when she'd been on hold with Social Security and her whole life had changed direction without her permission. The same gas stations, the same flat white sky.

She fills the tank. She goes inside for coffee. She stands at the counter waiting for her change and becomes aware, gradually, of something she can't immediately name.

Not a sound. Not a feeling exactly. More like — a direction. A pull, faint but specific, the way you can feel a draft in a room without being able to say where it's coming from. It is coming from the northeast. She knows this without turning to check. She knows it the way she knew the lake was there even when she couldn't see it from the driveway, the weight of it, the presence.

She takes her change. She takes her coffee. She stands in the parking lot.

The highway is right there. South. Milwaukee is four hours. Her apartment, her job, the radiator that will start banging at four in the morning, the building that looks indifferent when she leaves it.

She gets in the car.

She pulls back onto the highway. The basketball game comes back on. She drives south and the pull is still there, specific and quiet, from the northeast, and she notes it the way her father would have noted it — in the language of observation, without interpretation, a fact to be recorded and returned to.

She drives for another twenty minutes.

There is an exit. She has not planned to take it. It goes northeast, toward a part of Wisconsin she has driven through but doesn't know well — lake country, small towns, a different kind of quiet than the iron range. She doesn't know why this exit. She doesn't know what is up that road.

She takes it.

The highway recedes behind her. The trees close in on either side, not the pines of the U.P. but hardwoods, bare-branched, gray and white in the March light. The road narrows. A lake appears through the trees on her right, frozen, the ice still holding at the edges, open water showing dark at the center.

She slows.

The pull is stronger here. Not urgent, not alarming — the same quality as the presence under the hatch in the sauna, the sense of something that has been waiting a long time and has the patience of something that has been waiting a long time.

She pulls over on the shoulder and sits with the engine running and looks at the lake through the trees.

She thinks about what Louise said. It's a beginning. Not an ending.

She thinks about her father's note. Maybe you will.

She thinks about what she said in the sauna the second time, to the cold and the heat and whatever was listening: I'm going to find out how to make it right.

She picks up her phone. She searches for the county. She searches for recorded land disputes, for townships, for families. She is a paralegal. She knows how to read a paper trail.

She finds something in ten minutes that takes her another ten to understand fully. A property on this lake, a deed dispute going back four generations, a family name that appears in county records in 1887 and then in different records in 1921 and the gap between those two dates is the shape of something she recognizes.

She puts the phone down.

She looks at the lake.

She will call Louise. She will ask if she knows this lake, this family, this name. She will find out if there is someone else who needs to know what she now knows how to look for.

She doesn't do any of that yet. She just sits in the car on the shoulder of a road she didn't plan to take and looks at a frozen lake she didn't know existed this morning, and she thinks about her father counting the minutes of phone calls, and her grandfather going to a pastor who didn't understand the question, and her great-grandfather writing kaunis in a notebook in 1919, beautiful, underlined.

She thinks: I can hear it now.

She doesn't say it out loud. She doesn't need to.

She puts the car in drive.

She does not go back to the highway.
