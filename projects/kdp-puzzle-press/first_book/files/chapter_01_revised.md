# Chapter 1
## The Drive Up

The radiator has been banging since four in the morning. Aino has been awake since four. By five-thirty she is dressed and packed and the Subaru is loaded and she is leaving an hour earlier than she meant to.

Tuesday, the eleventh of February. Twenty-six degrees. The sky is gray and low.

She backs out of the lot behind her building and waits at the light on Kinnickinnic for someone to honk at her so she can turn left without feeling rude about it. Nobody honks. The light changes. She turns. In the rearview the building looks the way it always looks when she leaves it for any length of time, which is indifferent.

The funeral is Thursday. She has packed for four days. The black dress is in a garment bag in the back. Two grocery bags of food — peanut butter, oatmeal, apples, a six-pack of bottled water, a sleeve of saltines — sit on the passenger seat. She does not trust the food at her father's house to still be edible. She has not been in his kitchen in nineteen years.

I-94 north. The Subaru moves forward without comment. She turns the radio on. Public radio, a story about something happening in Washington. She turns it off. Turns it back on. Turns it off. She drives in silence past the airport, past Bayshore, past the exit for her ex-husband's neighborhood, which she has been making a point of not noticing for five years.

By Fond du Lac the sky has gone from gray to the heavier gray that means snow. By Wausau the snow has started, fine and dry, blowing across the road in lines. She turns the wipers on the lowest setting and turns the radio back on. A man with a sports voice is calling a basketball game. She lets him.

Seven hours to her father's house. Six and a half if the weather holds and she does not stop. The town is Kivijärvi, Michigan, on the iron range. She has spent nineteen years correcting Wisconsinites who could not understand why she did not just say *Iron something.* It is pronounced *KEE-vee-yer-vee.* It means *stone lake.* The lake is at the edge of town. Her father's house is on the lake. Her father is dead.

She has been telling herself this for six days now and it has not yet acquired the texture of a real thing.

The lawyer called on a Wednesday afternoon while she was on hold with the Social Security Administration. She put the lawyer on hold to finish with Social Security. When she came back, he was still there.

*Your father has passed,* he said, in the voice older Midwesterners use for the worst possible information without using any of the words for it. *I'm very sorry, Aino.*

She said *thank you,* which was the wrong thing, and *when,* which was the right thing, and *how,* which she did not actually want answered but asked because not asking would have seemed strange. He died in the kitchen. He had been making coffee. The neighbor found him in the afternoon when he did not show up at the bar at his usual time. The neighbor's name was Toivo Maki. She had not thought of him in nineteen years and she remembered him completely — the smell of his pipe, the way he laughed.

*The funeral is Thursday,* the lawyer said. *Will you be coming?*

She said yes before she finished thinking about it. Then she sat at her desk for a long time looking at the form she had been filling out, a request for medical records on behalf of a man with rotator cuff damage who had not been able to lift his arms above his head since 2019. The form was due Friday. She finished it before she went home.

She is north of Tomahawk now. The snow has thickened. She has slowed to fifty and then to forty-five because the road is not yet plowed and the truck in front of her is doing forty. She thinks about her father in his kitchen. She thinks about whether the coffee was still warm when the neighbor came in. She thinks about whether he had still been using the percolator he had used for forty years or whether at some point he had bought one of the new machines, the kind with the pods, and the answer to that last question feels for a moment like the most important thing in her life. She does not know what kind of coffee maker her father owned when he died. She does not know what kind he owned the last time she was in his kitchen, because the last time she was in his kitchen she was nineteen and he made her instant Folgers because she had asked for instant Folgers.

The truck takes the exit for Highway 8. She does not. She stays on 51. The snow eases up, the sky lightens half a shade, and she pushes the Subaru back to sixty.

She does not cry. She has not cried about her father since the lawyer called. She has cried at three different commercials and once during a podcast about an architect in Oslo. She does not know why this is.

—

She crosses into Michigan at four-eighteen in the afternoon, later than she meant to but not as late as she feared. Highway 51 has become 45 has become 2, and the sun is somewhere west of her and dropping fast in the way it drops in February at this latitude, which is to say all at once. The trees on either side are up close in the way they get in the U.P., the right-of-way narrow, the pines crowding it. She has the sensation she had as a child driving up here with her father, that the road is tunneling rather than passing through. The trees do not stand back. They lean in.

The basketball man has been talking for an hour. He has called the game, done the postgame, moved into the postgame analysis with another man who has the same voice and a slightly different opinion. She is not following. She is using the noise the way one uses a window fan.

The radio cuts out.

She glances at the dashboard. The display shows the station is still tuned. The volume is still up. The signal indicator shows full bars, which on this stretch of highway is wrong. She has known this road since she was a child. There has never been signal here.

The radio is silent. Not static. Not the dead-air hiss of a station gone off transmission. Silent. The way a room is silent when someone has stopped talking in it.

She does not touch the volume knob. She does not know why she does not touch the volume knob.

A half-mile later the basketball man comes back mid-sentence — *— and that's the kind of decision that loses you games in March* — as though he had never been gone.

She lets out a breath she had not known she was holding.

The next exit sign says WATERSMEET 11. She does not need Watersmeet. Watersmeet is forty minutes south of where she is going. She passes it. The dashboard clock ticks over to four-twenty-two.

The radio is fine now. The wipers are off. The road is clear. She drives.

—

She crosses into the iron range a little after five. The sky is the color of a bruise an hour old. The towns get smaller and farther apart and then there are no towns at all, only the road and the trees and the occasional driveway disappearing into the woods, marked by a green address sign on a metal pole and nothing else. She passes a bar called Bear's that she remembers from childhood. She passes a gas station she does not. She passes a place where the woods open briefly and there is a pit, water in the bottom — the pit of a mine that has been closed for thirty or forty or sixty years. Her father always called these by their company names. The *Cleveland-Cliffs.* The *Mather B.* The *Pewabic.*

The road has not changed. The pits have not changed. The trees, she suspects, have not changed.

Her father told her once — she was perhaps eight, in the back seat of his truck on a fall afternoon — that the white pines along this stretch had been here when his great-grandfather got off the boat in 1903. *They were already big then,* he said. *Eino said when he saw them he thought he had come to the wrong country.*

She has not thought about that sentence in twenty years.

The sign for Kivijärvi appears at six-forty-one. POPULATION 711. The 711 is on a different metal panel than the rest of the sign, hooked underneath. When she was young the panel said 1,400, and even then it had been bolted on. They have just changed the panel. Twice.

She slows to thirty-five.

The town opens around her the way it always did. The Lutheran church on the left. The Finnish Hall on the right, dark, no cars. The diner that was open from six to two. Hakala's, lit up, two trucks in the lot. The gas station. The cross street that goes down to the lake.

She does not turn down the cross street. She goes another quarter mile and turns left onto a gravel road that has been freshly plowed, the snow piled high on either side. She follows it for half a mile until the road bends and the house comes into view.

It is the same house. She does not know why seeing it stops her breath.

She pulls into the driveway and kills the engine. The Subaru ticks. The pines around the house are completely still — no wind. The porch light is on. Her father's truck is in the driveway, where it has been parked, she now understands, since Wednesday. Nobody has moved it. Nobody is going to until she does.

She sits in the car for a long time.

She thinks: *I should have come last summer.*

She thinks: *I should have come ten years ago.*

She thinks: *He is in the funeral home in Iron Mountain. He is not here. There is no reason this is hard.*

She gets out of the car. The cold takes her by surprise the way the U.P. cold always does — not the temperature, twenty degrees and not even particularly cold for here, but the *quality* of it, the thinness, the sense of the woods having drawn the moisture out of the air and kept it. She closes the car door and the sound carries farther than it should. She can hear it bouncing off the trees.

Somewhere behind the house, down the slope, in the direction of the lake she cannot see from here but knows is there, something cracks. A long, low, ringing crack, like a piece of furniture giving up.

The ice. She tells herself it is the ice. The ice does that. She has heard this sound a thousand times in childhood and never once thought twice about it.

She picks up her overnight bag and the bag of saltines and water and walks toward the porch.

The light flickers. She watches it flicker and steady, then flicker again — twice, in a pattern that is not quite a flicker. Three.

She unlocks the door.

Inside, the house smells like her father, and like coffee, and like something else underneath both of those things. Something cold. She does not have a name for it.

She sets the bags down on the kitchen floor.

The percolator is on the counter.

It is the same one.

—

She does not sleep well. She tells herself this is normal. She is in a strange bed that is not strange, in the room she grew up in, under a quilt her grandmother made her in 1995.

Sometime around three in the morning she wakes to a sound she cannot place. It is not the house settling. It is not the wind. It is not the ice. It is coming from the direction of the lake, half a mile off, faint and rhythmic, stopping every few seconds and then starting again.

She lies very still. She listens. A snowmobile, she thinks, except snowmobiles do not stop and start that way. A generator at a neighbor's, except there are no neighbors close enough to hear a generator.

It stops.

She gets out of bed and goes to the kitchen window without turning on the light. The yard is bright with moonlight on snow, the line of pines black at its edge, the slope down to the water beyond. The snow in the yard is not unbroken.

A path of footprints runs from the tree line to the back porch and stops there. Single line. Small. Too small for a deer.

She stands at the window for a long time. The wind has not blown for hours. The prints are crisp.

She thinks she should call someone. She does not know who.

She thinks that in the morning she will see them in daylight and they will be a rabbit's, or some animal she is misreading because she has been awake for twenty-three hours and is not in her right mind.

She goes back to bed.

She does not sleep.
