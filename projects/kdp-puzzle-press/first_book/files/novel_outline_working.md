# Untitled Folk Horror Novel — Working Outline

*Working document. Subject to revision in drafting.*

---

## Premise

A Finnish-American woman returns to a dying iron-range town in Michigan's Upper Peninsula in February to bury her father and settle his estate. The estate cannot close until she deals with the rantasauna her great-grandfather built on the family's lakeshore in 1919, and that her grandfather and father spent a hundred years rebuilding and maintaining. What she finds inside is the start of understanding what her family did to be allowed to live on this land — and what's been waiting under the water, patient and counting, for the last thing her great-grandfather promised.

## Genre & Comps

Adult literary folk horror. ~85,000 words. Standalone with series potential.

Comps to study while writing:
- *The Hollow Kind* by Andy Davidson — generational guilt, rural horror, family complicity
- *The Only Good Indians* by Stephen Graham Jones — the wronged returning, the cost of inheritance
- *The Bewitching* by Silvia Moreno-Garcia — historical dread + modern anxiety
- *Mexican Gothic* by Silvia Moreno-Garcia — outsider/insider returning to a sick place
- *The Year of the Witching* by Alexis Henderson — community-as-threat
- Catriona Ward's body of work — for tone and unreliable interiority

## Setting

**Kivijärvi, Michigan.** Fictional iron-range town in the western U.P., somewhere between Ironwood and Ishpeming. Population peaked at 4,200 in the 1950s; ~711 now. Mine closed in 1987. Finnish Hall still stands but only opens for funerals. Lutheran church holds services every other Sunday. Hakala's bar (open since 1923), a gas station, a diner that closes at 2 PM. Lake Kivijärvi (Finnish: "stone lake") at the edge of town — deep, glacially carved.

## Protagonist

**Aino Marlene Hietala.** 38. Born July 1987 in Kivijärvi, Michigan. Lives in a two-bedroom apartment in Bay View, Milwaukee, in a 1920s building with radiator heat that bangs in the morning. Five-foot-six. Brown hair she's been cutting herself since the divorce, just past her shoulders, going gray at the temples in a way she hasn't decided what to do about. Pale in the Finnish way — translucent in winter, freckles by August. Strong, square hands. The body of a former athlete that holds the muscle memory but not the conditioning. Glasses for reading.

Named for her great-grandmother — the first Aino, wife of Eino, who watched what her husband did in 1919 and said nothing. Our Aino doesn't know this when the book opens.

### Body & Bearing

A tense person who doesn't know she's tense. Carries her shoulders up. Grinds her teeth. Drinks more coffee than she should. Has been told by a chiropractor and an ex-husband and a yoga teacher that she needs to relax, in increasingly resentful tones. Has spent her adult life refusing the kind of bodily surrender a sauna requires. The first time she lets the heat take her in Ch 10, something breaks open she's been holding closed since she was nineteen.

### Job

Paralegal at a midsize Milwaukee firm — workers' comp and Social Security disability claims. Twelve years. Meticulous, fast, can read a medical record and find the contradiction in ninety seconds. Her supervising attorney has been telling her for six years she should go to law school. She has not gone.

What this gives the book:
- She knows how to read a paper trail. The ledgers in Ch 7 register to her professionally, not as a confused civilian.
- She works adjacent to the working class she came from without being of it anymore — mill workers, miners' descendants on disability for the lung damage their fathers brought home. There's an ache there she doesn't fully name.
- She lives in the gap between competence and ambition. A person who *manages* her life rather than directs it. The book is about her finally directing something — at the cost of everything she manages.

### The Divorce

Married Daniel Karpinski at 26. Divorced at 33. Five years out at book open.

Daniel was a high school English teacher, two years younger, sweet and steady. Tried for a child for three years. Two miscarriages — one at 11 weeks, one at 19. After the second, she could not be in the same room with him without resenting that he was still there to be sad about it, and she hated herself for resenting him. He wanted to try again. She didn't. He wanted to talk. She didn't. He stayed kind. She got colder. He moved out. The divorce was uncontested and amicable and devastating in a way that doesn't have a vocabulary.

He's remarried now, to another teacher. They have a one-year-old. Aino sees this on Facebook every few months and has to put her phone down for an hour.

What this gives the book:
- She is a woman who has lost a child she did not give birth to and decided, without consulting herself, that she is the kind of person things slip away from. The book asks her to *hold on* to something — first her father's secret, then the right to repair it. Her arc is overcoming the deep groove of giving up on hard things.
- She has a body that has betrayed her before. The supernatural will not register to her as incomprehensible. She knows what it is to have something inside you that you cannot control. This will not be on the page often. It will be in *every* page.

### The Father (Henrik "Hank" Hietala)

Born 1948, died January 2026 at 77, in his kitchen, of a heart attack the coroner called sudden but the old men at Hakala's would call something else. Worked the iron mine until 1987. Switched to the paper mill in Iron Mountain after — 90-minute commute each way for sixteen years until they cut his shift. Retired at 66. Lived alone in the family house for the last 23 years, since Aino's mother left.

**Their relationship over the 19 years of estrangement.** Talked on the phone three or four times a year. Birthdays, Christmas, sometimes Father's Day. The calls were short and entirely about surface — weather, the mill, what she was reading. Toward the end she realized he was waiting for her to come home and ask him things, and she had decided years ago she would not, so they stayed in the holding pattern until he died with no one in the house.

She last saw him in person at her grandmother **Aili's** funeral when she was 15. They sat in the same pew. They did not speak much. Two months later was the rupture (the ledger discovery). She has not been in this house, or this town, since she was 19.

### The Rupture at 19

Summer she was 19, home from her first year at UW–Madison, she found one of her father's ledgers. Didn't know what it was. Read enough to understand he was writing things that did not make sense — about something in the lake, about *her*, about *the watch*. Thought he was losing his mind. Confronted him. He told her it was nothing. She didn't believe him. He told her again, harder, and the harder became something close to violent — he didn't hit her, but grabbed the ledger out of her hands hard enough to leave a mark and yelled at her in a way he had never yelled at her in her life. She left for school three weeks early and never came back to live in that house.

She has spent 19 years believing he was either a crank, a man with undiagnosed mental illness, or — in her darker moments — a violent man she had been wrong to trust as a child. The book opens with her holding all three of those beliefs simultaneously without resolving them.

**Ch 8 is where this resolves.** When she reads a ledger entry from that summer in his handwriting, she discovers he was not crazy. He was telling the truth and could not say it. The reason he yelled at her was that he was trying to keep her out of it. He failed her by trying to protect her from a thing she should have known about, and he died failing her in the same direction. She has 19 years of resentment for a man who was, in his limited and broken way, doing the only thing he knew how to do.

*This is the emotional spine of the book. The supernatural is the engine that surfaces it.*

### The Mother (Lila Maki Hietala)

Half-Finnish, half-something-else-Wisconsin. Married Hank at 22, had Aino at 23, left at 34. Now lives in Phoenix, remarried to a retired contractor named Bob, sells real estate, calls Aino every other month, doesn't ask questions she doesn't want answered.

She left because she figured out around year nine that her husband was spending most weekends at a sauna doing something that wasn't sweating, and when she pressed him, he gave her the same kind of refusal he would later give Aino. She left two years later, took Aino with her for a summer, and brought her back when 11-year-old Aino screamed at her in a Phoenix grocery store that she wanted to go home.

Lila has carried that guilt for 26 years. She has never spoken about it. **In Ch 19 or 20, in a single phone call, she says one sentence Aino has been waiting her whole life to hear:** *I shouldn't have brought you back.*

Lila is a phone call. That's right for the book.

### The Finnish Part of Herself

Knows more than she pretends to know. Grandmother Aili spoke Finnish in the kitchen. Grandfather Toivo spoke it at the bar. Her father didn't teach her Finnish but didn't keep her from absorbing it. She understands more than she can speak. Names of foods, weather, body parts, certain prayers her grandmother said, the words for *quiet, listen, don't, come, no, mine*.

Has spent her adult life letting this language die in her mouth. Old women at the funeral in Ch 4 speak to her in Finnish; she understands more than she lets on. By Ch 11 with Helmi, she lets herself remember. By Ch 24 when the sauna is burning, she is praying in Finnish without having decided to.

**Prose tool:** Show her relationship to her own past through what language she's willing to think in. Early chapters: the Finnish words she remembers feel like intrusions. Late chapters: English starts to feel insufficient.

**Grandmother Aili.** Second wife of grandfather Toivo (Toivo's first wife died young). Came over from Finland in 1956 as a war bride of sorts, having met Toivo when he visited the home country. Blunt, unsentimental. Knew Aino until Aino was 15. Once told Aino, when Aino was about 12, that the lake was *not for swimming*, in a tone that brooked no follow-up. Aino has been remembering that sentence for 26 years without understanding why it stayed.

**She has not been in a sauna since she was 18.** Avoided them after her father's behavior shifted around when she was 16 or 17. Has avoided saunas for 20 years — the kind of thing nobody would notice but a Finn. Mikko notices in Ch 9. He doesn't say anything.

### Religion

Lapsed Lutheran in a specifically Finnish-American Lutheran way. Confirmed at 14. Stopped going at 15. Has not been inside a church since her grandfather's funeral. The Lutheran service in Ch 4 lands on her with a complicated mix of dismissal and bone memory — she remembers the hymns, the smell of basement coffee, the women, and 20 years of rejecting this and a half-second of recognizing it undoes her in a way she didn't expect.

Functionally agnostic in the quiet Midwestern way. Not dramatic, not intellectualized. The book puts her in conversation with something that is *not* the Lutheran god, that is older than that god, and that her grandmother knew about and her grandmother's god uneasily accommodated. Her Lutheranism is not going to save her. It is going to be revealed as a thin layer over something her family never stopped knowing.

### Voice on the Page

**Dry. Observant. Self-deprecating in a contained Midwestern way.** Not funny exactly but capable of seeing the absurdity of her own situation without letting it become a coping mechanism. The kind of woman who says *yeah, that's fine* in tones ranging from genuine to murderous; the reader has to pay attention to which.

**Laconic.** Doesn't waste words even in her interior. Prose runs lean. Short sentences in moments of stress. Long sentences only when she's letting herself remember — which means long sentences are *warning signs* signaling that her armor has slipped.

**Specific.** Thinks in objects, temperatures, smells. Not in feelings. When she does land on a feeling, it hits hard precisely because she doesn't reach for them often.

**Untrusting of her own perceptions in a useful way.** Spent 19 years deciding her father was crazy. She has been wrong. Spent 5 years deciding her marriage failed because of who she is. She is also, possibly, wrong about that. The book persuades her, slowly, that her perceptions are correct — the literary horror note: the protagonist's reliability is not betrayed by the prose; it is *recovered* by the events.

**A person who has been holding her breath for half her life.** Prose should feel held. The release, when it comes — Ch 24 and Ch 27 — should feel like the first breath she has taken in twenty years.

## The Central Artifact: The Rantasauna

A traditional Finnish over-the-water shore sauna. Stone foundation set into the lakebed at the shoreline; lower portion of the structure extends out over the water. Cedar interior. Wood-burning stove (*kiuas*) with stones for *löyly* (steam). Bench platform (*lauteet*) raised off the floor. Three rooms: changing room, washing room, sauna proper. Has been continuously used and rebuilt by three generations of Hietala men. Original 1919 carved beam preserved and re-set into each rebuild. Stones in the foundation are Eino's; hearth is Toivo's; cedar interior was replaced by Aino's father in the 1980s.

**Hidden under the lauteet:** a hatch in the floor, opening to the water (or ice) below. The watch.

## The Buried Wrong

**1919.** Eino Hietala — Marja's great-grandfather, came over from Karelia in 1903, worked the mines — held a homestead claim on land at the edge of Lake Kivijärvi. The land had been used by an Ojibwe family for generations longer than any treaty acknowledged. One woman was still living in a cabin on what was now legally Eino's land. Her name was **Mindemoyen**. She would not leave. She had been told by the Indian agent, the priest, and the company that she had no claim.

What Eino did to make her leave, with three other Finnish men's help, is the family's hundred-year secret. The lake was involved. The rantasauna he built that winter was the first version of the structure his son and grandson kept rebuilding — not for sweating, but to *sit on top of*. To keep the lid on what didn't stay buried.

**Critical ethical guardrails:**
- Mindemoyen is not the antagonist. She is the wronged. What she has become is a consequence.
- The horror belongs to Eino, the three other Finnish men, and every Hietala who chose silence after.
- The supernatural is not given a name on the page. The Hietalas don't know what they're dealing with because they refused to learn. Their ignorance is part of their guilt.
- Sensitivity reader from an Anishinaabe background is **non-negotiable** before querying. Salt & Sage Books or Writing Diversely. Budget $300–600.

## What Her Father Leaves Her

- The house (paid off, in the family since 1921, ~$80k market value)
- 40 acres of land including shoreline and woods (paid off, no tax delinquency — *of course not, they needed the deed unchallenged*)
- ~$30,000 in a credit union savings account
- The rantasauna and its contents, listed separately in the will with a clause: *The estate cannot close until you handle it.*
- An envelope from the lawyer containing a key, a hand-drawn map of the lake with one spot marked, and a note in her father's shaky handwriting: *Sit. Watch. Don't open the floor.*

## Horror Engine

Buried family guilt. The supernatural is what the historical wrong has become. The land remembers. The lake keeps it. The sauna is the door.

## Structural Skeleton (~85,000 words, ~27 chapters)

### ACT ONE: ARRIVAL

**Ch 1 — The Drive Up.** Aino drives from Milwaukee on a Tuesday in February. Highway 51 to 45. Crosses into the U.P. at sundown. Radio cuts out for no reason on a stretch of highway with no terrain to block it. *(Voice, place, stakes.)*

**Ch 2 — Hakala's.** Stops at the bar because nothing else is open. Bartender is a high school classmate. Old Toivo Maki, her father's friend, says without preamble: *Don't go out on the lake.* She thinks he means the ice. *(Town texture, first warning.)*

**Ch 3 — The House.** The smell of him still in it. Basement workshop with tools whose purpose she doesn't understand. Photograph: Eino, her grandfather Toivo, her father — all three on the shoreline in front of progressively larger versions of the same sauna. Three generations of men keeping watch. *(Visual setup of the lineage.)*

**Ch 4 — The Funeral.** Lutheran church, half-full, mostly old people. In the basement after, an old woman named **Helmi Korhonen** takes Aino's hand and calls her by her great-grandmother's name. *Aino. You came back. Let it go through the ice this spring. Anna sen mennä. Your father did enough.* *(Central warning, language she half-remembers.)*

**Ch 5 — What He Left Her.** The lawyer's office. Will read aloud. House, land, account — standard. Then the unusual clause about the sauna. *Your father was very specific about this. The estate doesn't close until you handle it.* The envelope. The lawyer, quietly: *I've been doing this a long time. Most people leave their kids a list of debts. Your father left you a list of duties. I'm sorry.* *(Decision made for her by inheritance.)*

**Ch 6 — Night One.** Wakes at 3 AM to a sound from the direction of the lake. Tells herself it's the ice. At the kitchen window in the dark, sees a clear path of small steps in the snow from the lake to the back porch. Single line. Child-sized. *(First unmistakable wrongness. End of Act One.)*

### ACT TWO: THE SAUNA

**Ch 7 — The Sauna.** Daytime. She walks down to the shoreline. The structure is bigger than she remembered, weirder than she expected. Three rooms. Cedar darkened by decades of löyly. Tools she doesn't recognize. Carvings on the inside walls — Finnish, runic, something else mixed in that isn't either. Forty-two leather-bound notebooks on a shelf. Her father's ledgers. The newest one open to a date three days before he died. *(The sauna as character. Mystery of the ledgers as engine for Act Two.)*

**Ch 8 — The Ledgers.** Takes one back to the house. Daily entries, terse: weather, lake conditions. Interspersed: *Quiet tonight. Quiet again. Bad night, she came up. Three knocks. Said the names. Quiet by morning.* Older ledgers, her grandfather's handwriting back to 1962. Older still, her great-grandfather's, going back to 1919. *(She knows now the men of her family were doing something for a hundred years. Not what.)*

**Ch 9 — Old Boyfriend.** Runs into **Mikko Aaltonen** at the gas station. Her senior-year boyfriend, divorced, runs a small construction outfit, never left. Coffee. He asks why she's still in town. She says she's fixing a few things. He says, gently: *Don't fix the sauna. Let it go.* Same warning, different mouth. He's lying about not knowing more. *(Emotional stakes, town silence revealed as collective.)*

**Ch 10 — What Comes Up.** Goes back to the sauna at dusk against her own better judgment. Wants to read more ledgers. Gets it hot — the way her father would have. Steam thick. She has dismantled enough of the lauteet to find the hatch. She opens it. In the dim red light from the stove door, through the steam, through the open hatch, she sees something in the water below. A hand against the underside of the surface, palm flat, fingers spread. Then something passes. Then darkness. She runs. *(Midpoint of Act Two. No more disbelief possible.)*

**Ch 11 — Helmi Korhonen.** Goes to Helmi the next morning. Pulla and coffee. Helmi tells her, in fragments and reluctance, about *väki* — spirit-force in places. About *vetehinen*, water-beings. About the difference between what the Finns brought and what was already here when they came, and how Eino and the others made the mistake of treating those as the same. Helmi will not say what Eino did. She gives Aino a name and a number — a tribal elder in Watersmeet. *(Folklore vector. Honest acknowledgment that the answer is partly outside the Finnish community.)*

**Ch 12 — The Drive to Watersmeet.** *Most important chapter in Act Two. Workshop heavily.* An hour drive. The elder is sixty-something, named with appropriate cultural specificity (consult sensitivity reader). The conversation is hard. She is wary, then frank: *We know what your great-grandfather did. We have always known. We have our own word for what came of it. I will not give you that word. It is not yours to use. What I will tell you is this: the woman he killed had a name, and it was Mindemoyen, and she was my grandmother's aunt. She is not a monster. What she has become is what was done to her. You cannot undo it. You can stop adding to it.* *(The book earns its right to use this material here or it doesn't earn it at all.)*

**Ch 13 — Going Home.** Drives back in the dark. Sits in the kitchen of her father's house. Begins to understand: the men of her family have been *containing* what they did, generation after generation. None of them tried to right it. The ledgers are not a vigil. They are guard duty. *(The protagonist's understanding shifts from supernatural mystery to moral inheritance.)*

**Ch 14 — Mikko Tells Her.** She goes to him. Tells him what she knows. He is quiet a long time. Then: the men of the town whose grandfathers were there in 1919 have always known. Three families helped Eino. Three families have been keeping watch in different ways for a hundred years. Mikko's grandfather was one. He has been hoping she wouldn't figure it out. *(Mikko shifts from love interest to co-conspirator-by-inheritance. End of Act Two.)*

### ACT THREE: THE COUNTING

**Ch 15 — Spring Coming.** The light is changing. Sap rising. The lake's edge is starting to soften. The watch is specifically tuned to *spring break-up* — when whatever is in the lake becomes mobile again after winter dormancy. The Hietalas have always been most attentive in the weeks of the thaw. Her father timed his death badly. *(Ticking clock — seasonal, thematic. The climate is ending the lie whether anyone consents or not.)*

**Ch 16 — The Other Two Families.** Mikko takes her to the Saaris and the Lehtinens. Old men, older women. They are not happy she's been told. Two factions emerge: those who want to keep watching forever, and those who think the time has come to let it end. A third position — Aino's, forming — goes unspoken: try to repair instead of contain. *(External conflict. She takes a position.)*

**Ch 17 — The Snowmobiler.** A man from a nearby town goes missing on the lake. Snowmobile found at the edge of a dark spot. No body. Search and rescue called off after two days. Aino knows what happened. So does Mikko. So does everyone in the three families. The thing under the water knows the watch is broken. *(First death. Stakes external.)*

**Ch 18 — Mindemoyen.** Goes back to the elder in Watersmeet. Asks: what would repair look like. The elder is angry. The elder is also tired. *I do not know. My people have not tried because we did not know we were allowed to want it. Your family made it impossible for a hundred years by keeping her trapped. Now you ask me how to fix it.* She gives Aino nothing. Aino drives home. *(The book refuses easy answers.)*

**Ch 19 — What Her Father Wrote at the End.** Reads the last ledger. The final entries are different. Half-prayers. *I am tired. I do not know if my daughter will come. I do not want her to. If she comes, I want her to do what I never did.* The next-to-last entry is a list of names — the four men who were there in 1919, the people they hurt, the descendants on both sides. He had been trying. *(Her grief becomes specific. He was a man who failed and wanted better.)*

**Ch 20 — The Lid Comes Off.** One of the Saaris — the faction that wanted it ended — goes down to his own family's spot on the lake with a chainsaw and an idea. Says some words he half-remembers. Doesn't understand what he's doing. The thing takes him. The ice cracks across half the lake in a single night. The sauna's pilings groan. The watch is over whether anyone wanted it to be. *(End of Act Three. Beyond anyone's control.)*

### ACT FOUR: THE RECKONING

**Ch 21 — Town Meeting.** The three families meet at the Finnish Hall. For the first time in a hundred years, what was done is spoken aloud. Not all descendants are in the room. Enough are. Aino speaks. Mikko speaks. An old Saari woman silent her whole life speaks. The town does not arrive at consensus. It arrives at the truth. *(Thematic core. The chapter does most of the moral work of the novel.)*

**Ch 22 — The Last Visit.** Aino goes to Watersmeet a third time. Not to ask. To tell. *We will decide what we want. Not you. Bring what your family kept. Leave it with us. Then go home.* *(Agency returns to where it belongs.)*

**Ch 23 — What She Brings Back.** Goes through the sauna one last time. Removes the original 1919 carved beam. The ledgers. The tools. Loads them into Mikko's truck. Drives to Watersmeet. Does not enter the building. Leaves them on the porch. Drives home. *(The absence of ceremony is the point.)*

**Ch 24 — The Sauna Burns.** Three nights later, ice break-up — record early. Aino watches from the shore with Mikko. The sauna goes up — fire from the kiuas, deliberate or not, real ambiguity here. Something happens on the water as it burns. Something visual, sustained, terrible and not entirely terrible. The thing under the water is not destroyed. It is *acknowledged*. The difference matters. *(Climax. Not a battle — a release. And incomplete.)*

**Ch 25 — After.** Spring. Mud season. Two of the four old men of the three families have died in the weeks since. Helmi is moving to her daughter's in Marquette. Hakala's is for sale. Aino is selling the house at a loss to a young couple from downstate who want a vacation property and don't know any of this. She is choosing this. *(The world after.)*

**Ch 26 — Mikko.** A conversation. He asks her to stay. She says she can't. He asks if she'll come back. She says she doesn't know. They are honest with each other in a way her father was honest with no one. She drives south the next morning. *(Emotional resolution without closure.)*

**Ch 27 — She Does Not Get Far.** Highway south. Outside Wausau. Pulls into a gas station. Becomes aware of something — not a sound exactly, not a presence exactly, but a *direction.* Something pulling at her attention from the northeast. She gets back on the highway. At an exit she doesn't plan to take, she takes it. Last line, internal: *I can hear it now.* *(Book one closes. Book two begins.)*

## Craft Decisions

- **POV:** Close third, present tense. The camera lives just behind Aino's shoulder. The narrator can hold knowledge for a beat longer than Aino has it, can let the lake and the sauna register on the page in ways her refusal wouldn't allow. Present tense means she's in it with the reader, not reflecting from survival. Discipline: never let the reader notice present tense as a technique. *She drives.* Not *she drove.* Trust the rest.

- **Voice tools:**
  - Short sentences in stress; long sentences only when armor slips.
  - She thinks in objects, temperatures, smells. Not feelings.
  - Finnish words function as a thread. Early: intrusions, translated away. Late: she lets them stay. By Ch 24 she is praying in Finnish without deciding to.
  - The lake and the sauna are characters. The narrator can register them on the reader before Aino lets herself register them.

## Lore Document — Internal Use Only

*None of this goes on the page directly. The Hietalas' ignorance is part of their guilt; the reader's incomplete understanding is part of the dread. This document exists so we don't contradict ourselves in drafting.*

### What Was Done in 1919

Late autumn 1919 — November, after first hard freeze but before the lake was fully solid. Eino Hietala and three other Finnish men (one Saari, one Lehtinen, one Mäkelä — the Mäkelä line goes extinct by 1947) went to Mindemoyen's cabin at the edge of what was now Eino's homestead. She was 60–70, lived alone, had refused for two years to leave land the federal government, the mining company, and Eino's deed all said was no longer hers.

They did not intend to kill her. They intended to frighten her into leaving by burning her cabin while making sure she got out — the plan being to demonstrate, in the brutal language of homesteaders, that staying was no longer possible. They had been drinking. The cabin went up faster than they expected. She came out but not the way they planned. What happened next happened on the lake's edge in the dark, in the shallows where the new ice had formed in a thin shelf along the shore.

She did not die quickly. The Mäkelä man was the worst of them. Eino was not the worst but he was there. They put her in the lake under the new ice, weighted her with stones from the foundation of the cabin they had just burned, and they walked back to Eino's homestead and they did not speak of it again for the rest of their lives.

Three weeks later, Eino built the first version of the rantasauna directly above the place where they had put her. He told the first Aino it was for the family. She knew. She did not say so. She lived another forty-three years and never once entered that sauna.

### What She Became

The book never names what she becomes. The Anishinaabe traditions of this region (Lac Vieux Desert Band of Lake Superior Chippewa is the appropriate cultural frame) have their own understanding of what happens to a person whose death is unbalanced and whose remains are not properly returned. **We do not name that understanding in the book.** We do not put a word in any character's mouth that claims to translate it. The elder in Watersmeet refuses to give Aino the word. That refusal is the right answer.

What we show on the page: a presence under the water, growing stronger when the watch lapses. A pattern of disturbances escalating slowly across decades and rapidly when something specific changes. Specific responsiveness to *her name*, to *her descendants*, and to *the four families' descendants*.

She is not entirely confined to the lake — most present under the water above her remains, with influence reaching outward in concentric weakening (shoreline, woods, town, declining intensity).

### How the Watch Functioned

The watch worked for a hundred years because *being known by the Hietalas constrained her.* She was bounded by their narrow perception of what she was. Their refusal to learn what the Anishinaabe community knew was, perversely, what kept her contained. The watch was *acknowledgment without restitution.* They were attending to her without honoring her, without restoring anything.

The Anishinaabe community across the lake has known about her continuously for a hundred years and has *not* gone near. They have grieved her in their own ways and let her be. The Hietalas' watch has prevented those ways from doing what they might have done.

### Mechanics — The Rules

**She is strongest in or near water.** Lake, shoreline, sauna floor with hatch over water. Outside water, weakens with distance.

**She manifests most clearly to the four families' descendants.** Outsiders may sense wrongness but rarely perceive her directly. Mindemoyen's own descendants perceive her in a different register, not the book's to describe.

**Channels of communication:**
- *Knocking.* Three knocks. The number matters.
- *Names.* She knows the four families' names and every descendant. Speaks them. Ledgers record this: *said the names.*
- *Children.* Throughout the hundred years, certain children — Hietala or town — have seen her, drawn pictures, been dismissed. (The footprints in Ch 6 are a child's; we leave the question of which child unanswered.)
- *Dreams.* Of being underwater. Of breath being taken. Of a hand reaching up.
- *Cold.* Even in the heated sauna, even in summer.
- *The lake's surface.* In specific weather, light, times. Forms that are not quite reflections.

**What she cannot do:**
- Cannot leave the lake's reach permanently — bounded by the geography of where she was put.
- Cannot kill at will. Deaths in the book happen because those people *came to her.* The snowmobiler crossed water that knew his grandfather's name. The Saari man went to break the ice himself.
- Cannot speak in full sentences. Speaks in names, single words, the register the dead are allowed.
- **Cannot be destroyed.** There is no destroying her because there is no destroying what was done. The climax is a *release,* not a victory.

### What the Watch Did to the Hietala Men

- **Eino (1880–1962):** went strange and silent. Drank quietly his whole life. Died in his sleep at 82 — the only one of the four 1919 men who lived to old age.
- **Toivo (1923–1962):** Hank's father, Aino's grandfather. Drowned in the lake in 1962, at 39, leaving Aili and 14-year-old Hank. Officially an accident. Aili and the older Saaris and Lehtinens believed he had decided he could not do it anymore.
- **Aili (1925–2002):** Toivo's wife, Aino's grandmother. War-bride era arrival from Finland in 1956. Knew enough to never enter the sauna after Toivo's death. Told Aino at age 12 that the lake was *not for swimming.* Died when Aino was 15. Aino's last in-person visit with her father was at her funeral.
- **Hank (1948–2026):** absorbed the watch as obsession. Married late (32), had Aino late (39), watched his wife leave when Aino was 11. Spent his last 23 years alone. Started, in his final year, trying to write down what he should have said. Failed. Died in his kitchen at 77, three days after his last ledger entry.

### The Climate Variable

Crucial for stakes. The watch has functioned partly because the lake has reliably frozen for five months a year — the frozen surface a *physical constraint* on her, layered on top of the spiritual constraint of the watch. Both are weakening.

The U.P. has had progressively shorter, thinner, less reliable ice cover since roughly 2005. By February 2026 with break-up in mid-March, Lake Kivijärvi is freezing later and breaking up earlier than at any point in the Hietala century. Hank's last several ledgers note this. The watch was timed to the old climate. The new climate is undoing it.

The Saari man in Ch 20 cuts through the ice with a chainsaw because the ice is barely a foot thick when in his grandfather's day it would have been three feet. She does not gain traction *because* of climate, but climate gives her opportunities the earlier generations didn't have to face.

### What Resolves at the End

**What ends:** The Hietala watch. The hundred-year refusal-to-know. The sauna burns. The ledgers leave the property. Aino takes the original 1919 carved beam to Watersmeet and leaves it on the porch. The four families stop pretending to manage what they did.

**What does not end:** Mindemoyen herself. She is not destroyed. The Anishinaabe community now has the option of doing whatever they decide on their own terms, without the Hietalas in the way. **The book ends without showing what they do. We do not get to see that. We do not deserve to.**

**What changes for Aino:** She has been audible to Mindemoyen and now she is audible to her own perceptions. The pull from the northeast at the end of Ch 27 is not Mindemoyen — it is something else. Other lakes, other towns, other families who did versions of what Eino did. The watch ending in Kivijärvi has not changed those. Aino, opened by what happened, can now perceive the shape of similar wrongness elsewhere.

This is the sequel architecture: not a battle won that opens new battles, a perception gained that won't shut off.

### What the Reader Knows vs. What We Know

**Reader knows by end:**
- What the four men did in 1919, in approximate terms (burning, lake, stones).
- That the woman was named Mindemoyen, great-aunt of the Watersmeet elder.
- That the Hietalas have been "watching" her for a hundred years, and what the watch consisted of.
- That what she became is a consequence of what was done, not a malevolent force.
- That the watch ends and the materials are returned.
- That something is now perceptible to Aino that wasn't before.

**Reader does not know:**
- What Mindemoyen *is* in any named or systematized sense. The book offers no taxonomy.
- What the Anishinaabe community will do.
- Whether what Aino senses at end of Ch 27 is supernatural or psychological. *(It is supernatural. The book leaves it ambiguous.)*
- Whether the burning of the sauna was deliberate. *(It was, in part. Aino lights it. Mikko knows. They never speak of it.)*
- The exact nature of what passes through the water in Ch 24.

These ambiguities are deliberate. They are the book's faith with the reader.

## Holes To Watch For

- The 1919 timing against actual U.P. mining history (Copper Strike was 1913–14; need to verify what 1919 looked like in the iron range specifically).
- The drive from Milwaukee to western U.P. is ~7 hours; check that her arrival timing in Ch 1 is realistic for a February day.
- The rantasauna's structural plausibility for 100 years of continuous use — confirm with research that this is actually how rantasaunas are built and maintained.
- The Anishinaabe sensitivity reader process — start identifying readers early, before drafting Ch 12 in earnest.
