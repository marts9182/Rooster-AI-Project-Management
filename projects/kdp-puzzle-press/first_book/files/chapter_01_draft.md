# Chapter 1
## The Drive Up

The radiator in Aino's apartment has been banging since four in the morning, which means she has been awake since four in the morning, which means she has been packing since four-thirty and is now leaving Milwaukee an hour earlier than she planned. It is Tuesday, the eleventh of February, twenty-six degrees and falling. The sky is the color of an aluminum sink.

She tells herself this is a good thing. The early start. The empty interstate. She tells herself she will make it before dark.

She backs the Subaru out of the lot behind her building and waits at the light on Kinnickinnic for someone to honk so she can turn left without feeling rude about it. Nobody honks. The light changes. She turns. In the rearview, her building looks the way every building she has ever lived in looks when she leaves it for any length of time, which is *fine*. Indifferent. Already forgetting her.

The funeral is Thursday. She has packed for four days. The black dress is in a garment bag in the back. The two grocery bags of food she made herself bring — peanut butter, oatmeal, apples, a six-pack of bottled water, a sleeve of saltines — are in the passenger seat next to her purse. She does not trust the food at her father's house to still be edible. She has not been in his kitchen in nineteen years. She has not been in any room of that house in nineteen years. She does not know what nineteen years does to a refrigerator and she would prefer not to find out on an empty stomach.

I-94 north. The Subaru does what Subarus do, which is move forward without comment. She turns the radio on. Public radio is doing a story about something happening in Washington. She turns it off. She turns it back on. She turns it off again. She drives in silence past the airport, past Bayshore, past the exit for her ex-husband's neighborhood, which she has been making a point of not noticing for five years and which she does not notice today. The phrase *making a point of not noticing* is one of her ex-husband's. He used it once about her father. She was furious for a week. She has been using it ever since.

By Fond du Lac the sky has gone from aluminum to wool. By Oshkosh she has eaten one of the apples. By the time she hits Highway 51 north of Wausau the snow has started, fine and dry and not really committing to itself, the kind that blows across the road in lines like something pulled tight. She turns the wipers on the lowest setting. She turns the radio on again. A man with the kind of voice radio men used to have is talking about a basketball game. She lets him.

There are seven hours of this drive. Six and a half if the weather holds and she does not stop. Her father's house is in Kivijärvi, Michigan, which is a town on the iron range whose name people from outside the U.P. cannot pronounce and whose existence most of them are unaware of. She has spent nineteen years correcting the pronunciation of Wisconsinites who could not understand why she did not just say *Iron something*. The town is pronounced *KEE-vee-yer-vee.* It means *stone lake.* The lake is at the edge of town. Her father's house is on the lake. Her father is dead.

She has been telling herself this last fact for six days now and it has not yet acquired the texture of a real thing. Her father's lawyer called her on a Wednesday afternoon while she was on hold with the Social Security Administration. She put the lawyer on hold to finish with Social Security. When she came back, the lawyer was still there, which was the first thing about the day that struck her as wrong. Old Norse lawyers in U.P. towns did not wait on hold for Milwaukee paralegals. He was waiting because what he had to say was the kind of thing that would not be improved by an answering machine.

*Your father has passed,* he said, in the voice older Midwesterners use for conveying the worst possible information without using any of the words for it. *I'm very sorry, Aino. He was a good man.*

She had said *thank you,* which was the wrong thing to say, and *when,* which was the right thing, and *how,* which was a question she did not actually want answered but asked anyway because not asking would have seemed strange. He died in the kitchen. He had been making coffee. The neighbor found him in the afternoon when he did not show up at the bar at his usual time. The neighbor's name was Toivo Maki. She remembered him. She had not thought of him in nineteen years and she remembered him completely and instantly, the smell of his pipe and the way he laughed, which was once and then not again for a long time.

*The funeral is Thursday,* the lawyer said. *Will you be coming?*

She had said yes before she finished thinking about it. Then she sat at her desk for a long time looking at the form she had been filling out, which was a request for medical records on behalf of a fifty-eight-year-old man with rotator cuff damage who had not been able to lift his arms above his head since 2019. The form was due on Friday. She finished it before she went home.

Now she is somewhere north of Tomahawk and the sky has dropped. The snow is committing to itself now, fat flakes against the windshield, and she has slowed to fifty-five and then to fifty and then to forty-five because the road is not yet plowed and the truck in front of her is doing forty. She thinks about her father in his kitchen. She thinks about the coffee he had been making. She thinks about whether the cup was still on the counter when the neighbor came in and whether the coffee was still warm and whether he had used the percolator he had been using for forty years or whether at some point he had bought one of the new machines, the kind with the pods, and the answer to that last question feels for a moment like the most important thing in her life. She does not know what kind of coffee maker her father owned when he died. She does not know what kind he owned the last time she was in his kitchen because the last time she was in his kitchen she was nineteen and he made her instant Folgers because she had asked for instant Folgers, and she does not know if he had used the percolator daily or only when company was over.

The truck in front of her takes the exit for Highway 8. She does not. She stays on 51 and the snow eases up almost immediately, the way snow on the back of a front does, and the sky lightens a half a shade, and she pushes the Subaru back up to sixty.

She does not cry. She has not cried about her father since the lawyer called. She has cried at three different commercials and once during a podcast about an architect in Oslo, but she has not cried about her father. She is not a woman who decides not to feel things. She is a woman things take a long time to reach.

—

She crosses the Wisconsin–Michigan line at four-eighteen in the afternoon, which is later than she meant to but not as late as she feared. Highway 51 has become Highway 45 has become Highway 2, and the sun is somewhere west of her and dropping fast in the way it drops in February at this latitude, which is to say all at once. The snow has stopped. The trees on either side of the road are up close in the way they get in the U.P., the right-of-way narrow and the pines crowding it, and she has the sensation she always had as a child driving up here with her father, which is that the road is tunneling rather than passing through. The trees do not stand back. They lean in.

She has been listening to the basketball man for an hour. He has called the game and now he is doing the postgame and now he is into the postgame analysis with another man who has the same voice and a slightly different opinion. She is not following the conversation. She is using it the way one uses a window fan. She is keeping a noise on.

The radio cuts out.

She glances at the dashboard. The display says she is still on the station. The volume is still up. The signal indicator shows full bars, which on this stretch of highway is wrong — there is no signal here, there has not been signal here for as long as she has known this road. The display is lying to her. But the radio is silent. Not static. Not the dead-air hiss of a station that has gone off transmission. Silent. The way a room is silent when someone has been talking in it and stopped.

She reaches for the volume knob and then does not turn it. She does not know why she does not turn it. She watches the road for another half-mile and the radio remains silent and then it comes back, the basketball man mid-sentence, *— and that's the kind of decision that loses you games in March,* as though he had never been gone.

She lets out a breath. She had not known she was holding it.

The next exit sign says WATERSMEET 11. She does not need Watersmeet. Watersmeet is forty minutes south of where she is going. She will not see the sign for Kivijärvi for another two hours. She passes the Watersmeet sign and the woods close in further and the dashboard clock ticks over to four-twenty-two.

The radio is fine now. The basketball man has moved on to a story about a coach. The wipers are off. The Subaru is doing sixty. The road is clear.

She thinks, with a clarity she does not examine: *I am going to Kivijärvi.*

She has been going to Kivijärvi for five hours. It is the entire reason she is in this car. It is the only fact she has not allowed herself to lose track of since the lawyer called. But the sentence in her head just now — *I am going to Kivijärvi* — has the weight of a thing being acknowledged for the first time. As though some other version of her, the version that has spent five hours pretending this is a logistical problem, has now been overruled.

She is going home.

She has not used the word *home* about Kivijärvi in nineteen years. She does not use it now. She does not let it form. But she feels the shape it would have made if she had let it, and she keeps her eyes on the road, and she drives.

—

She crosses into the iron range proper a little after five. The sky is the color of a bruise an hour old. The towns get smaller and farther apart and then there are no towns at all, only the road and the trees and the occasional driveway disappearing into the woods, marked by a green address sign on a metal pole and nothing else. She passes a bar called Bear's that she remembers from childhood. She passes a gas station that she does not. She passes a place where the woods open up briefly and there is a pit, water in the bottom, the pit that means a mine that has been closed for thirty or forty or sixty years, the pits that are everywhere up here and that her father always called by their company names — the *Cleveland-Cliffs,* the *Mather B,* the *Pewabic.* Everyone here has the names of ghosts in their mouth without knowing it.

She has not been in this part of the country in nineteen years. The road has not changed. The pits have not changed. The trees, she suspects, have not changed, which is the part that always surprises people who do not live in places like this. The trees in the U.P. are slow. Her father once told her — she was perhaps eight, perhaps ten, in the back seat of his truck on a fall afternoon — that the white pines along this stretch of road had been here when his great-grandfather got off the boat in 1903. *They were already big then,* he said. *Eino said when he saw them he thought he had come to the wrong country.*

She has not thought about that sentence in twenty years. She does not know why it has come back to her now. She does not know whose voice she heard it in, just then, in her head — her father's, or her own.

The sign for Kivijärvi appears at six-forty-one. POPULATION 711. The 711 is on a different metal panel than the rest of the sign, hooked underneath, and she remembers without wanting to that when she was young the panel had said 1,400 and that even then the panel had been bolted on. It is the same sign. They have just changed the panel. Twice.

She slows to thirty-five. The town opens up around her the way it always did, which is not very much. The Lutheran church on the left. The Finnish Hall on the right, dark, no cars. The diner that was open from six to two and that she does not need to read the sign of to know is open from six to two. Hakala's, lit up, two trucks in the lot. The gas station. The cross street that goes down to the lake.

She does not turn down the cross street that goes down to the lake. She goes another quarter mile and turns left onto a gravel road that has been freshly plowed, the snow piled high on either side and the gravel showing through black, and she follows it for half a mile until the road bends and the house comes into view.

It is the same house. She did not expect anything else. She does not know why seeing it stops her breath.

She pulls into the driveway. She kills the engine. The Subaru ticks. The pines around the house are completely still — no wind, the kind of February stillness that is not peace, that is the absence of anything bothering to move. The light from the porch is on. Her father's truck is in the driveway, where it has been parked, she now understands, since six days ago. Nobody has moved it. Nobody is going to until she does.

She sits in the car for a long time.

She thinks: *I should have come last summer.*

She thinks: *I should have come ten years ago.*

She thinks: *He is in the funeral home in Iron Mountain. He is not here. There is no reason this is hard.*

She gets out of the car. The cold takes her by surprise the way the U.P. cold always does — not the temperature, which is twenty degrees and not even particularly cold for here, but the *quality* of it, the way the air has a thinness to it, an edge, a sense of the woods having drawn the moisture out of it and kept it. She closes the car door and the sound of the door closing carries farther than it should. She can hear it bouncing off the trees.

Somewhere behind the house, down the slope, in the direction of the lake she cannot see from here but knows is there, something cracks. A long, low, ringing crack, like a piece of furniture giving up.

The ice. She tells herself it is the ice. The ice does that. The ice cracks all winter. It is doing what ice does. She has heard this sound a thousand times in childhood and never once thought twice about it.

She picks up her overnight bag and the bag of saltines and water and walks toward the porch.

The light on the porch flickers. She watches it flicker. She watches it steady. She watches it flicker again, twice, in a pattern that is not quite a flicker, that is — if she is being honest with herself — three.

Three.

She unlocks the door. She lets herself in. Inside, the house smells like her father, and it smells like coffee, and it smells like something else underneath both of those things, something cold, something she does not have a name for.

She sets the bags down on the kitchen floor.

The percolator is on the counter.

It is the same one.

—

She does not sleep well that night. She tells herself this is normal. She is in a strange bed. She is in a strange house that is not strange. She is in the room she grew up in, in the bed she grew up in, under a quilt her grandmother made her in 1995. She tells herself to sleep.

Sometime around three in the morning she wakes to a sound she cannot place. It is not the house settling. It is not the wind. It is not the ice. It is coming from the direction of the lake, half a mile off, faint and rhythmic and stopping every few seconds and then starting again.

She lies very still. She listens. She tells herself it is a snowmobile in the distance, except snowmobiles do not stop and start that way. She tells herself it is a generator at a neighbor's, except there are no neighbors close enough for her to hear a generator. She tells herself it is nothing.

It stops.

She gets out of bed and goes to the kitchen window and looks out at the back yard. The yard is bright with moonlight on snow, the line of pines black at the edge of it, the slope down to the water beyond. The snow in the yard is not unbroken.

There is a path of footprints in the snow. They run from the tree line at the back of the property to the porch and stop there. Single line. Small. Child-sized.

She stands at the window for a long time. She does not turn on the kitchen light. She watches the path. The wind has not blown for hours; the prints are crisp.

She thinks, eventually, that she should call someone. She does not know who. She thinks that in the morning she will see them in daylight and they will be a deer's, or a rabbit's, or some animal she is misreading because she has been awake for twenty-three hours and is not in her right mind.

She goes back to bed. She does not sleep. She lies in the dark and listens for the sound from the lake and does not hear it again, and toward dawn she falls into a kind of half-sleep, and in it she dreams of cold water, and of a hand against the underside of something flat and clear, and of the percolator on her father's counter, still warm, still warm, still warm.

She wakes at seven. The house is silent. The light through the window is gray.

The funeral is tomorrow.
