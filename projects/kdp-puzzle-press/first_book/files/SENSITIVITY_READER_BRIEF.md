# Sensitivity-Reader Brief — *Anna Sen Mennä* (Seven Martin)

*Prepared 2026-06-01 from the inline `SENSITIVITY-FLAG` markers in the manuscript. Second draft, ~63k words, 26 chapters.*

## What the book is
A literary folk-horror novel set in a fictional Finnish-American town (Kivijärvi) on Michigan's Upper Peninsula. In 1919, four Finnish immigrant men — led by the protagonist's great-grandfather, Eino Hietala — killed an elderly **Ojibwe** woman who would not leave land they had claimed under a federal homestead, and put her remains in the lake. For a hundred years the Hietala family kept a "watch" (a sauna built over the site, a weekly vigil) to contain what she became. The protagonist, **Aino Hietala**, inherits the house and undertakes, with the guidance of a band representative (**Louise Swifthawk**), an act of acknowledgment: stopping the watch, returning the records, and letting the band do their own part. The horror belongs entirely to the **white settler families**; the Ojibwe characters are wronged parties with full agency, never the threat.

## The author's stance (what we tried to do — please pressure-test it)
1. **Characters remain recognizably Ojibwe / Lake Superior Ojibwe.** Specificity as respect; we did not invent a wholly fictional people. We DID fictionalize the institutions (see #5).
2. **Maximum restraint on cultural/spiritual content.** The band's beliefs, what the woman "became," and the interior content of their ceremony are **never named, systematized, translated, or depicted from inside**. Aino observes the ceremony from a respectful distance and explicitly looks away from parts of it.
3. **No Ojibwe belief is voiced through the white characters or the narration.** Where Finns reach for explanation, they use *their own* (inadequate) folk concepts, explicitly marked as a borrowed lens (see Ch 11).
4. **The elder/Louise withholds.** Louise sets an explicit boundary on what is and isn't hers to share; the white characters do not ask her to cross it.
5. **Institutions fictionalized.** The real town of Watersmeet → **Tamarack**; the real **Lac Vieux Desert Band of Lake Superior Chippewa** → the invented **Tamarack Lake Band of Lake Superior Ojibwe**. ("Lake Superior Ojibwe," the true cultural grouping, is retained as accurate and respectful.)

## What we most want a reader's judgment on (standing questions)
- **A. The name "Mindemoyen."** The murdered woman is named "Mindemoyen" throughout (she is given a name rather than left anonymous, as an act of restitution within the story). *Mindemoyen* is an Ojibwe word (commonly glossed "old woman"). **Is using it as this character's name appropriate, or should it be replaced/handled differently?** This is the single highest-priority question; the name recurs widely (Ch 11, 12, 15, 17, 18, 22, 23) and is easy to change globally if advised.
- **B. The ceremony (Ch 22).** Depicted only from Aino's outside vantage — words spoken (untranslated), objects carried (not described in their meaning), a posture, the lake's response. **Is the level of restraint right? Is even this outside-only depiction acceptable, or should it be further abstracted?**
- **C. Louise / the elder's dialogue and agency (Ch 10, 11, 12, 22, 23).** Louise leads, withholds, and defines what "acknowledgment" means. **Does she read as a full person with agency, or does she tip into a "guide/wise-elder" function? Is the boundary-setting handled respectfully?**
- **D. The folk-concept framing (Ch 11): *väki* / *vetehinen*.** Finnish folk terms are offered by an elderly Finnish woman (Helmi) **explicitly as the settlers' inadequate lens**, NOT as a description of Ojibwe reality. **Does the framing hold, or does it risk reading as explaining the Ojibwe woman through Finnish folklore?**
- **E. The protagonist's Finnish prayer at the climax (Ch 22): *"Anna meidän anteeksi"* ("forgive us").** It surfaces involuntarily — her own family's language and plea, NOT an appropriation of Ojibwe practice. (We deliberately chose this over *anna sen mennä* / "let it go," to avoid a settler instructing the victim to release her grievance.) **Confirm this reads as the family asking forgiveness, not as appropriation.**
- **F. The character name "Louise Swifthawk."** "Swifthawk" is an invented surname. **Does it read as authentically plausible, or as a stereotyped "Native-sounding" name?** (Out of scope for the draft's fictionalization pass; flagged here for the reader to rule on. Easy to change.)
- **G. The carved figure (Ch 14).** A small wrapped wooden figure in the root cellar, deliberately left unidentified — Aino does not unwrap it ("not hers to unwrap"). **Is leaving it ambiguous the right call, or does its presence imply something that needs handling?**
- **H. "Mindemoyen remained" / what she became (Ch 12, and the haunting throughout).** The narrative frames the haunting as the consequence of a wrong, and the band's stance (Ch 12) is that "something remained" and they chose not to interfere because it was not theirs to interfere with. **Does the supernatural framing of a murdered Ojibwe woman's persistence read as respectful (a wrong that cannot rest) or as problematic? This is intertwined with B and the whole premise.**
- **I. The closing untranslated line (Ch 22).** Louise speaks a closing line in her own language that the text says means "you are not forgotten," but the English gloss is deliberately **not printed** — only "Aino doesn't understand the words. She understands what they mean." **Is leaving it untranslated the right choice?**

## Decisions already made (for the reader's awareness; all reversible)
- Band + town fictionalized to Tamarack / Tamarack Lake Band of Lake Superior Ojibwe (Task 30).
- Climax prayer is *anna meidän anteeksi* ("forgive us"), not *anna sen mennä*.
- "You are not forgotten" left untranslated on the page.
- The four guilty families are all Finnish; the Ojibwe characters are never the source of the horror.

## Flagged passages, in order

### Ch 8 — The Ledgers
- **8:99** — Aino searches online and writes down the band's name and a tribal contact. *Q: Is the "outsider googling the tribe" beat handled respectfully? (Band name now fictionalized.)*

### Ch 10 — What Comes Up
- **10:69** — Aino's spoken apology into the water to the woman (later named Mindemoyen). *Q: Does a settler-descendant apologizing to the victim read as earned, or presumptuous, at this early stage?*
- **10:86–87** — Louise Swifthawk's voicemail (first contact; the band member who will guide the response). *Q: Louise's introduction/characterization; see standing question C and F (surname).*

### Ch 11 — Helmi Korhonen
- **11:65** — Helmi (elderly Finnish woman) recounts the 1919 killing and names Mindemoyen; the name's origin in Eino's sleep-talk. *Q: standing question A (the name) + is it appropriate that a Finnish character is the one who first names her?*
- **11:76** — *väki* / *vetehinen* offered as the settlers' inadequate lens, NOT as Ojibwe reality. *Q: standing question D.*

### Ch 12 — The Drive to Tamarack
- **12:49** — Louise sets the boundary on what is and isn't hers to share (what the woman was to the band, their beliefs, what they call what she became). *Q: standing question C; is the boundary-setting respectful and sufficient?*
- **12:54** — Louise gives the band's history: homestead claim over long-used land, the Indian agent, the four men, the remains placed in the lake. *Q: Is the historical framing accurate/respectful in its specifics?*
- **12:57** — Aino's silent bodily reception of the murder facts (no Ojibwe content, only her response). *Q: low risk; confirm.*
- **12:70** — Louise describes the band's century of grief, their awareness that "something remained," and their choice not to interfere. *Q: standing question H (the supernatural-persistence framing) + the band's spiritual stance.*
- **12:93** — The Mäkelä/Mäkinen reveal (Mikko's great-grandfather was one of the four); received without asking Louise for detail that is hers. *Q: low risk; confirm.*
- **12:108** — Louise defines "acknowledgment": stop the watch, return the records that are the woman's history, restore the band's ability to grieve and name her. *Q: Does "acknowledgment" as defined here ring true / avoid a tidy settler-redemption arc?*
- **12:123** — Louise reserves the band's own preparations before the sauna is destroyed ("their own way, on their side"). *Q: confirm restraint.*
- **12:146** — Mikko privately absorbing his great-grandfather's guilt (interior only). *Q: low risk.*

### Ch 14 — Mikko Tells Her
- **14:25** — The carved figure in the root cellar, left ambiguous/unidentified. *Q: standing question G.*

### Ch 15 — Spring Coming
- **15:15** — Use of the name Mindemoyen ("whose patience is running out"). *Q: standing question A.*

### Ch 17 — The Convening
- **17:57** — Aino speaks Mindemoyen's name aloud to the Finnish families (the first time the name is said among them in 100 years); no Ojibwe belief voiced. *Q: standing question A + is a settler "naming" the victim to other settlers appropriate as an act of acknowledgment?*

### Ch 18 — The Snowmobiler
- **18:69** — The name Mindemoyen used as the Hietala family's framing of their own victim (not a claim about Ojibwe belief). *Q: standing question A.*

### Ch 22 — The Ceremony *(highest concentration — see standing questions B, C, E, I)*
- **22:17** — Louise addresses the gathered group before the ceremony.
- **22:22** — Louise's family begins to take part; Ojibwe ceremonial content; Aino does not look directly at what they carry.
- **22:25** — Louise speaks in her own language; the ceremony begins; described only from Aino's outside vantage.
- **22:32** — The ceremony itself, rendered only from Aino's outside perspective, specifics not depicted.
- **22:37 / 22:58** — The lake responds to Louise's uncle's voice as he leads the ceremony.
- **22:87 / 22:92** — Aino speaks Mindemoyen's name aloud at the water's edge (twice).
- **22:111** — The involuntary Finnish prayer *Anna meidän anteeksi* (her family's plea, not appropriation). *Q: standing question E.*
- **22:140** — Louise's closing line in her own language meaning "you are not forgotten," left untranslated. *Q: standing question I.*

### Ch 23 — Burning
- **23:51–52** — Aino grieves for Mindemoyen and acknowledges the band's hundred years of carrying her name. *Q: Does the settler-descendant's grief for the victim read as earned, or as centering her own feeling?*

### Ch 26 — She Does Not Get Far
- **26:71** — Aino resolves to seek out the band/family connected to a new lake, extending the acknowledgment work. *Q: Does the ending's "she will do this again elsewhere" read as hopeful stewardship or as a white-savior note? (We tried to keep it as her learning to *look* and to *defer*, not to fix.)*
